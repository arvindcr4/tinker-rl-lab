     An Evidence-Tiered Audit of RL Post-Training Implementations

10   ANONYMOUS AUTHOR(S)
11
12   Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a dominant approach for post-training language models, yet the field lacks standardized
13   benchmarks for comparing RL methods across training frameworks, and it remains unclear which empirical conclusions transfer across
14   implementations, model families, and scales. We present TinkerRL-Bench, a unified benchmark spanning 11 implementations across
15   7 RL libraries (TRL, Stable Baselines3, CleanRL, Tianshou, PufferLib, rl_games, d3rlpy) evaluated on mathematical reasoning (GSM8K,
16
     arithmetic), preference learning, and knowledge distillation tasks. Results to date cover five end-to-end stacks (TRL, Tinker, SB3, CleanRL,
17
     Tianshou); the remaining libraries are scaffolded but not yet run. Using standardized reward functions, hyperparameter mappings,
18
19
     and statistical evaluation protocols based on rliable, we provide a cross-library comparison of RL post-training implementations
20   for language models at scales from 0.6B to 30B parameters. We report three conservative findings. First, the large measured gap
21   between LLM-native GRPO stacks (TRL, Tinker) and classic RL libraries (SB3, CleanRL, Tianshou) is best read as an implementation
22   and task-encoding effect—the classic-RL stacks run a small MLP policy over a discrete-action arithmetic MDP, not the autoregressive
23   language model—rather than evidence of algorithmic superiority. Second, although online GSM8K training reward improves with
24
     GRPO, a held-out control shows the mean Qwen3-8B-Instruct gain over the same checkpoint’s pre-RL held-out accuracy is small and
25
     not statistically significant (83.3% vs. 82.0%, 𝑝=0.26). Third, implementation and stack choices are a substantial source of cross-library
26
     performance variance, though we do not claim they account for the majority of it absent a fully matched variance decomposition. We
27
28   release code, evaluation tooling, and checkpoints where licensing and the managed backend permit; closed-backend base weights are
29   not reproducible artifacts.

79        Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and its variants—including GRPO [14], DPO [12], and knowl-
80
      edge distillation—have become essential for aligning language models with human preferences. However, the prolifera-
81
      tion of training frameworks (TRL, OpenRLHF, veRL, Tinker) has made it increasingly difficult to attribute performance
82
83    differences to algorithmic innovations versus implementation details.
84        The RL community has long recognized this reproducibility challenge. Henderson et al. [5] demonstrated that
85
      implementation details can cause larger performance gaps than the algorithms themselves. Cavenaghi et al. [3]
86
      conducted a systematic study finding that none of 60 examined RL papers met strict ACM reproducibility badge
87
88    definitions. More recently, Jordan et al. [7] argued that rigorous benchmarking in RL requires computational resources
89    often exceeding what researchers can afford, leading to underpowered comparisons.

      2     Related Work
125
126       RL for Language Models. PPO [13] has been the standard algorithm for RLHF since InstructGPT [10]. Recent
127   alternatives include GRPO [14], which eliminates the value model, and DPO [12], which bypasses RL entirely. Multiple
128
      training frameworks have emerged (TRL, OpenRLHF, veRL, Tinker), each with different implementation choices.
129
130
          RL Benchmarking. Henderson et al. [5] first highlighted reproducibility issues in deep RL. The rliable framework [2] es-
131
      tablished rigorous statistical methodology. Lynnerup et al. [9] surveyed reproducibility on real-world robots. Yamerenko
132
133   et al. [15] proposed generative benchmarking with converse optimality for evaluating sensitivity and robustness.
134
135
          Reproducibility in ML.. Pineau et al. [11] formalized ML reproducibility criteria, including machine-learning repro-
136   ducibility checklists now widely adopted across venues. ACM’s Artifact Review and Badging policy [1] established
137   standards for evaluated, available, and validated artifacts. Leeser [8] documented the growing adoption of artifact
138
      evaluation across ACM venues.

        Training Dynamics. Our experiments reveal consistent GRPO training dynamics across tasks. First, GRPO exhibits a
356
357   two-phase learning pattern: during early steps (phases 1–20) the model learns answer format (accuracy 0–14%), then
358   rapidly internalizes reasoning logic in later steps (phases 21–25: 14–58%). Second, a prerequisite for effective RL training
359
      is that the base model must already occasionally solve the target problems; without any positive reward signal, the RL
360
      gradient cannot propagate useful updates. Third, Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models exhibit volatile training curves due
361
362   to sparse routing, requiring careful monitoring and potentially larger batch sizes to stabilize updates. These observations
363   motivated the model-selection choice of Qwen3-4B-Instruct over a 30B MoE model for the multi-stage reasoning task.

      8     Conclusion
503
504   We present TinkerRL-Bench, a unified benchmark for comparing RL post-training methods across 7 libraries and
505
      11 implementations, providing standardized evaluation protocols and comprehensive reproducibility infrastructure.
506
507
      The evidence it offers is deliberately narrow. The current release most strongly supports three conservative claims.
508   First, the large measured gap between LLM-native GRPO stacks and classic-RL PPO libraries is an implementation
509   and task-encoding effect (the classic-RL stacks run a small MLP policy over a discrete-action arithmetic MDP, not the
510
      autoregressive language model), not evidence that any one algorithm is superior. Second, trainability in our short-horizon
511
512
