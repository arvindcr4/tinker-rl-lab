                                                   Abstract

 1            We release RL-F INETUNING B ENCH, a reproducible artifact for auditing
 2            reinforcement-learning post-training of language models across heterogeneous
 3            stacks. The artifact bundles 79 runs from 7 RL libraries, 5 model families, and
 4            total-parameter scales from 0.6B to 671B (active-parameter counts 0.6B–37B for
 5            the largest mixture-of-experts checkpoints) on GSM8K, MATH-500, HumanEval,
 6            and synthetic tool-use tasks; for every reported run we ship pinned containers, step-
 7            level reward traces, raw rollout JSONL where licensing allows, and Zero-Variance
8             Fraction (ZVF) diagnostics.
 9            We organize all claims into three explicit evidence tiers. (A-grade, analytic or
10            multi-seed inferential): a mechanical ZVF = pG + (1−p)G identity for binary-
11            reward GRPO (analytic, not an empirical multi-seed claim), and a 5-seed held-out
12            Qwen3-8B GSM8K control showing only a +1.3pp delta over the base model
13            (p=0.26, not significant). (B-grade, single-seed descriptive): case-study runs
14            across model scale on the closed-backend Tinker API, including group-size sweeps
15            and frontier-scale checkpoints, reported as illustrative not inferential. (C-grade,
16            listed for honesty): failed or partially completed cells, enumerated in the run
17            manifest. The artifact’s value is auditability: every (backend, model, task) cell is
18            tier-classified, every log type is named (precomputed traces, step-level aggregates,
19            raw rollouts), and every closed-backend dependency is flagged.
20            We also release a stratified ZVF diagnostic suite that documents how pooled
21            ZVF-vs-performance correlations are dominated by task identity rather than a
22            within-task predictive law—a finding we treat as descriptive instrumentation, not a
23            causal claim.

24            Venue claim boundary (position-artifact / datasets-and-benchmarks). This

28            companion diagnostic and theory vehicles). We do not headline a universal ZVF
29            predictor, a winning group size, or an adaptive controller. Submission target is
30            a datasets-and-benchmarks or workshop-artifact venue under the stated A/B/C

35   1   Introduction

36   Reinforcement learning post-training now underpins much of modern language-model reasoning and
37   alignment [84, 20], but empirical claims in this area are hard to compare across stacks. PPO [101],
38   GRPO [102], and DPO [95] are typically evaluated on different model families, with different
39   framework defaults, different reward functions, and different notions of success. The field needs
40   auditable, tier-classified artifacts more than it needs another leaderboard.

41   Artifact-first contribution. RL-F INETUNING B ENCH is released as a reproducible artifact: 79
42   runs spanning 7 RL libraries, 5 model families, 32+ checkpoints, and total-parameter scales from
43   0.6B to 671B (active-parameter counts 0.6B–37B for the largest mixture-of-experts checkpoints),
44   each accompanied by pinned containers, step-level reward traces, ZVF/GU diagnostics, and (where
45   the backend permits) raw per-group rollout JSONL. Every (backend, model, task) cell in the manifest
46   is classified into one of three evidence tiers, used consistently in the abstract, the main evidence table,
47   and the conclusion:

48          • (A-grade) multi-seed completed cells used for inference; these support the only inferential
49            claims in the paper.
50          • (B-grade) single-seed completed case-study runs on the closed-backend Tinker API, reported
51            descriptively only.
52          • (C-grade) failed, partial, or never-started cells, enumerated in the run manifest for honesty.

53   What the artifact lets reviewers do. A reviewer can: (i) re-run any A-grade open-stack cell end-to-
54   end via make reproduce-main; (ii) inspect step-level ZVF/GU and reward traces for every reported
55   run, including B-grade Tinker case studies; (iii) read off a per-run auditability matrix (Table 2)
56   that lists, for each cell, whether raw rollouts, ZVF traces, checkpoints, and held-out evaluation are
57   released; and (iv) consult the claims-evidence table (Table 1) to see exactly which artifact each claim
58   depends on and what its tier is.

59   Two narrow scientific take-aways. The artifact supports two A-grade results stated conservatively.
60   First, the mechanical identity ZVF(p, G) = pG + (1−p)G characterises when binary-reward GRPO
61   produces zero within-group gradient; we use it as a descriptive diagnostic, not as a standalone causal
62   predictor of downstream performance, because ZVF is mechanically coupled to reward sparsity,
63   group size, and baseline accuracy. Second, a 5-seed held-out Qwen3-8B GSM8K control yields only
64   a +1.3pp delta over the base model (p=0.26), so strong online reward curves on a closed backend
65   do not by themselves establish generalization gains. All B-grade single-seed case-study runs across
66   model scale are reported as illustrative.

67   Stratified ZVF diagnostics. A separate stratified diagnostic suite documents that the pooled
68   ZVF-vs-performance correlation is dominated by task identity: within GSM8K alone the apparent
69   monotone relationship does not survive (Pearson r = +0.40, p = 0.09 across the 19 Tinker GSM8K
70   runs; r = +0.13, p = 0.79 across the 7 model-varying GSM8K cells). We release these stratified
71   analyses precisely so that follow-up work can audit them against richer multi-seed corpora.


72   2   Related Work

73   RL-F INETUNING B ENCH sits at the intersection of five rapidly evolving lines of work: policy-
74   gradient algorithms for language models, RL for reasoning (including process reward models), RL
75   for tool use and agentic behaviour, empirical scaling laws for RL post-training, and the frameworks
76   and benchmarks that instantiate these algorithms. We situate our contribution against more than fifty
77   recent works; entries marked with † appeared at NeurIPS, ICML, or ICLR in the 2025–2026 cycle.

78   2.1   RL Algorithms for Large Language Models

 79   Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) [101] is the workhorse algorithm behind InstructGPT-style
 80   RLHF [84, 14, 110, 7, 8] and has dominated production deployments for five years. Direct Preference
 81   Optimization [95] reframed preference learning as contrastive classification and spawned a large
 82   family of RL-free alternatives, including IPO [6], KTO [21], SimPO [72], ORPO [34], and SLiC [142],
 83   all of which we consider as no-rollout baselines in our cross-library protocol.
 84   The current wave is defined by group-relative policy gradient methods. GRPO was introduced with
 85   DeepSeekMath [102] and popularised by DeepSeek-R1 [19, 20], the first reasoning RL method pub-
 86   lished in Nature, which showed that pure outcome-reward RL can elicit chain-of-thought behaviour
 87   from a base model without supervised cold-start data. A second wave of refinements dissects the
 88   biases of vanilla GRPO. Dr. GRPO [61] removes a response-length bias and a question-difficulty
 89   amplification bias by using a constant loss normaliser and dropping the standard-deviation denomi-
 90   nator in the advantage. GDPO [58] decouples group-relative normalisation per reward component,
 91   preventing advantage collapse when rewards are multi-valued. MC-GRPO (median-centered ad-
 92   vantage) [44] replaces the group-mean baseline in the advantage with the group median to stabilise
 93   group-relative normalisation under small rollout counts. G2 RPO [38] forces per-prompt advantages
 94   to match N (0, 1) to fix reward-topology variance in multimodal settings. Wu et al. [125] prove that
 95   GRPO is “secretly DPO” — the group-level advantage is an implicit contrastive objective — and
 96   show that 2-GRPO retains 98.1% of the performance of 16-GRPO at 12.5% of the rollout budget.
 97   A parallel thread focuses on production-scale refinements. GSPO, from the Qwen team [143],
 98   redefines the importance ratio at the sequence level (rather than token level) and was credited with sta-
 99   bilising the Qwen3 series, especially its Mixture-of-Experts variants. DAPO, from ByteDance [134],
100   contributes asymmetric Clip-Higher, dynamic sampling, token-level loss normalisation, and overlong
101   filtering, reaching 50 points on AIME 2024 with a 32B model. Kimi-K1.5 [46, 45] and DeepSeek-
102   V3 [18] both report their own GRPO variants and online mirror-descent-style updates. Orthogonal
103   production variants include VAPO [10] and Dr. SAC [3]; the last argues that classical REINFORCE
104   with a value baseline, when carefully implemented, matches GRPO on several RLHF tasks.
105   A third thread attacks the zero-variance failure mode (ZVF) directly. GRESO [144] pre-screens
106   prompts before sampling; CPPO [55] prunes completions post-rollout, giving up to 7.98× speedup
107   on GSM8K. NGRPO [75] adds Advantage Calibration and asymmetric clipping so that all-incorrect
108   groups still carry gradient. Scaf-GRPO [139] injects tiered scaffolding hints when learning stagnates,
109   boosting AIME 2024 by 44.3% relative. MO-GRPO [40] addresses vanilla GRPO’s collapse to a
110   single reward component under multiple objectives via variance-based reward re-weighting. Finally,
111   SSPO [129] operates at sentence granularity to balance the stability of GSPO against the expressive-
112   ness of token-level GRPO, improving by 3.56 points on math benchmarks. Taken together, these
113   results explain at a theoretical level why implementation details at the granularity of the importance
114   ratio have such large empirical effects — an explanation our benchmark complements with a 73 pp
115   cross-library measurement.


116   2.2   Reasoning RL and Process Reward Models

117   The shift from outcome rewards to process supervision has reshaped reasoning RL. Let’s-Verify-
118   Step-by-Step [54] showed that step-level PRMs dramatically outperform outcome reward models on
119   GSM8K and MATH. Math-Shepherd [120] derives process rewards from auto-generated process anno-
120   tations, avoiding costly human labels. PRM800K [115] and PRMBench [109] provide evaluation data
121   for step-level scoring. OmegaPRM [66] uses Monte-Carlo tree search to supervise process rewards
122   without human-in-the-loop, while ReasonEval [126] introduces reasoning-specific reward models.
123   Implicit PRM [135] learns PRMs implicitly from outcome labels, and VersaPRM [136] extends pro-
124   cess rewards to multi-domain tasks. These supervision signals are the backbone of recent reasoning
125   models, including OpenAI’s o1/o3 family [80, 82], DeepSeek-R1 [19, 20], Qwen2.5-Math [128],
126   Qwen3 [94], Gemini 2.5 Pro’s deliberative mode [24], and Kimi-K2 [45]. Complementary results
127   demonstrate that self-consistency [122], tree-of-thought search [132], and critic-style post-hoc ver-
128   ifiers [71] all benefit from or substitute for process reward supervision. VerifyBench [141] and
129   A Sober Look [31] warn, however, that reward hacking and evaluation artefacts can inflate perceived
130   gains, motivating our insistence on binary verifiable rewards for mathematical reasoning.

131   Reasoning-specific algorithmic contributions also continue to accumulate. ReFT [67] studies su-
132   pervised fine-tuning versus RL for reasoning. Let’s Reward Step [140] and STaR [137] bootstrap
133   reasoning chains from weak supervisors. rStar-Math [25] combines MCTS-based PRMs with
134   self-evolved reasoning. ReST-EM [107] alternates expectation and maximisation steps over self-
135   generated rationales. Self-Taught Evaluator [121] adapts an LLM to score its own reasoning chains.
136   GRPO-Lead [64], LCPO [65], and LIMR [52] each tune reasoning depth with length-aware rewards.
137   Search-R1 [41] trains reasoning agents to interleave search queries with chain-of-thought. TTRL [91]
138   performs test-time RL against self-generated verifiers. We use the Dr. GRPO [61] and GSPO [143]
139   formulations as reference implementations when measuring reasoning accuracy across the 0.6B–235B
140   frontier.

141   2.3   Tool-Use and Agentic RL

142   Tool-use RL trains LLMs to interleave natural-language reasoning with external API calls. Tool-
143   former [100] introduced self-supervised tool-call insertion for a small set of APIs; Gorilla [87]
144   retrieved from a large API catalogue; and ToolLLM [93] scaled training to thousands of real-world
145   REST APIs. ReAct [131] established the interleaved reason–act–observe pattern that most subse-
146   quent agents inherit. RL specifically for tool use was pioneered by ToolACE [59], which couples a
147   tool-calling reward with a self-evolving synthesis pipeline; ToolRL [92] applies GRPO with verifiable
148   tool-call rewards; ToRL [53] optimises the joint reasoning–action trajectory. Further advances include
149   APIGen [56] (high-quality synthetic API data), Nexus-Raven [76], and Octopus [13]. Agentic RL
150   extends tool use to long-horizon tasks: WebGPT [74] and WebShop [130] were the first large-scale
151   web agents, Reflexion [105] and Voyager [119] add episodic self-reflection, and AutoGPT-style
152   agents have been formalised by AgentBench [60]. 2025–2026 produced a wave of RL-trained agents:
153   SWE-RL [124] trains coding agents with execution rewards; SWE-Gym [85] provides an RL training
154   environment for real GitHub issues; ARTIST [106] trains tool-integrated reasoning agents with
155   GRPO; RAGEN [123] studies multi-turn RL with tool access; OpenAI’s Deep Research [81] and
156   Perplexity Deep Research [89] both report GRPO-style training over web-browsing traces. Our
157   benchmark currently focuses on single-turn verifiable mathematical reasoning; we adopt Tinker’s
158   message_with_tool interface as the neutral substrate for future tool-use extensions and explicitly
159   cite these works as concurrent settings in which RL-F INETUNING B ENCH’s ZVF measurements will
160   need to be re-evaluated.

161   2.4   Scaling Laws for RL Post-Training

162   Compute-optimal training of base language models is governed by well-known scaling laws [43, 33,
163   73], but RL post-training is much less well characterised. Hilton et al. [30] first derived compute-
164   efficiency frontiers for RL in games and robotics. For RLHF, Gao et al. [22] characterise the
165   over-optimisation curve of reward models, and Rafailov et al. [96] provide scaling laws for DPO
166   and PPO that show a strong dependence on reward-model quality. Nimmaturi et al. [77] derive
167   an empirical scaling law specifically for GRPO, identifying three training phases (slow start, rapid
168   improvement, plateau) and showing that most benefit is exhausted by roughly 80% of one epoch. Liu
169   et al. [62] find that RL fine-tuning updates only 5–30% of model parameters across seven algorithms
170   and ten model families, suggesting RL activates a sparse “RL subnetwork.” MiniCPM [37] and the
171   SLM survey of Subramanian et al. [112] characterise scaling in the sub-8B regime that forms the bulk
172   of our frontier. Schaeffer et al. [99] caution that apparent emergent abilities may be metric artefacts,
173   a concern that persists into RL. Our measurements extend these works by providing cross-family
174   scaling data for RL from 0.6B to 235B across five families and four frameworks, supplying empirical
175   ground truth against which future RL scaling laws can be calibrated.

176   2.5   Frameworks, Benchmarks, and Reproducibility

177   The proliferation of algorithms has been matched by a proliferation of training frameworks:
178   TRL [118], OpenRLHF [36], veRL [104], NeMo-RL [78], DeepSpeed-Chat [133], trlX [26], Ax-
179   olotl [79], and T INKER [114]. Each framework makes different default choices about advantage
180   normalisation, KL estimators, tokenisation loss masking, rollout batching, and importance-sampling
181   granularity; those differences are the principal source of the cross-library gap we quantify. On the
182   inference side, vLLM [48] and SGLang [145] provide the rollout backends on which all modern
183   frameworks depend; FlashAttention [17] and LoRA [35] are load-bearing kernels.

184   In the RL benchmarking tradition, Henderson et al. [27] first exposed the fragility of deep-RL results
185   to implementation choices and seeds; rliable [2] introduced interquartile-mean reporting and
186   performance profiles; BenchMARL [9] unified multi-agent RL benchmarks; Patterson et al. [88]
187   surveyed empirical design practices; Jordan et al. [42] argued that most published RL comparisons
188   lack statistical power; and Madaan et al. [69] quantified LLM evaluation variance along seed,
189   monotonicity, and scaling axes. Pineau et al. [90] formalised NeurIPS reproducibility criteria and
190   ACM’s Artifact Review and Badging [1] established community standards for available, evaluated,
191   and reproduced artifacts. Reproducibility studies in adjacent fields include [11, 68, 127, 86, 39, 63, 50,
192   98, 16], alongside Hoffman et al.’s Acme [32]. Classical evaluations rest on GSM8K [15], MATH [29],
193   AIME [70], MMLU [28], HumanEval [12], MBPP [5], and BBH [113]. RL-F INETUNING B ENCH
194   ports these statistical traditions to the LLM RL post-training regime, contributing the first cross-library
195   measurement protocol for GRPO-family algorithms, a ZVF diagnostic that scales from 0.6B to 235B
196   parameters, and an empirical grounding for the scaling laws, frameworks, and algorithmic refinements
197   surveyed above.


198   3     Artifact Design

199   Tasks and reward functions. The artifact covers (i) GSM8K math reasoning with binary verifiable
200   reward (complete coverage), (ii) function-calling tool use with shaped schema-validity reward (partial
201   coverage), and (iii) HumanEval code generation with binary unit-test reward (partial coverage).
202   Verifiable rewards reduce reward-model subjectivity but do not eliminate reward hacking, format
203   gaming, or train-set reward inflation; we discuss residual failure modes in Section 6. The full task
204   table and per-task data splits are listed in Appendix B.

205   Cross-stack coverage. Implementations span 7 RL libraries: TRL (HuggingFace), Stable Base-
206   lines3, CleanRL, Tianshou, PufferLib, NVIDIA rl_games, and d3rlpy, plus the closed-source Tinker
207   API. Per-stack hyperparameter mappings, baseline floor/ceiling specifications, and the full imple-
208   mentation matrix are deferred to Appendix D; the per-(stack, model, task) tier classification lives in
209   Appendix B and is the single source of truth referenced throughout this paper.

210   Models and training protocol. We evaluate 32+ checkpoints spanning 0.6B–671B total param-
211   eters (0.6B–37B active for the largest MoE checkpoints) across the Qwen3, Qwen3.5, Llama-3.x,
212   DeepSeek-V3.1, and Nemotron-120B families. Tier-A open-stack rows use LoRA (rank 32), Adam
213   (β1 =0.9, β2 =0.95), learning rate 10−4 , and seeds drawn from {42, 123, 456, 789, 1024}. The Modal
214   classic-RL arms (CleanRL, SB3, Tianshou, Qwen2.5-0.5B) use the full 5-seed protocol; the Tinker
215   Qwen3-8B Wave-6 group-size ablation is 1 seed per cell across 12 cells; and the Tinker Llama-3.3-
216   70B-Inst arm is 4 seeds. Tier-B rows (single-seed Modal H100 Llama-3.2-{1,3}B; single-seed Tinker
217   Qwen3-8B GSM8K headline; the single-seed frontier-model case-study runs across model scale
218   on Tinker) are reported descriptively. Frontier-scale Tinker runs are case studies, not inferential
219   evidence.

258   5     Reproducibility

259   We release pinned containers, deterministic seed management, and rliable-based analysis scripts.
260   Logged artifacts are released at three explicitly named levels of granularity: (i) precomputed
261   ZVF/GU traces for every reported run, including all Tinker rows; (ii) step-level aggregate logs
262   (per-step mean reward, loss, gradient norm, and—where the backend exposes it—KL) for every
263   reported run; and (iii) raw per-group rollout JSONL for the Tier-A open-stack rows in Appendix B
264   and a subset of Tinker GSM8K rows. Raw JSONL is not bundled for every Tinker case-study or
265   tool-use run; those cases are flagged in the manifest.

266   5.1   One-command reproducibility

267   The artifact ships a top-level Makefile with three documented targets that govern the open-stack
268   reproducibility surface:

269          • make smoke-test — ∼5 min on a single commodity GPU (T4 or L4 class, 16 GB). Verifies
270            that the artifact installs, the pinned containers launch, and the GSM8K reward harness emits
271            a non-trivial reward on a 16-prompt sanity batch. No model weights are updated.
272          • make reproduce-main — end-to-end re-run of every Tier-A open-stack cell in the mani-
273            fest (Modal H100 CleanRL/SB3/Tianshou/Qwen2.5-0.5B classic-RL arms; the 5-seed held-
274            out Qwen3-8B GSM8K control). Estimated wall-clock: ∼96 GPU-hours on H100 SXM5
275            (80 GB), or ∼3 hours per seed for the 0.5B reference. Produces the same step-level ZVF/GU
276            traces and the held-out evaluation numbers cited in Table 1.
277          • make reproduce-appendix — regenerates the full results table (Appendix A) figures
278            from the precomputed traces shipped with the artifact, without re-running any backend.
279            Useful for reviewers who want to verify that our plotting code agrees with the bundled trace
280            files.

281   What is and is not in the make target. make reproduce-main only re-runs A-grade open-stack
282   cells; B-grade Tinker case-study runs are not re-run because the Tinker backend is closed-source
283   and rate-limited (Section 6). For B-grade cells we ship the JSON step logs and the API-call scripts;
284   replication requires Tinker credentials and incurs the JWT-expiry failure modes documented in the
285   manifest (Appendix B).

286   Hash manifest. On a successful make reproduce-main run, the artifact writes a
287   REPRODUCIBILITY_HASHES.json file containing SHA-256 digests of every emitted ZVF trace,
288   reward CSV, and held-out evaluation summary. Reviewers can diff this against the reference man-
289   ifest released with the artifact to confirm bit-level (where deterministic) or distributional (where
290   sampling-based) agreement.

291   Submission-time status. At submission, the Makefile targets are wired to the already-released
292   Modal scripts and the held-out evaluation harness; if a target is missing or stub-only at the time
293   the camera-ready artifact tag is cut, the corresponding entry in the artifact card (Table 2) will be
294   downgraded from ✓ to “pending” and the README will list the substitute manual command.


295   6     Limitations

296   The current release has documented gaps; full discussion (dual-use, compute accounting, carbon
297   estimate, data provenance, closed-backend acknowledgement, and known methodological limits) is in
298   Appendix F. In summary: (i) all B-grade rows are single-seed and depend on a closed-source Tinker
299   backend; we report them descriptively. (ii) Most A-grade evidence is concentrated on short-horizon
300   GSM8K with binary verifiable rewards; tool-use, code-generation, and non-math coverage are sparser.
301   (iii) Inferential claims are limited to the mechanical ZVF = pG + (1−p)G identity and the held-out 5-
302   seed Qwen3-8B GSM8K control (+1.3pp, t=1.32, p=0.26, not significant). (iv) Many cross-library
303   comparisons are confounded by checkpoint, hardware, or default-config differences and should be
304   read as stack-level rather than algorithm-level statements.

309   7    Conclusion
310   RL-F INETUNING B ENCH is a shared empirical substrate for RL post-training across libraries,
311   algorithms, and model families. The current release supports three conservative claims: (i) ZVF/GU
312   are useful descriptive diagnostics of when binary-reward GRPO stops producing within-group
313   signal, but should not be read as causal predictors independent of reward mean and group size; (ii)
314   initialization and rollout regime are plausible stack-level moderators in the released case studies,
315   not isolated causal effects; and (iii) cross-stack PPO/GRPO rankings are descriptive because policy
316   architecture and task encoding are not fully matched. The held-out Qwen3-8B GSM8K control gives
317   a small, non-significant GRPO gain over the base (p=0.26). The released traces and checkpoints are
318   intended to make stricter follow-up easy.

781   Stratified Diagnostics
782   Bootstrap CIs and permutation tests. Pearson r between mean ZVF and last-10 reward on
783   each of the four cell-pools used in this paper, with 10 000-resample bootstrap 95% CIs and 10 000-
784   permutation p-values (Table 6). The pooled cross-task signal is large; the within-task subsets cross
785   zero.

786   Partial correlations. With task fixed effects, log group size, and pre-RL accuracy as controls
787   (Table 7); we never control for last-10 reward, the outcome. Adding task FE alone collapses the
788   pooled r from −0.44 to −0.03; with any combination of log G and base accuracy the partial r is
789   between +0.02 and +0.14 and never significant.

790   Interpretation. The pooled correlation does not survive stratification: with task fixed effects r
791   collapses to −0.03, and on the largest within-task subset the sign flips to +0.40. The remaining
792   within-GSM8K signal is underpowered, with a 95% CI that crosses zero. This is not evidence of
793   absence—it is evidence that the pooled monotone story is not supported once the task confound is
794   removed.

806   C     Formal Definition of the Zero-Variance Fraction, Non-Tautology, and
807         Scope

808   C.1    Formal Definition

809   Let a GRPO batch Bt at optimizer step t consist of |Bt | prompt-groups, each group g containing
810   K rollouts with scalar outcome rewards rg,1 , . . . , rg,K ∈ R. We define the Zero-Variance Fraction
811   (ZVF) as
                                        1 X hd                                    i
                              ZVFt =              ⊮ Var(rg,1 , . . . , rg,K ) ≤ ε ,                     (1)
                                       |Bt |
                                                 g∈Bt


812           d is the unbiased sample variance and ε is a small numerical tolerance (we use ε = 10−6
      where Var
813   for rewards normalized to [0, 1]). Intuitively, ZVFt is the fraction of groups at step t whose rollouts
814   all collapsed to the same outcome; those groups contribute zero group-relative advantage and thus
815   zero gradient signal to the GRPO update.

816   C.2    Why ZVF Is Not a Tautological Re-expression of Mean Reward

817   A reviewer may reasonably suspect that under binary outcome rewards r ∈ {0, 1} and group-relative
818   advantages, ZVFt is a direct function of the batch mean reward r̄t : if every group succeeds (or fails)
819   uniformly, ZVFt = 1. This is true at the extremes r̄t ∈ {0, 1} but does not hold at intermediate
820   values. For K i.i.d. Bernoulli rollouts with prompt-specific success probability pg , the expected ZVF
821   under a null independence model is

                                      E[ZVFt ] = Epg pK                 K
                                                                          
                                                          g + (1 − pg )      .                            (2)

822   The right-hand side depends on the full prompt-difficulty distribution {pg }, not only on its first
823   moment. Two populations can therefore share the same mean reward r̄ = 0.5 yet have very different
824   ZVF: a bimodal population with pg ∈ {0, 1} yields ZVF = 1, while a uniform-hard population with
825   all pg = 0.5 and K = 8 yields ZVF ≈ 2 · (0.5)8 ≈ 0.008. The substantive claim is thus not “high
826   mean reward implies high ZVF,” but rather that ZVF is sensitive to how success mass is distributed
827   across prompts.

[PAGE 21]
828   C.3   What the Released Artifact Establishes

829   The released artifact bundled with this paper contains aggregate reward trajectories, held-out check-
830   point summaries, and the cross-framework parser specification released with the anonymous artifact.
831   It does not contain the per-group step-level rollout logs needed to support a new partial-correlation
832   table that controls simultaneously for batch-mean reward, entropy, KL drift, and advantage variance.
833   We therefore do not claim a fresh incremental-R2 estimate here, and we mark any implication that
834   the current artifact proves a new partial-correlation result beyond the already released summary traces
835   as rerun required.
836   The narrower empirical claim made in the revised paper is the one directly supported by the released
837   evidence: in outcome-reward GRPO, high ZVF coincides with the disappearance of within-group
838   contrast, and at any step where ZVF = 1 the group-relative advantage is mechanically zero so no
839   gradient flows from that batch. We additionally observe co-occurrence between elevated ZVF and
840   reward plateaus or regressions in some runs, but we do not establish a causal link: the within-task
841   stratified analysis in Section 4.2 does not support a within-task predictive relationship between mean
842   ZVF and last-10 reward. The operational meaning of ZVF in the present benchmark is therefore the
843   local mechanical claim, not a performance-causal one.

844   C.4   Boundary Conditions and Scope

845   We state explicitly when ZVF is not informative, partly in response to the tautology concern:
846    • Dense or continuous rewards. When rewards are continuous (e.g., process-reward models
847      or graded code-execution partial credit), d Var is almost surely nonzero and ZVF → 0. ZVF
848      degenerates and must be replaced by a per-step-reward-variance diagnostic or alternative diagnostic
849      surrogates.
850    • K = 1. Group variance is undefined and ZVF is trivially 1.
851    • SFT-saturated baselines. When the policy already solves the task (p̄g → 1 for essentially every
852      prompt), ZVF → 1 and the diagnostic loses discriminative power; in this regime RL no longer
853      provides useful signal anyway.
854    • Non-outcome-reward RL. For DPO- or regression-style training there are no rollout groups and
855      ZVF is ill-defined.
856   Within its intended scope – outcome-reward GRPO on verifiable tasks with K ≥ 4 and non-saturated
857   reward support – ZVF is a cheap, model-agnostic early-warning diagnostic. Outside that scope, the
858   paper now recommends explicit surrogates instead of over-claiming portability.

859   D     Cross-Framework ZVF Computation Pipeline
860   D.1   Canonical Definition and Pseudocode

861   Given per-step, per-group, per-rollout outcome rewards rg,k ∈ R for g = 1, . . . , |Bt | and k =
862   1, . . . , K, ZVF is computed as in Eq. (1). The reference implementation is:
863   def zvf(rewards_2d: np.ndarray, eps: float = 1e-6) -> float:
864       # rewards_2d shape: [num_groups, K]
865       var_per_group = rewards_2d.var(axis=-1, ddof=1)
866       return float((var_per_group <= eps).mean())

914   E     Reconciling the Group-Size Result: Token-Budget-Normalized Sweeps

915   E.1   The Apparent Contradiction

916   A reviewer correctly identified that Table 6 reports G=4 as the highest last-10 reward (52.1%) under
917   a fixed-step budget, while the main text claims that G≈32 “maximizes gradient utilization”. These
918   observations are compatible but require the axis of comparison to be made explicit. Under a fixed
919   number of optimizer steps, larger G costs proportionally more tokens per step, so a step-budget
920   comparison benefits small G; under a fixed total token budget, larger G amortizes wasted (all-correct
921   or all-incorrect) rollouts and yields higher effective gradient signal per token.

933   E.3   Token-Budget-Normalized Sweep

934   We re-analyze the G-sweep under a fixed token rather than step budget. Let T denote total optimizer-
935   visible tokens. At fixed T , the number of optimizer steps is nstep (G) = T /(G · K · L̄), which
936   decreases with G. The question is whether the larger per-step gradient signal of larger G outweighs
937   the reduced number of updates.

938   Accuracy-optimal G shifts with budget. The per-row accuracy-maximizing group size increases
939   with the token budget (G⋆ = 8, 16, 32, 32 at T = 1, 4, 16, 64 M) in the reconstructed grid, so the
940   hypothesis is budget-dependent. We report this as a qualitative argmax pattern to test—no trained
941   matched-budget G=32 arm validates it and no committed script fits a parametric inverted-U envelope
942   or its bootstrap confidence interval, so we make no such quantitative claim.

943   E.4   Reconciliation Statement

944   We therefore revise the main-text claim as follows.

945            Under a fixed step budget at small total token counts, G=4 attains the highest
946            last-10 reward (52.1%, Table 6). Under fixed total tokens at the canonical training
947            scale used elsewhere in the paper (T ≥ 16M), G≈32 maximizes reconstructed
948            held-out accuracy in this illustrative reanalysis; it is not a measured recommen-
949            dation. (The per-token gradient-efficiency estimator GE  dtok is not co-maximized:
950            it decreases monotonically in G, so it favors small G and is reported only to
951            show that the accuracy gain at large G comes at a per-token efficiency cost.) The
952            accuracy-optimal G shifts rightward with T , so the recommended G depends on
953            the practitioner’s compute budget, not on a universal heuristic.

954   The reviewer’s concern is taken seriously: the “more rollouts is always better” heuristic is false,
955   and the opposite heuristic “small G is always better” is equally false; the correct claim is that
956   there exists a budget-dependent optimum and practitioners should locate it via the reanalysis script
957   experiments/group_size_token_normalized.py.

 974   This statement is written to satisfy the NeurIPS Code of Ethics and Broader Impact requirements.
 975   It complements the shorter Limitations and Broader Impact subsections embedded in Section 6 by
 976   consolidating in one place a full dual-use analysis, itemised compute accounting, carbon footprint
 977   estimation, data provenance disclosures, a candid acknowledgment of the closed-source training

 980   F.1   Dual-Use Analysis: Misuse Risks of Reasoning RL

 981   Threat model. RL-F INETUNING B ENCH releases (i) training scripts that apply GRPO to the
 982   GSM8K [15] mathematical reasoning benchmark and to the Salesforce xLAM-Function-Calling-
 983   60k [57] tool-use corpus, (ii) a set of LoRA adapters published on the HuggingFace Hub
 984   (anonymous/tinker-rl-bench-*), and (iii) diagnostic code for Zero-Variance Fraction, reward-
 985   stability, and length-bias analysis. We analyse four concrete misuse pathways these artefacts could,
 986   in principle, enable, and we describe the existing guardrails and the residual risk we judge acceptable
 987   for publication.

 988   M1. Weaponisation of mathematical reasoning. The most capability-relevant artefact we release
 989   is GRPO training code that improves grade-school arithmetic and multi-step reasoning. A malicious
 990   operator could attempt to extend this pipeline to tasks of greater dual-use concern, e.g., chemistry
 991   olympiad problems, cryptographic key recovery, or financial fraud. We judge the marginal uplift
 992   provided by our release to be small for three reasons. First, GRPO at our training scale does not
 993   create new capabilities; the base-model control for GSM8K (Section 4, 82.0%) shows that the bulk
 994   of held-out accuracy is attributable to Qwen3-8B’s pretraining. Our full GRPO run only adds +1.3
 995   percentage points (p=0.26, not statistically significant). Second, the public literature already contains
 996   GRPO implementations [102, 83, 103, 117] that are at least as capable. Third, any reasoning uplift is
 997   bounded by the rewarder: GSM8K rewards use exact-match against a human-written gold answer,
 998   which does not generalise to the domains listed above without a new reward signal, which itself
999    requires expertise and labelled data.

1000   M2. Reward hacking and the long-term alignment risk. GRPO, like all policy-gradient RL
1001   from a proxy reward, is vulnerable to reward hacking: the model learns to exploit idiosyncrasies
1002   of the reward function rather than solve the intended task [108, 47]. We observed verbosity drift in
1003   some of our own GRPO runs, which is a concrete demonstration of this failure mode in our own data.
1004   Users who apply our scripts to under-specified reward functions in production settings (e.g., a custom
1005   “helpfulness” classifier) may observe models that appear to improve on held-out metrics while silently
1006   optimising against unintended proxies. We include the Zero-Variance Fraction and reward-stability
1007   diagnostics precisely so that downstream users can detect such drift.

1008   M3. Circumventing safety training via distillation or tool-use. The tool-use track trains LoRA
1009   adapters that teach a base model to emit schema-valid JSON function calls [57]. A motivated
1010   adversary could in principle use the same pipeline to teach a base model to emit jailbreak prompts as
1011   “tool calls” or to invoke an adversarial external tool. We mitigate this by (a) releasing only adapters,
1012   not merged weights, so that the safety-trained instruct checkpoint must be applied separately and any
1013   instruct-layer guardrails remain active; (b) documenting in model cards that the tool-use adapters are
1014   trained on a narrow schema (five synthetic tools) and have not been safety-evaluated for open-ended
1015   function calling; and (c) not releasing any function-calling harness that would actually execute emitted
1016   calls.


                                                          24

[PAGE 25]
1017   M4. Compute-efficiency externalities. Our work argues that critic-free RL with budget-dependent
1018   group-size selection (the optimal G depends on total token budget; see Appendix E) can be run
1019   on modest budgets. Improved training efficiency is itself dual-use: it lowers the cost of running
1020   adversarial fine-tuning at scale. We judge this externality to be modest relative to the already-low
1021   cost of fine-tuning a 0.6B–8B model with commodity LoRA recipes [35], but we flag it explicitly.

1022   Residual risk. After weighing M1–M4 we judge that the reproducibility, calibration, and peda-
1023   gogical benefits of releasing RL-F INETUNING B ENCH outweigh the residual misuse risk. We adopt
1024   the standard mitigation of releasing adapters (not merged weights), documenting intended use and
1025   out-of-scope use in model cards, and publishing alongside the diagnostics a researcher would need to
1026   detect reward hacking.

1122   F.5   Closed-Source Tinker Acknowledgment

1123   A central tension in RL-F INETUNING B ENCH is that a substantial fraction of our headline numbers
1124   come from a closed-source commercial platform while our paper simultaneously advocates for
1125   reproducibility and platform independence. We do not resolve this tension by hiding it; we describe it
1126   precisely.

1127   What Tinker is and is not. Tinker [114] is a managed LLM fine-tuning and inference service
1128   provided by Thinking Machines, Inc. It exposes a Python SDK (tinker==0.16.1) that accepts
1129   custom loss functions (forward_backward_custom), a limited set of optimisers, and standard LoRA
1130   hyperparameters. It does not expose (i) the exact server-side GRPO loss implementation, (ii) reward
1131   normalisation or baseline subtraction scheme, (iii) minibatch construction or gradient-accumulation
1132   strategy, (iv) hardware configuration (GPU type, inter-node bandwidth), or (v) system-level telemetry
1133   (energy, throughput, queueing).

1134   What this means for our claims. Tinker results in this paper measure the platform’s implementa-
1135   tion of GRPO, not an abstract specification of the algorithm. Cross-stack reward gaps between Tinker
1136   GRPO and an open-source GRPO implementation cannot be fully attributed from our data: we are
1137   able to rule out a handful of candidate explanations (seed variance, model-size confound) but not
1138   the implementation itself. We therefore draw quantitative conclusions only from the open-source
1139   side (TRL, veRL on Modal H100, OpenRLHF) where every hyperparameter is auditable, and use
1140   Tinker results primarily as descriptive case studies of what a carefully engineered production stack
1141   can achieve for critic-free RL at our scales.

1142   Reproducibility commitments we can make.

1143         1. All Tinker experiment scripts, configuration files, JSON step logs, and per-run W&B projects
1144            are archived in the repository. Researchers with Tinker access can attempt replication with
1145            our exact configurations.
1146         2. Every figure and every summary statistic derived solely from Tinker data is marked with “†”
1147            and a footnote stating that independent replication requires Tinker API access.
1148         3. Primary inferential claims are limited to (i) the mechanical zero-gradient theorem (Ap-
1149            pendix C) and (ii) the held-out 5-seed Qwen3-8B GSM8K control reported in Section 6.
1150            The Tinker-derived stratified ZVF correlations (Section 4.2) are reported as artifact analyses
1151            with explicit closed-backend caveats and single-seed-per-cell limitations, not as platform-
1152            independent statistical conclusions.
1153         4. We commit to re-running any Tinker-only experiment on an open backend if an equivalent
1154            open-source service becomes available, and to issuing a revised version of this paper if any
1155            Tinker result is ever shown to be due to an undisclosed implementation choice rather than
1156            the algorithm.

1162   F.6   Known Methodological Limits

1163   In addition to the infrastructure failures and platform-specific caveats enumerated in Section 6, we
1164   flag the following methodological limits.

1165   Short training horizons. All Tinker GRPO runs were capped at 30–50 gradient steps. This is a
1166   deliberate cost-control choice and means our Tinker numbers are early-training snapshots. We cannot
1167   rule out that longer training would change the qualitative picture (e.g., the 82%→83.3% held-out
1168   gain might widen, or might collapse to reward hacking).

1169   Single-seed Tinker experiments. Each Tinker configuration was run once, not 3–5 times as
1170   best practice prescribes [27]. Tinker results therefore lack variance estimates and do not support
1171   significance testing; we report them descriptively. Only the 5-seed TRL baseline and the 5-seed
1172   held-out GSM8K evaluation carry proper confidence intervals.

1173   Train-set reward as primary Tinker metric. Tinker runs primarily report reward on training
1174   prompts. Only the GSM8K track was followed up with a separate 200-example held-out evaluation
1175   per seed. Other Tinker tracks (tool-use, xLAM) remain train-set-only and we cannot distinguish
1176   memorisation from generalisation for those settings.

1177   LoRA only; no full fine-tuning. All experiments use LoRA [35] with ranks 8–64. We have
1178   not tested whether full fine-tuning would re-order the library/algorithm comparisons. The LoRA
1179   constraint is practical (cost) but it means our claims about PPO vs. GRPO and TRL vs. Tinker are
1180   LoRA-conditional.

1181   Benchmark coverage is narrow. We evaluate four domains: GSM8K, MATH-500 (exploratory),
1182   HumanEval (subset), and synthetic/xLAM tool-use. We do not evaluate on MT-Bench, ArenaHard,
1183   safety benchmarks (HarmBench, ToxicChat), or truthfulness benchmarks (TruthfulQA). Any claim
1184   about “reasoning” in the paper is strictly a claim about the covered benchmarks.

1185   No human preference data. We use verifiable rewards only (exact-match for math, unit-test for
1186   code, schema-validity for tool-use). We do not study RLHF with human preference data; findings
1187   should not be extrapolated to reward-model–based alignment without further work.

1188   Closed-source implementation opacity (reiterated). Comparisons between Tinker GRPO and
1189   TRL GRPO are confounded by the closed-source nature of Tinker’s implementation. See Section F.5
1190   for detail.

1191   Cross-platform hardware confounding. Tinker, Modal, Colab, and the Institutional A100 node
1192   use different accelerators, different memory hierarchies, and different inference/training stacks
1193   (Tinker proprietary, Modal vLLM, Colab PyTorch-native, TRL+Accelerate). Observed differences in
1194   reward dynamics are not purely algorithmic.

1195   Carbon estimates are approximate. As discussed in Section F.3, Tinker GPU-hour and TDP are
1196   inferred, not measured. Grid intensity is region-average, not marginal. Numbers in Table 13 should
1197   be treated as order-of-magnitude.

1198   Exploratory, not confirmatory. This is an exploratory case study, not a pre-registered confirmatory
1199   experiment. We did not pre-register primary hypotheses. All p-values are un-corrected for the number
1200   of comparisons actually made across the paper; the Bonferroni-surviving comparisons in Section 4
1201   are so flagged, but most of our secondary analyses are descriptive.

1203   withheld here for anonymity but disclosed in the non-anonymous version of the paper. Our choice of
