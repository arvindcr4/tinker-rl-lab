                                                  Abstract
         The Zero-Variance Fraction (ZVF) is the fraction of prompt groups whose within-group
     reward variance is zero in group-relative RL with binary rewards. It is a useful mechanistic
     diagnostic, but calibration does not automatically grant control authority. We separate those
     questions through three conditional results. T1 treats ZVF correctly as a sample mean of
     Bernoulli group indicators and gives binomial-proportion confidence intervals; under curriculum
     ordering these intervals cover a local-stage estimand unless batch composition is stratified. T2
     converts a population ZVF into a reliability budget: the number of independent groups required
     to observe at least one informative group with probability 1 − δ. This bounds rollouts to
     a nonzero reward-gradient event, not rollouts to policy improvement. T3 audits a proposed
     signal-per-rollout objective. Under that declared objective its optimum collapses to the prior-
     independent tie G ∈ {2, 3}; it therefore supplies no data-adaptive group-size set-point. A
     richer variance-, Fisher-, wall-clock-, or outcome-aware objective is required before theory can
     justify adaptive G. Checked-in sampling experiments validate the operational accounting, while
     every assumption and scope boundary is stated inline. The result is a calibrated sensor and a
     conservative reliability trigger—not a proof that a particular controller or group size improves
     held-out performance.



1    Introduction
The ZVF Program’s Pillar 1 contributed a mechanism: in group-relative, critic-free RL (GRPO and
relatives [3]), the policy gradient of a prompt-group is mediated entirely by the within-group spread
of rewards. When all K rollouts of a group collapse to the same binary outcome, the group-relative
advantage is identically zero and the group contributes no gradient. The ZVF counts the fraction of
such “dead” groups in a batch. Pillar 1 made this definition rigorous, showed it is not a tautological
re-expression of the mean reward, and validated it as a portable early-warning signal [1].
    A mechanistic diagnostic, however, is a measurement, and a measurement is only as good as its
calibration, its predictive content, and its actionability. Three gaps remain before ZVF can drive a
training-time controller rather than merely annotate a training curve:
1. Identifiability / calibration. The reported ZVFt is an estimate from finitely many groups.
   Two runs reporting ZVFt = 0.86 and 0.81 may be statistically indistinguishable. We need

    This paper supplies one theorem per gap (T1–T3). We deliberately keep notation aligned with
the Pillar 1 appendix and with the U-statistic analysis of Zhou et al. [9]. The mixed-group proba-
bility used below is the elementary Bernoulli identity 1 − pG − (1 − p)G , so it requires no separate
attribution. This alignment lets the three results compose with existing work rather than re-deriving
it.

Related work: interventions without a measurement theory. The phenomenon ZVF mea-
sures is no longer a niche observation. The TMLR survey of agentic RL [6] catalogs, in a single
table, more than twenty GRPO-family variants, and a striking fraction of them intervene directly
on dead groups: DAPO’s dynamic sampling drops zero-gradient groups online [5]; GRESO filters
prompts before rollout when they are predicted to produce single-outcome groups [8]; EDGE-GRPO
reshapes advantages explicitly to mitigate what its authors — and now the survey — call advantage
collapse [7]; DARS reallocates rollout budget from medium-difficulty prompts to the hardest ones
[4]. We adopt advantage collapse as the field’s name for the phenomenon and read this convergence
as evidence for the premise of the ZVF Program: the dead-group fraction is a first-order quantity in
critic-free RL. Our position, stated plainly, is that every one of these methods is a controller
acting on a signal it never calibrates. Each filters, reweights, or reallocates based on an im-
plicit point estimate of the very quantity whose sampling distribution (T1), compute cost (T2),
and optimal set-point (T3) this paper supplies. A filtering rule triggered by an uncalibrated ZVF
estimate is a decision made on a point estimate masquerading as a measurement (§3); a reallocation
rule without a wasted-compute bound cannot say what it saves (§4); and an adaptive group-size
heuristic without an optimality target has no set-point to track (§5). The theory below is therefore

under the assumptions stated below. This does not make the assumptions empirically universal, and
T3 does not elevate its declared proxy into a uniquely correct learning objective. The companion

Standing assumptions. We collect the assumptions the three results lean on; each theorem cites
the subset it needs.

Assumption 1 (Binary verifiable rewards). rg,k ∈ {0, 1}, produced by a deterministic verifier on
policy samples.

Assumption 2 (Within-group i.i.d.). Conditional on xg and π, the K rollouts of group g are i.i.d.
Bernoulli(pg ).

Assumption 3 (Across-group i.i.d. prompts). The prompts (hence p1 , . . . , pm ) in a batch are
i.i.d. draws from a fixed population distribution. Curriculum sampling, replay, and within-epoch
correlation violate this assumption; in those settings T1 estimates a local stage quantity unless the
design or variance estimator is changed.

Assumption 4 (Non-degenerate support). The reward-density prior ϕ assigns positive mass to
(0, 1), so that Eϕ [hK (p)] ∈ (0, 1) and the population ZVF is interior (rules out the saturated and
trivial regimes flagged in Pillar 1’s scope section).

Remark 1 (Connection to “Demystifying GRPO” and mixed-group probability). The kernel hK (p) =
pK +(1−p)K is the complement of the mixed-group probability 1−hK (p), an elementary consequence
of independent Bernoulli trials. T1 turns that per-prompt probability into a calibrated batch-level
estimand with a CI. Zhou et al. [9] instead analyze the GRPO policy gradient as a U-statistic and
derive group-size scaling results. The present Bernoulli estimand is complementary: it quantifies
whether a sampled group supplies any reward contrast, not the full policy-gradient distribution. No
stronger equivalence between the two analyses is claimed here.


4    T2 — A Wasted-Compute Lower Bound
We now show that a high population ZVF yields a reliability budget for observing a non-degenerate
reward-gradient event. The result does not imply a policy-improvement guarantee.

What “improvement is possible” means. A GRPO update at step t produces a nonzero gra-
dient only if at least one group in the batch is informative, i.e. has Zg = 0 (non-uniform outcomes).

5    T3 — Auditing a Signal-per-Rollout Group-Size Objective
T2 says high ZVF raises the reliability budget but does not say what action improves learning.
T3 audits a natural signal-per-rollout proxy. Its failure to produce a data-dependent optimum is a
result: this objective cannot supply an adaptive controller set-point.

Learning signal per group. For binary rewards at group size G, a natural scalar measure of
the gradient “signal” a single group contributes is the within-group reward variance the advantage
normalization exposes. The expected (population) within-group signal of a prompt with success rate
p is proportional to its Bernoulli variance p(1 − p), but only realized when the group is informative;
we therefore model the expected per-group signal as

6    Connection to the Pillar 1 Adaptive Controller
Scope note (2026-07-11 canonicalization). This paper is canonical for the theorem-level treatment
(T1–T3 and their proof gaps); the controller’s engineering — trigger design, hysteresis, multi-
seed validation, and design rules — is treated in the companion Pillar-7 paper “From Diagnostic
to Controller: A Signal-Starvation Theory of GRPO and an Adaptive Group-Size Intervention”
(platform_hybrid/paper/paper_P7_zvf_controller).
    The three results compose into an online control law that the Pillar 1 controller can execute per
training step t:

1. Measure (T1). Compute ZVFt and its 95% CI (5); this turns a point estimate into a calibrated
   reading with a lower confidence bound ZVFt .
2. Decide (T2). If the reliability budget N (ZVFt ) from Corollary 1 exceeds the remaining per-step
   rollout budget, the step fails to produce any informative group with probability greater than
   δ (at the stated confidence on ZVF): trigger intervention rather than gamble the remaining
   budget.
3. Act (T3, revised). Under objective (9) the per-rollout optimum is the universal G⋆ ∈ {2, 3}
   (eq. 13), so the controller cannot derive a data-dependent group size from T3 as stated; re-
   estimating ϕbt adds nothing. The escalation policy must instead come from the T2 reliability
   budget: raise G when the current G’s budget-failure probability exceeds δ.

This is the formal interface between Pillar 2 (theory) and Pillar 1 (the mechanistic controller): T1
supplies sensor calibration, T2 supplies a reliability trigger, and T3 rules out the proposed proxy as
a data-adaptive actuator set-point. Any escalation rule remains an empirical policy until a richer
objective and a prospective matched-budget test support it. The obsolete recursion G⋆ ← ϕb ← data
is not part of this controller because T3 proves that the proxy optimum is prior-independent.
The remaining finite-action escalation rule is an empirical policy with hysteresis; its closed-loop
performance is tested prospectively in Pillar 7 rather than asserted as a theorem here.

The Wald interval (5) is adequate at m ≥ 64 and the Wilson interval is well calibrated everywhere;
we adopt Wilson operationally. The curriculum-ordering “failure” is an estimand mismatch: the
interval remains calibrated for the local (curriculum-stage) ZVF while losing the global one, and
stratified batch composition restores global validity (conservatively). This resolves the across-group-
i.i.d. threat (Assumption 3) into a labeling requirement rather than an invalidation.

E-T2: the waiting-time quantile. With G=8 and δ=0.1, the geometric (1−δ)-quantile G⌈ln δ/ ln ZVF⌉
matched the observed (1−δ)-quantile of rollouts-to-first-informative-group within 0–5% in all six dif-
ficulty strata. In the hardest stratum (p̂ ≈ 0.01, ZVF = 0.886) the quantile is 160 rollouts (ratio
1.00). The geometric waiting-time model is essentially exact on this reward distribution. Note
what is validated: a reliability budget — the rollouts needed to make a fully uninformative batch
improbable at level δ — not a minimum; informative groups typically arrive much sooner.

E-T3a: the optimal group size (reinterpreted). The measured J-argmax was 2–3 in every
stratum and pooled, agreeing with the analytic argmax. This agreement is now understood
to be algebraically guaranteed, not an empirical test: by (13), J(2) = J(3) exactly and
G⋆ ∈ {2, 3} for every prior, so the measurement can only confirm the objective’s arithmetic (which
it does — a model-fit sanity check); it cannot validate a data-dependent prediction, because the
objective makes none. What survives is objective-relative: if signal-per-rollout (9) is the accepted
accounting, prevailing G ∈ [8, 16] practice is several-fold inefficient per rollout. A first training-side
corroboration: a G=2 adapter matched a G=16 adapter’s held-out pass@32 improvement at 8×
fewer rollouts per step (single seed; extended to a two-seed matched-budget panel in E-R2b below,
which also shows the endgame cost of small G). Experiment E-T3b under a richer objective remains
the decisive test.

E-B: controller pilot (offline). Treating stratum selection as a Thompson-sampling bandit with
reward = “group is informative” recovers 97% of the static-oracle’s informative groups per 1,000
rollouts (120.7 vs. 124.3), beats uniform allocation by 28%, and exceeds a hardest-first policy by
8.8× — difficulty and signal are not the same axis, exactly as J(G, p) predicts. Caveat: frozen-pool
simulation; the training-time pilot must confirm under the distribution shift induced by learning
itself.

E-R2b: matched-budget training at G=2 vs. G=16. Four LoRA (rank 4) training runs on
Qwen3-8B/GSM8K at a matched budget of 2,560 rollouts per arm — G=2×160 steps vs. G=16×20

sustained ZVF ≈ 0.75–1.0: the all-correct zero-variance wall, the mirror image of T2’s all-incorrect
wall. The G=16 arms end mid-learning (train reward ≈ 0.3–0.5 after their 20 steps) with ZVF at
0–0.25 throughout and signal intact. Small G buys more optimizer steps per rollout early, then
exhausts its own gradient signal as p̂ → 1; group-size policy is therefore a schedule question, exactly
the regime the controller of Section 6 targets. Artifacts: tinker-runs/results/er2b_*.json.

E-P4: advantage normalization and the length-bias premise. An uncapped (1,024-token)
six-arm panel — GRPO vs. Dr.GRPO’s /σ-free advantage [2], three seeds each, 30 steps, G=8,
batch 4, Qwen3-8B/GSM8K — tests the premise that std-normalized advantages inflate completion
length. Neither loss inflates length: mean completion length declines 3.8–12.2% over training in
all six arms (GRPO 1004→905, 981→944, 996→900; Dr.GRPO 999→931, 972→902, 1000→878
tokens). Nor does the /σ removal show a ZVF signature: late-run ZVF is 0.45/0.70/0.72 (Dr.GRPO)
vs. 0.47/0.47/0.55 (GRPO). At this scale the length-bias motivation for the normalization change
has no observable footprint on either axis (Tier C, n=3 seeds per loss). Provenance: an earlier
version of this panel was invalidated by an unwired loss flag — both “arms” had trained vanilla
GRPO — and was caught only by reading the runner, not from any output-level signal; the invalid
artifacts are preserved under explicit .invalid_actually_grpo.json names and the panel was
rerun (2026-07-11). The companion survival-audit paper turns this class of failure into protocol.

Threats to validity. Single base model and task family (Qwen3-8B, GSM8K); binary rewards
only; p̂ plug-in bias at R=32 rollouts per prompt; and training-side corroborations use two seeds
per arm (E-R2b) or three per loss (E-P4). These measurements do not establish the assumptions
on other models, tasks, or reward types.


8    Discussion and Limitations of the Theory
What is conditionally established. Under i.i.d. groups and binary verifiable rewards: (T1)
ZVFt is an unbiased, asymptotically normal estimator of a well-defined population quantity with a
closed-form CI; (T2) high ZVF implies a logarithmically diverging reliability budget — the (1−δ)-
quantile of rollouts to a nonzero update; (T3) the signal-per-rollout objective has a well-defined
optimum, which is the universal G⋆ ∈ {2, 3} (eq. 13), prior-independent.

What is not established.

 • T1 is a binomial-proportion result, not a new U-statistic theorem. A different degree-2
   estimand would require a separate degeneracy analysis; no such claim is made here.
 • T2 bounds rollouts-to-nonzero-gradient, not rollouts-to-improvement. Corollary 1
   supplies an exact- coverage lower bound when the population ZVF is estimated.
 • T3’s objective S(p, G) is a modelling choice, and under it the optimum is the universal,
   prior-independent {2, 3} (eq. 13) — so T3 as stated supports no data-adaptive group-size policy;
   a richer objective and prospective experiment are required for any adaptive-G⋆ claim.

• Across-group i.i.d. (Assumption 3) underlies all three and is violated by curriculum,
   replay, and within-epoch correlation. Resolved empirically (E-T1): under curriculum ordering
   the CI remains calibrated for the local (curriculum-stage) ZVF — the quantity a stage-local
   controller acts on — while global inference requires stratified batch composition. The threat is
   thus an estimand-labeling requirement, not an invalidation.
