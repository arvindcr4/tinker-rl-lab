                                                   Abstract
 1            We release RL-F INETUNING B ENCH, a reproducible artifact for auditing
 2            reinforcement-learning post-training of language models across heterogeneous
 3            stacks. The artifact bundles 79 runs from 7 RL libraries, 5 model families, and
 4            total-parameter scales from 0.6B to 671B (active-parameter counts 0.6B–37B for
 5            the largest mixture-of-experts checkpoints) on GSM8K, MATH-500, HumanEval,
 6            and synthetic tool-use tasks; for every reported run we ship pinned containers, step-
 7            level reward traces, raw rollout JSONL where licensing allows, and Zero-Variance
 8            Fraction (ZVF) diagnostics.
 9            We organize all claims into three explicit evidence tiers. (A-grade, analytic or
10            multi-seed inferential): a mechanical ZVF = pG + (1−p)G identity for binary-
11            reward GRPO (analytic, not an empirical multi-seed claim), and a 5-seed held-out
12            Qwen3-8B GSM8K control showing only a +1.3pp delta over the base model
13            (p=0.26, not significant). (B-grade, single-seed descriptive): case-study runs
14            across model scale on the closed-backend Tinker API, including group-size sweeps
15            and frontier-scale checkpoints, reported as illustrative not inferential. (C-grade,
16            listed for honesty): failed or partially completed cells, enumerated in the run
17            manifest. The artifact’s value is auditability: every (backend, model, task) cell is
18            tier-classified, every log type is named (precomputed traces, step-level aggregates,
19            raw rollouts), and every closed-backend dependency is flagged.
20            We also release a stratified ZVF diagnostic suite that documents how pooled
21            ZVF-vs-performance correlations are dominated by task identity rather than a
22            within-task predictive law—a finding we treat as descriptive instrumentation, not a
23            causal claim.

27   1   Introduction
28   Reinforcement learning post-training now underpins much of modern language-model reasoning and
29   alignment [84, 20], but empirical claims in this area are hard to compare across stacks. PPO [101],
30   GRPO [102], and DPO [95] are typically evaluated on different model families, with different
31   framework defaults, different reward functions, and different notions of success. The field needs
32   auditable, tier-classified artifacts more than it needs another leaderboard.

33   Artifact-first contribution. RL-F INETUNING B ENCH is released as a reproducible artifact: 79

51   Two narrow scientific take-aways. The artifact supports two A-grade results stated conservatively.
52   First, the mechanical identity ZVF(p, G) = pG + (1−p)G characterises when binary-reward GRPO
53   produces zero within-group gradient; we use it as a descriptive diagnostic, not as a standalone causal
54   predictor of downstream performance, because ZVF is mechanically coupled to reward sparsity,
55   group size, and baseline accuracy. Second, a 5-seed held-out Qwen3-8B GSM8K control yields only
56   a +1.3pp delta over the base model (p=0.26), so strong online reward curves on a closed backend
57   do not by themselves establish generalization gains. All B-grade single-seed case-study runs across
58   model scale are reported as illustrative.

59   Stratified ZVF diagnostics. A separate stratified diagnostic suite documents that the pooled
60   ZVF-vs-performance correlation is dominated by task identity: within GSM8K alone the apparent
61   monotone relationship does not survive (Pearson r = +0.40, p = 0.09 across the 19 Tinker GSM8K
62   runs; r = +0.13, p = 0.79 across the 7 model-varying GSM8K cells). We release these stratified
63   analyses precisely so that follow-up work can audit them against richer multi-seed corpora.

64   2     Related Work
65   RL-F INETUNING B ENCH sits at the intersection of five rapidly evolving lines of work: policy-
66   gradient algorithms for language models, RL for reasoning (including process reward models), RL
67   for tool use and agentic behaviour, empirical scaling laws for RL post-training, and the frameworks
68   and benchmarks that instantiate these algorithms. We situate our contribution against more than fifty
69   recent works; entries marked with † appeared at NeurIPS, ICML, or ICLR in the 2025–2026 cycle.

70   2.1   RL Algorithms for Large Language Models

71   Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) [101] is the workhorse algorithm behind InstructGPT-style
72   RLHF [84, 14, 110, 7, 8] and has dominated production deployments for five years. Direct Preference
73   Optimization [95] reframed preference learning as contrastive classification and spawned a large
74   family of RL-free alternatives, including IPO [6], KTO [21], SimPO [72], ORPO [34], and SLiC [142],
75   all of which we consider as no-rollout baselines in our cross-library protocol.
76   The current wave is defined by group-relative policy gradient methods. GRPO was introduced with
77   DeepSeekMath [102] and popularised by DeepSeek-R1 [19, 20], the first reasoning RL method pub-
78   lished in Nature, which showed that pure outcome-reward RL can elicit chain-of-thought behaviour
79   from a base model without supervised cold-start data. A second wave of refinements dissects the
80   biases of vanilla GRPO. Dr. GRPO [61] removes a response-length bias and a question-difficulty
81   amplification bias by using a constant loss normaliser and dropping the standard-deviation denomi-
82   nator in the advantage. GDPO [58] decouples group-relative normalisation per reward component,
83   preventing advantage collapse when rewards are multi-valued. MC-GRPO (median-centered ad-
84   vantage) [44] replaces the group-mean baseline in the advantage with the group median to stabilise


                                                         2

[PAGE 3]
85   group-relative normalisation under small rollout counts. G2 RPO [38] forces per-prompt advantages
 86   to match N (0, 1) to fix reward-topology variance in multimodal settings. Wu et al. [125] prove that
 87   GRPO is “secretly DPO” — the group-level advantage is an implicit contrastive objective — and
 88   show that 2-GRPO retains 98.1% of the performance of 16-GRPO at 12.5% of the rollout budget.
 89   A parallel thread focuses on production-scale refinements. GSPO, from the Qwen team [143],
 90   redefines the importance ratio at the sequence level (rather than token level) and was credited with sta-
 91   bilising the Qwen3 series, especially its Mixture-of-Experts variants. DAPO, from ByteDance [134],
 92   contributes asymmetric Clip-Higher, dynamic sampling, token-level loss normalisation, and overlong
 93   filtering, reaching 50 points on AIME 2024 with a 32B model. Kimi-K1.5 [46, 45] and DeepSeek-
 94   V3 [18] both report their own GRPO variants and online mirror-descent-style updates. Orthogonal
 95   production variants include VAPO [10] and Dr. SAC [3]; the last argues that classical REINFORCE
 96   with a value baseline, when carefully implemented, matches GRPO on several RLHF tasks.
 97   A third thread attacks the zero-variance failure mode (ZVF) directly. GRESO [144] pre-screens
 98   prompts before sampling; CPPO [55] prunes completions post-rollout, giving up to 7.98× speedup
 99   on GSM8K. NGRPO [75] adds Advantage Calibration and asymmetric clipping so that all-incorrect
100   groups still carry gradient. Scaf-GRPO [139] injects tiered scaffolding hints when learning stagnates,
101   boosting AIME 2024 by 44.3% relative. MO-GRPO [40] addresses vanilla GRPO’s collapse to a
102   single reward component under multiple objectives via variance-based reward re-weighting. Finally,
103   SSPO [129] operates at sentence granularity to balance the stability of GSPO against the expressive-
104   ness of token-level GRPO, improving by 3.56 points on math benchmarks. Taken together, these
105   results explain at a theoretical level why implementation details at the granularity of the importance
106   ratio have such large empirical effects — an explanation our benchmark complements with a 73 pp
107   cross-library measurement.

108   2.2   Reasoning RL and Process Reward Models

109   The shift from outcome rewards to process supervision has reshaped reasoning RL. Let’s-Verify-
110   Step-by-Step [54] showed that step-level PRMs dramatically outperform outcome reward models on
111   GSM8K and MATH. Math-Shepherd [120] derives process rewards from auto-generated process anno-
112   tations, avoiding costly human labels. PRM800K [115] and PRMBench [109] provide evaluation data
113   for step-level scoring. OmegaPRM [66] uses Monte-Carlo tree search to supervise process rewards
114   without human-in-the-loop, while ReasonEval [126] introduces reasoning-specific reward models.
115   Implicit PRM [135] learns PRMs implicitly from outcome labels, and VersaPRM [136] extends pro-
116   cess rewards to multi-domain tasks. These supervision signals are the backbone of recent reasoning
117   models, including OpenAI’s o1/o3 family [80, 82], DeepSeek-R1 [19, 20], Qwen2.5-Math [128],
118   Qwen3 [94], Gemini 2.5 Pro’s deliberative mode [24], and Kimi-K2 [45]. Complementary results
119   demonstrate that self-consistency [122], tree-of-thought search [132], and critic-style post-hoc ver-
120   ifiers [71] all benefit from or substitute for process reward supervision. VerifyBench [141] and
121   A Sober Look [31] warn, however, that reward hacking and evaluation artefacts can inflate perceived
122   gains, motivating our insistence on binary verifiable rewards for mathematical reasoning.
123   Reasoning-specific algorithmic contributions also continue to accumulate. ReFT [67] studies su-
124   pervised fine-tuning versus RL for reasoning. Let’s Reward Step [140] and STaR [137] bootstrap
125   reasoning chains from weak supervisors. rStar-Math [25] combines MCTS-based PRMs with
126   self-evolved reasoning. ReST-EM [107] alternates expectation and maximisation steps over self-
127   generated rationales. Self-Taught Evaluator [121] adapts an LLM to score its own reasoning chains.
128   GRPO-Lead [64], LCPO [65], and LIMR [52] each tune reasoning depth with length-aware rewards.
129   Search-R1 [41] trains reasoning agents to interleave search queries with chain-of-thought. TTRL [91]
130   performs test-time RL against self-generated verifiers. We use the Dr. GRPO [61] and GSPO [143]
131   formulations as reference implementations when measuring reasoning accuracy across the 0.6B–235B
132   frontier.

133   2.3   Tool-Use and Agentic RL

134   Tool-use RL trains LLMs to interleave natural-language reasoning with external API calls. Tool-
135   former [100] introduced self-supervised tool-call insertion for a small set of APIs; Gorilla [87]
136   retrieved from a large API catalogue; and ToolLLM [93] scaled training to thousands of real-world
137   REST APIs. ReAct [131] established the interleaved reason–act–observe pattern that most subse-

138   quent agents inherit. RL specifically for tool use was pioneered by ToolACE [59], which couples a
139   tool-calling reward with a self-evolving synthesis pipeline; ToolRL [92] applies GRPO with verifiable
140   tool-call rewards; ToRL [53] optimises the joint reasoning–action trajectory. Further advances include
141   APIGen [56] (high-quality synthetic API data), Nexus-Raven [76], and Octopus [13]. Agentic RL
142   extends tool use to long-horizon tasks: WebGPT [74] and WebShop [130] were the first large-scale
143   web agents, Reflexion [105] and Voyager [119] add episodic self-reflection, and AutoGPT-style
144   agents have been formalised by AgentBench [60]. 2025–2026 produced a wave of RL-trained agents:
145   SWE-RL [124] trains coding agents with execution rewards; SWE-Gym [85] provides an RL training
146   environment for real GitHub issues; ARTIST [106] trains tool-integrated reasoning agents with
147   GRPO; RAGEN [123] studies multi-turn RL with tool access; OpenAI’s Deep Research [81] and
148   Perplexity Deep Research [89] both report GRPO-style training over web-browsing traces. Our
149   benchmark currently focuses on single-turn verifiable mathematical reasoning; we adopt Tinker’s
150   message_with_tool interface as the neutral substrate for future tool-use extensions and explicitly
151   cite these works as concurrent settings in which RL-F INETUNING B ENCH’s ZVF measurements will
152   need to be re-evaluated.


153   2.4   Scaling Laws for RL Post-Training

154   Compute-optimal training of base language models is governed by well-known scaling laws [43, 33,
155   73], but RL post-training is much less well characterised. Hilton et al. [30] first derived compute-
156   efficiency frontiers for RL in games and robotics. For RLHF, Gao et al. [22] characterise the
157   over-optimisation curve of reward models, and Rafailov et al. [96] provide scaling laws for DPO
158   and PPO that show a strong dependence on reward-model quality. Nimmaturi et al. [77] derive
159   an empirical scaling law specifically for GRPO, identifying three training phases (slow start, rapid
160   improvement, plateau) and showing that most benefit is exhausted by roughly 80% of one epoch. Liu
161   et al. [62] find that RL fine-tuning updates only 5–30% of model parameters across seven algorithms
162   and ten model families, suggesting RL activates a sparse “RL subnetwork.” MiniCPM [37] and the
163   SLM survey of Subramanian et al. [112] characterise scaling in the sub-8B regime that forms the bulk
164   of our frontier. Schaeffer et al. [99] caution that apparent emergent abilities may be metric artefacts,
165   a concern that persists into RL. Our measurements extend these works by providing cross-family
166   scaling data for RL from 0.6B to 235B across five families and four frameworks, supplying empirical
167   ground truth against which future RL scaling laws can be calibrated.


168   2.5   Frameworks, Benchmarks, and Reproducibility

169   The proliferation of algorithms has been matched by a proliferation of training frameworks:
170   TRL [118], OpenRLHF [36], veRL [104], NeMo-RL [78], DeepSpeed-Chat [133], trlX [26], Ax-
171   olotl [79], and T INKER [114]. Each framework makes different default choices about advantage
172   normalisation, KL estimators, tokenisation loss masking, rollout batching, and importance-sampling
173   granularity; those differences are the principal source of the cross-library gap we quantify. On the
174   inference side, vLLM [48] and SGLang [145] provide the rollout backends on which all modern
175   frameworks depend; FlashAttention [17] and LoRA [35] are load-bearing kernels.
176   In the RL benchmarking tradition, Henderson et al. [27] first exposed the fragility of deep-RL results
177   to implementation choices and seeds; rliable [2] introduced interquartile-mean reporting and
178   performance profiles; BenchMARL [9] unified multi-agent RL benchmarks; Patterson et al. [88]
179   surveyed empirical design practices; Jordan et al. [42] argued that most published RL comparisons
180   lack statistical power; and Madaan et al. [69] quantified LLM evaluation variance along seed,
181   monotonicity, and scaling axes. Pineau et al. [90] formalised NeurIPS reproducibility criteria and
182   ACM’s Artifact Review and Badging [1] established community standards for available, evaluated,
183   and reproduced artifacts. Reproducibility studies in adjacent fields include [11, 68, 127, 86, 39, 63, 50,
184   98, 16], alongside Hoffman et al.’s Acme [32]. Classical evaluations rest on GSM8K [15], MATH [29],
185   AIME [70], MMLU [28], HumanEval [12], MBPP [5], and BBH [113]. RL-F INETUNING B ENCH
186   ports these statistical traditions to the LLM RL post-training regime, contributing the first cross-library
187   measurement protocol for GRPO-family algorithms, a ZVF diagnostic that scales from 0.6B to 235B
188   parameters, and an empirical grounding for the scaling laws, frameworks, and algorithmic refinements
189   surveyed above.

250    5                            Reproducibility
251   We release pinned containers, deterministic seed management, and rliable-based analysis scripts.
252   Logged artifacts are released at three explicitly named levels of granularity: (i) precomputed
253   ZVF/GU traces for every reported run, including all Tinker rows; (ii) step-level aggregate logs
254   (per-step mean reward, loss, gradient norm, and—where the backend exposes it—KL) for every
255   reported run; and (iii) raw per-group rollout JSONL for the Tier-A open-stack rows in Appendix B
256   and a subset of Tinker GSM8K rows. Raw JSONL is not bundled for every Tinker case-study or
257   tool-use run; those cases are flagged in the manifest.

258    5.1                               One-command reproducibility

259   The artifact ships a top-level Makefile with three documented targets that govern the open-stack
260   reproducibility surface:


                                                                                               7

[PAGE 8]
261          • make smoke-test — ∼5 min on a single commodity GPU (T4 or L4 class, 16 GB). Verifies
262            that the artifact installs, the pinned containers launch, and the GSM8K reward harness emits
263            a non-trivial reward on a 16-prompt sanity batch. No model weights are updated.
264          • make reproduce-main — end-to-end re-run of every Tier-A open-stack cell in the mani-
265            fest (Modal H100 CleanRL/SB3/Tianshou/Qwen2.5-0.5B classic-RL arms; the 5-seed held-
266            out Qwen3-8B GSM8K control). Estimated wall-clock: ∼96 GPU-hours on H100 SXM5
267            (80 GB), or ∼3 hours per seed for the 0.5B reference. Produces the same step-level ZVF/GU
268            traces and the held-out evaluation numbers cited in Table 1.
269          • make reproduce-appendix — regenerates the full results table (Appendix A) figures
270            from the precomputed traces shipped with the artifact, without re-running any backend.
271            Useful for reviewers who want to verify that our plotting code agrees with the bundled trace
272            files.

273   What is and is not in the make target. make reproduce-main only re-runs A-grade open-stack
274   cells; B-grade Tinker case-study runs are not re-run because the Tinker backend is closed-source
275   and rate-limited (Section 6). For B-grade cells we ship the JSON step logs and the API-call scripts;
276   replication requires Tinker credentials and incurs the JWT-expiry failure modes documented in the
277   manifest (Appendix B).

278   Hash manifest. On a successful make reproduce-main run, the artifact writes a
279   REPRODUCIBILITY_HASHES.json file containing SHA-256 digests of every emitted ZVF trace,
280   reward CSV, and held-out evaluation summary. Reviewers can diff this against the reference man-
281   ifest released with the artifact to confirm bit-level (where deterministic) or distributional (where
282   sampling-based) agreement.

283   Submission-time status. At submission, the Makefile targets are wired to the already-released
284   Modal scripts and the held-out evaluation harness; if a target is missing or stub-only at the time
285   the camera-ready artifact tag is cut, the corresponding entry in the artifact card (Table 2) will be
286   downgraded from ✓ to “pending” and the README will list the substitute manual command.


287   6   Limitations

288   The current release has documented gaps; full discussion (dual-use, compute accounting, carbon
289   estimate, data provenance, closed-backend acknowledgement, and known methodological limits) is in
290   Appendix F. In summary: (i) all B-grade rows are single-seed and depend on a closed-source Tinker
291   backend; we report them descriptively. (ii) Most A-grade evidence is concentrated on short-horizon
292   GSM8K with binary verifiable rewards; tool-use, code-generation, and non-math coverage are sparser.
293   (iii) Inferential claims are limited to the mechanical ZVF = pG + (1−p)G identity and the held-out 5-
294   seed Qwen3-8B GSM8K control (+1.3pp, t=1.32, p=0.26, not significant). (iv) Many cross-library
295   comparisons are confounded by checkpoint, hardware, or default-config differences and should be
296   read as stack-level rather than algorithm-level statements.


297   7   Conclusion

298   RL-F INETUNING B ENCH is a shared empirical substrate for RL post-training across libraries,
299   algorithms, and model families. The current release supports three conservative claims: (i) ZVF/GU
300   are useful descriptive diagnostics of when binary-reward GRPO stops producing within-group
301   signal, but should not be read as causal predictors independent of reward mean and group size; (ii)
302   initialization and rollout regime are plausible stack-level moderators in the released case studies,
303   not isolated causal effects; and (iii) cross-stack PPO/GRPO rankings are descriptive because policy
304   architecture and task encoding are not fully matched. The held-out Qwen3-8B GSM8K control gives
305   a small, non-significant GRPO gain over the base (p=0.26). The released traces and checkpoints are
306   intended to make stricter follow-up easy.

803   zero gradient signal to the GRPO update.

804   C.2   Why ZVF Is Not a Tautological Re-expression of Mean Reward

805   A reviewer may reasonably suspect that under binary outcome rewards r ∈ {0, 1} and group-relative
806   advantages, ZVFt is a direct function of the batch mean reward r̄t : if every group succeeds (or fails)
807   uniformly, ZVFt = 1. This is true at the extremes r̄t ∈ {0, 1} but does not hold at intermediate
808   values. For K i.i.d. Bernoulli rollouts with prompt-specific success probability pg , the expected ZVF
809   under a null independence model is
                                      E[ZVFt ] = Epg pK                 K
                                                                          
                                                          g + (1 − pg )      .                            (2)
810   The right-hand side depends on the full prompt-difficulty distribution {pg }, not only on its first
811   moment. Two populations can therefore share the same mean reward r̄ = 0.5 yet have very different
812   ZVF: a bimodal population with pg ∈ {0, 1} yields ZVF = 1, while a uniform-hard population with
813   all pg = 0.5 and K = 8 yields ZVF ≈ 2 · (0.5)8 ≈ 0.008. The substantive claim is thus not “high
814   mean reward implies high ZVF,” but rather that ZVF is sensitive to how success mass is distributed
815   across prompts.

816   C.3   What the Released Artifact Establishes

817   The released artifact bundled with this paper contains aggregate reward trajectories, held-out check-
818   point summaries, and the cross-framework parser specification released with the anonymous artifact.
819   It does not contain the per-group step-level rollout logs needed to support a new partial-correlation
820   table that controls simultaneously for batch-mean reward, entropy, KL drift, and advantage variance.
821   We therefore do not claim a fresh incremental-R2 estimate here, and we mark any implication that
822   the current artifact proves a new partial-correlation result beyond the already released summary traces
823   as rerun required.
824   The narrower empirical claim made in the revised paper is the one directly supported by the released
825   evidence: in outcome-reward GRPO, high ZVF coincides with the disappearance of within-group
826   contrast, and at any step where ZVF = 1 the group-relative advantage is mechanically zero so no
827   gradient flows from that batch. We additionally observe co-occurrence between elevated ZVF and
828   reward plateaus or regressions in some runs, but we do not establish a causal link: the within-task
829   stratified analysis in Section 4.2 does not support a within-task predictive relationship between mean
830   ZVF and last-10 reward. The operational meaning of ZVF in the present benchmark is therefore the
831   local mechanical claim, not a performance-causal one.

847   D     Cross-Framework ZVF Computation Pipeline
848   D.1   Canonical Definition and Pseudocode

849   Given per-step, per-group, per-rollout outcome rewards rg,k ∈ R for g = 1, . . . , |Bt | and k =
850   1, . . . , K, ZVF is computed as in Eq. (1). The reference implementation is:
851   def zvf(rewards_2d: np.ndarray, eps: float = 1e-6) -> float:
852       # rewards_2d shape: [num_groups, K]
853       var_per_group = rewards_2d.var(axis=-1, ddof=1)
854       return float((var_per_group <= eps).mean())

855   D.2   Per-Framework Reward-Field Locations

856   Each framework emits rollouts in a different layout.    We consolidate them via
857   scripts/zvf_compute_cross_framework.py which normalizes every source into a [|Bt |, K]
858   reward matrix. Field mappings are in Table 9.

859   D.3   Variance Threshold ε

860   We fix ε = 10−6 for rewards normalized to [0, 1]. This tolerates fp32 round-off while treat-
861   ing any distinguishable reward difference as “nonzero variance”. A sensitivity sweep over
862   ε ∈ {10−8 , 10−6 , 10−4 , 10−2 } shifts cross-framework ZVF estimates by at most 0.4% absolute
863   on our logs, well below between-run variability. Rewards not in [0, 1] are scaled to that range before
864   thresholding.

902   E     Reconciling the Group-Size Result: Token-Budget-Normalized Sweeps
903   E.1   The Apparent Contradiction

904   A reviewer correctly identified that Table 6 reports G=4 as the highest last-10 reward (52.1%) under
905   a fixed-step budget, while the main text claims that G≈32 “maximizes gradient utilization”. These
906   observations are compatible but require the axis of comparison to be made explicit. Under a fixed
907   number of optimizer steps, larger G costs proportionally more tokens per step, so a step-budget
908   comparison benefits small G; under a fixed total token budget, larger G amortizes wasted (all-correct
909   or all-incorrect) rollouts and yields higher effective gradient signal per token.

933            Under a fixed step budget at small total token counts, G=4 attains the highest

946   E.5   Measured Small-Scale Confirmation of the Inverted-U

947   Because the token-normalized table above is an illustrative reanalysis, we separately
948   ran a measured group-size sweep to confirm the qualitative inverted-U with fresh per-
949   seed data (Table 11; driver experiments/modal/modal_groupsize_zvf_sweep.py, artifact
950   experiments/results/groupsize_zvf_sweep.tsv). On the ungated Qwen2.5-0.5B with a
951   verifiable correctness reward on two-operand addition, G ∈ {2, 4, 8, 16} at three seeds each, held-
952   out accuracy traces an inverted-U shape with its numerical maximum at the intermediate G=8
953   (0.982 → 0.988 → 0.990 → 0.978); we emphasize the apex is not statistically separable from its

968   F.1   Dual-Use Analysis: Misuse Risks of Reasoning RL

969   Threat model. RL-F INETUNING B ENCH releases (i) training scripts that apply GRPO to the
970   GSM8K [15] mathematical reasoning benchmark and to the Salesforce xLAM-Function-Calling-
971   60k [57] tool-use corpus, (ii) a set of LoRA adapters published on the HuggingFace Hub
972   (anonymous/tinker-rl-bench-*), and (iii) diagnostic code for Zero-Variance Fraction, reward-
973   stability, and length-bias analysis. We analyse four concrete misuse pathways these artefacts could,
974   in principle, enable, and we describe the existing guardrails and the residual risk we judge acceptable
975   for publication.


                                                        23

[PAGE 24]
976   M1. Weaponisation of mathematical reasoning. The most capability-relevant artefact we release
 977   is GRPO training code that improves grade-school arithmetic and multi-step reasoning. A malicious
 978   operator could attempt to extend this pipeline to tasks of greater dual-use concern, e.g., chemistry
 979   olympiad problems, cryptographic key recovery, or financial fraud. We judge the marginal uplift
 980   provided by our release to be small for three reasons. First, GRPO at our training scale does not
 981   create new capabilities; the base-model control for GSM8K (Section 4, 82.0%) shows that the bulk
 982   of held-out accuracy is attributable to Qwen3-8B’s pretraining. Our full GRPO run only adds +1.3
 983   percentage points (p=0.26, not statistically significant). Second, the public literature already contains
 984   GRPO implementations [102, 83, 103, 117] that are at least as capable. Third, any reasoning uplift is
 985   bounded by the rewarder: GSM8K rewards use exact-match against a human-written gold answer,
 986   which does not generalise to the domains listed above without a new reward signal, which itself
 987   requires expertise and labelled data.

 988   M2. Reward hacking and the long-term alignment risk. GRPO, like all policy-gradient RL
 989   from a proxy reward, is vulnerable to reward hacking: the model learns to exploit idiosyncrasies
 990   of the reward function rather than solve the intended task [108, 47]. We observed verbosity drift in
 991   some of our own GRPO runs, which is a concrete demonstration of this failure mode in our own data.
 992   Users who apply our scripts to under-specified reward functions in production settings (e.g., a custom
 993   “helpfulness” classifier) may observe models that appear to improve on held-out metrics while silently
 994   optimising against unintended proxies. We include the Zero-Variance Fraction and reward-stability
 995   diagnostics precisely so that downstream users can detect such drift.

 996   M3. Circumventing safety training via distillation or tool-use. The tool-use track trains LoRA
 997   adapters that teach a base model to emit schema-valid JSON function calls [57]. A motivated
 998   adversary could in principle use the same pipeline to teach a base model to emit jailbreak prompts as
 999   “tool calls” or to invoke an adversarial external tool. We mitigate this by (a) releasing only adapters,
1000   not merged weights, so that the safety-trained instruct checkpoint must be applied separately and any
1001   instruct-layer guardrails remain active; (b) documenting in model cards that the tool-use adapters are
1002   trained on a narrow schema (five synthetic tools) and have not been safety-evaluated for open-ended
1003   function calling; and (c) not releasing any function-calling harness that would actually execute emitted
1004   calls.

1005   M4. Compute-efficiency externalities. Our work argues that critic-free RL with budget-dependent
1006   group-size selection (the optimal G depends on total token budget; see Appendix E) can be run
1007   on modest budgets. Improved training efficiency is itself dual-use: it lowers the cost of running
1008   adversarial fine-tuning at scale. We judge this externality to be modest relative to the already-low
1009   cost of fine-tuning a 0.6B–8B model with commodity LoRA recipes [35], but we flag it explicitly.

1010   Residual risk. After weighing M1–M4 we judge that the reproducibility, calibration, and peda-
1011   gogical benefits of releasing RL-F INETUNING B ENCH outweigh the residual misuse risk. We adopt
1012   the standard mitigation of releasing adapters (not merged weights), documenting intended use and
1013   out-of-scope use in model cards, and publishing alongside the diagnostics a researcher would need to
1014   detect reward hacking.

1050   GPU-hour estimates. Tinker GPU-hour counts are not disclosed by the platform. We estimate
1051   them from wall-clock run duration and assume a single H100-class accelerator per job, which is
1052   consistent with Tinker’s published architecture for the model sizes we trained. Modal GPU-hours are
1053   measured directly from Modal’s billing dashboard.

1054   Interpretation and uncertainty. Our best estimate of ~296 kg CO2 -eq for the entire project is
1055   comparable to a single round-trip domestic economy flight in the same region (~300 kg). The
1056   dominant uncertainty is the Tinker GPU-hour count: because Tinker does not expose hardware
1057   telemetry, a factor-of-two error in GPU-hours or TDP is plausible. Under a pessimistic assumption
1058   (2× GPU-hours, H100 running near 100% TDP continuously), the Tinker contribution could be
1059   as high as ~540 kg CO2 -eq. Under an optimistic assumption (Tinker backends actually use more
1060   energy-efficient accelerators than H100, 50% average utilisation) the contribution could be as low
1061   as ~130 kg. We report the central estimate in the main table and caution readers that it should
1062   not be interpreted as precise. All numbers should be treated as order-of-magnitude estimates with
1063   documented sensitivity to Tinker hardware assumptions; subsequent users can additionally skip the
1064   failed runs and the ablation sweeps to lower the cost of reproducing our work.

1065   Offset and mitigation. We did not purchase carbon offsets. Instead, we have (a) released all trained
1066   checkpoints on HuggingFace Hub so that downstream users do not need to re-run our experiments;
1067   (b) released step-level CSV logs so that learning curves can be inspected without re-training; and (c)
1068   documented in Section F.2 which runs were wasteful, so that replicators can skip them. We regard
1069   reproducibility-by-artefact as a more durable mitigation than offsets.

1110   F.5   Closed-Source Tinker Acknowledgment

1111   A central tension in RL-F INETUNING B ENCH is that a substantial fraction of our headline numbers
1112   come from a closed-source commercial platform while our paper simultaneously advocates for
1113   reproducibility and platform independence. We do not resolve this tension by hiding it; we describe it
1114   precisely.

1115   What Tinker is and is not. Tinker [114] is a managed LLM fine-tuning and inference service
1116   provided by Thinking Machines, Inc. It exposes a Python SDK (tinker==0.16.1) that accepts
1117   custom loss functions (forward_backward_custom), a limited set of optimisers, and standard LoRA
1118   hyperparameters. It does not expose (i) the exact server-side GRPO loss implementation, (ii) reward
1119   normalisation or baseline subtraction scheme, (iii) minibatch construction or gradient-accumulation
1120   strategy, (iv) hardware configuration (GPU type, inter-node bandwidth), or (v) system-level telemetry
1121   (energy, throughput, queueing).


                                                         26

[PAGE 27]
1122   What this means for our claims. Tinker results in this paper measure the platform’s implementa-
1123   tion of GRPO, not an abstract specification of the algorithm. Cross-stack reward gaps between Tinker
1124   GRPO and an open-source GRPO implementation cannot be fully attributed from our data: we are
1125   able to rule out a handful of candidate explanations (seed variance, model-size confound) but not
1126   the implementation itself. We therefore draw quantitative conclusions only from the open-source
1127   side (TRL, veRL on Modal H100, OpenRLHF) where every hyperparameter is auditable, and use
1128   Tinker results primarily as descriptive case studies of what a carefully engineered production stack
1129   can achieve for critic-free RL at our scales.

1150   F.6   Known Methodological Limits

1151   In addition to the infrastructure failures and platform-specific caveats enumerated in Section 6, we
1152   flag the following methodological limits.

1153   Short training horizons. All Tinker GRPO runs were capped at 30–50 gradient steps. This is a
1154   deliberate cost-control choice and means our Tinker numbers are early-training snapshots. We cannot
1155   rule out that longer training would change the qualitative picture (e.g., the 82%→83.3% held-out
1156   gain might widen, or might collapse to reward hacking).

1157   Single-seed Tinker experiments. Each Tinker configuration was run once, not 3–5 times as
1158   best practice prescribes [27]. Tinker results therefore lack variance estimates and do not support
1159   significance testing; we report them descriptively. Only the 5-seed TRL baseline and the 5-seed
1160   held-out GSM8K evaluation carry proper confidence intervals.

1161   Train-set reward as primary Tinker metric. Tinker runs primarily report reward on training
1162   prompts. Only the GSM8K track was followed up with a separate 200-example held-out evaluation
1163   per seed. Other Tinker tracks (tool-use, xLAM) remain train-set-only and we cannot distinguish
1164   memorisation from generalisation for those settings.

1165   LoRA only; no full fine-tuning. All experiments use LoRA [35] with ranks 8–64. We have
1166   not tested whether full fine-tuning would re-order the library/algorithm comparisons. The LoRA
1167   constraint is practical (cost) but it means our claims about PPO vs. GRPO and TRL vs. Tinker are
1168   LoRA-conditional.


                                                        27

[PAGE 28]
1169   Benchmark coverage is narrow. We evaluate four domains: GSM8K, MATH-500 (exploratory),
1170   HumanEval (subset), and synthetic/xLAM tool-use. We do not evaluate on MT-Bench, ArenaHard,
1171   safety benchmarks (HarmBench, ToxicChat), or truthfulness benchmarks (TruthfulQA). Any claim
1172   about “reasoning” in the paper is strictly a claim about the covered benchmarks.

1173   No human preference data. We use verifiable rewards only (exact-match for math, unit-test for
1174   code, schema-validity for tool-use). We do not study RLHF with human preference data; findings
1175   should not be extrapolated to reward-model–based alignment without further work.

1176   Closed-source implementation opacity (reiterated). Comparisons between Tinker GRPO and
1177   TRL GRPO are confounded by the closed-source nature of Tinker’s implementation. See Section F.5
1178   for detail.

1179   Cross-platform hardware confounding. Tinker, Modal, Colab, and the Institutional A100 node
1180   use different accelerators, different memory hierarchies, and different inference/training stacks
1181   (Tinker proprietary, Modal vLLM, Colab PyTorch-native, TRL+Accelerate). Observed differences in
1182   reward dynamics are not purely algorithmic.

1183   Carbon estimates are approximate. As discussed in Section F.3, Tinker GPU-hour and TDP are
1184   inferred, not measured. Grid intensity is region-average, not marginal. Numbers in Table 13 should
1185   be treated as order-of-magnitude.

1186   Exploratory, not confirmatory. This is an exploratory case study, not a pre-registered confirmatory
1187   experiment. We did not pre-register primary hypotheses. All p-values are un-corrected for the number
1188   of comparisons actually made across the paper; the Bonferroni-surviving comparisons in Section 4
1189   are so flagged, but most of our secondary analyses are descriptive.

1190   Geographic and demographic limits. The specific city and country of the authors’ institution are
1191   withheld here for anonymity but disclosed in the non-anonymous version of the paper. Our choice of
1192   benchmarks, prompt phrasings, and evaluation design reflects that single-region context. Independent
1193   replication by groups in other regions, on non-English tasks, and with non-Qwen / non-Llama families
1194   is an open question.
