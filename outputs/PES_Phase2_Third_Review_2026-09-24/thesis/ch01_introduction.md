# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Background: critic-free RL post-training and the group-relative estimator

Post-training is now the stage at which a base language model is turned into something that reliably follows instructions and solves problems. For several years the dominant recipe was reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), in which a learned reward model is trained on human preference comparisons and then used as the reward signal for a policy-optimisation loop. RLHF works, but it introduces a second learned model whose errors the policy can exploit, and it makes reward construction expensive and hard to audit. The alternative that has taken hold more recently is reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR): instead of a learned reward model, the reward comes from a programmatic checker — exact-match against a reference answer for grade-school mathematics, unit tests for code, a formal or semi-formal validator for structured output. The checker is deterministic, transparent, and free to run. What it gives up in generality it gains in measurability: every reward is a fact about a generated string rather than a prediction from a model (source: platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex).

The workhorse optimiser for both settings has been proximal policy optimisation (PPO), which optimises a clipped surrogate objective against a value function critic. The critic is a second network, usually of comparable size to the policy, trained to predict the expected return of the current state; its predictions supply the baseline that the advantage estimator subtracts. This works, but it is expensive and fragile. The critic consumes memory that could hold policy parameters, it has its own learning rate and its own failure modes, and its errors propagate directly into the policy gradient as biased advantages.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) removes the critic entirely. Rather than learn a value function, GRPO samples a group of $G$ completions for the same prompt, scores all of them with the verifier, and derives the advantage of each completion from the group's own reward statistics. Concretely, for a group with rewards $r_1, \dots, r_G$, the advantage assigned to completion $i$ is a within-group standardisation,

$$A_i = \frac{r_i - \mu_g}{\sigma_g + \varepsilon}, \qquad \mu_g = \frac{1}{G}\sum_j r_j, \qquad \sigma_g^2 = \frac{1}{G}\sum_j (r_j - \mu_g)^2,$$

which is then used in a clipped, KL-regularised policy-gradient update. The empirical baseline is the group mean; there is no value network to train, no value loss to weight, and no critic to keep in sync with the policy. The trade is a much simpler optimiser for a much smaller effective batch, since $G$ samples of the same prompt now carry one learning signal rather than $G$ independent ones.

That trade has a sharp consequence, and it is the observation this project is organised around. Because the advantage is a *within-group* standardisation, its numerator is a deviation from the group mean and its denominator is the group's own spread. When every completion in a group receives the same verifier outcome — all correct, or all incorrect — the group contributes nothing: $\sigma_g = 0$, every numerator is zero, and every advantage $A_i$ is exactly zero. The group has been fully sampled and fully scored, at full token cost, and the reward-relative part of the update is identically zero. The KL penalty toward the reference model still acts, so the policy does drift slightly toward the reference; but no *reward-driven* learning occurs on that group. We call the fraction of a training batch that falls into this state the **Zero-Variance Fraction (ZVF)**, and it is the single measurement that most of this report's diagnostics are built around (source: platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex).

ZVF is not a rare edge case. In the regime this project studies it is the *typical* state of a batch: the Phase-1 report records a ZVF of approximately 0.72–0.77 on its main configuration, meaning that roughly three quarters of a rollout batch contributes no reward-relative gradient at all (source: platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex). The fraction is not fixed, however, and it responds to the one knob that most directly controls it: ZVF falls from 0.845 at $G = 2$ to 0.631 at $G = 16$, asymptoting at 0.631 on the measured sweep, a decline of 0.214 across that range (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/frontier_synthesis_zvf.tex; source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/zvf_iter122.tex). Larger groups buy more contrast, with diminishing returns that saturate well before the group is small enough to be cheap.

There is a second and less comfortable observation in the background, and it is about attribution rather than mechanism. "GRPO" is not a single algorithm. It is a label that covers implementations in Tinker, TRL, SkyRL, verl, OpenRLHF and a growing number of research forks, each of which chooses its own answer parser, sampling temperature, top-$p$, KL coefficient, clip range, LoRA target set, precision and evaluation harness. Two runs that both say "GRPO" in their titles may differ in a dozen ways that plausibly move the outcome, and the reported headline number does not distinguish between them. The project's own stack-sensitivity probe makes this concrete: a nominally matched configuration (Qwen3-8B, seed 42, $G = 8$, learning rate $1\times10^{-5}$, GSM8K-500, 30 steps, visible hyperparameters held equal) was run across four framework paths with results recorded in `experiments/results/framework_comparison.json` (source: platform_hybrid/paper/main_eai_body.tex). A variance decomposition over 42 experiments attributed $\eta^2 = 0.546$ of the variation to library or framework, $\eta^2 = 0.558$ to training algorithm, $\eta^2 = 0.471$ to model family and $\eta^2 = 0.322$ to model scale — with the important caveat, stated by the source itself, that these factors are confounded with one another and with back-end, reward implementation, hardware and task (source: platform_hybrid/paper/main_eai_body.tex). The framework term is not a nuisance parameter to be wished away. It is of the same order as the algorithm term.

## 1.2 A Motivating Example

Consider a single training step with a group size of $G = 8$ on an easy GSM8K prompt that the current policy already solves. All eight sampled completions produce the correct final answer, so each receives reward $r_i = 1$. The group mean is $\mu_g = 1$ and the group standard deviation is $\sigma_g = 0$, so every advantage $A_i = 0$. The compute spent on that group is real — eight full forward-decode passes at whatever temperature the sampler is configured with — and the group supplies no task-learning signal. The KL-regularisation term still nudges the policy toward the reference, but the point stands: no reward-driven learning happens on this group. Now imagine a batch of 16 such prompts in which 12 are already solved and 4 are not: $12/16 = 0.75$ of the batch is "dark" compute (source: platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex). What the Semester 4 work adds to this picture is that 0.75 is not a worst case. It is the neighbourhood of the measured typical regime, ZVF $\approx 0.72$–0.77, and it is therefore the baseline against which any claim that GRPO is learning efficiently has to be read.

Now suppose, having noticed this, that one wants to answer the obvious follow-up question: is the group-relative estimator actually a better choice than a learned critic? The natural experiment is a matched comparison — same model, same training prompts, same verifier, same step budget, same evaluation, with PPO and GRPO as the only difference. The project ran that contrast on Qwen3-8B and recorded the result in its own test family. The Welch $t$-test on PPO versus GRPO gives $p = 0.7605$, the least significant entry in the report's twenty-test family after Benjamini–Hochberg correction and the only one that does not survive it (source: platform_hybrid/paper/main_eai_body.tex; source: REPRODUCE.md). The accompanying effect-size table carries Cohen's $d = -0.14$ with a 95% confidence interval of $[-1.02, 0.74]$, observed power $0.06$ and a minimum detectable effect of $1.25$ in the units of the outcome (source: platform_hybrid/paper/main_eai_body.tex). The unit behind those statistics must be read carefully: the $n = 10$ is the final ten steps of a single run per arm, not ten independent runs, and those steps are autocorrelated by construction. The project's own methods note is explicit that single-seed Tinker experiments have "zero statistical power", that "no inferential test can be computed and no confidence intervals can be formed" on them, and that such results are "treated as descriptive only and are not subject to significance testing" (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/_shared_methods.tex). The interval and power figures are therefore reproduced here because they appear in the source's table, but they cannot bear the weight a confidence interval normally carries, and nothing in this section rests on them. Read carefully, that is a failure to detect a difference at this sample size, not a demonstration that the two estimators are equivalent; the confidence interval is wide enough to contain effects in both directions, and the study was in no position to rule out anything but a very large one.

Two further observations from the same study make the example sharper rather than cleaner. First, the same PPO-versus-GRPO contrast on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct produces $d = 12.75$ with a 95% confidence interval of $[8.49, 17.00]$ and $p < 0.001$ (source: platform_hybrid/paper/main_eai_body.tex). The apparent answer is not merely weak on Qwen; it reverses across model families. Second, the Qwen arms were not executed on a common back-end — the GRPO arm ran through Tinker-managed infrastructure and the PPO arm on a Modal H100 cluster — and the source states this explicitly and declines to draw the algorithmic conclusion it invites: "We therefore treat the PPO/GRPO rows as stack-conditioned evidence, not as an algorithm diagnostic baseline", and "We therefore withhold a directional Qwen algorithm claim" (source: platform_hybrid/paper/main_eai_body.tex). The same section records an aggregation gap that illustrates why such care is warranted: the Qwen PPO last-10 GSM8K figure is 22.5% in the primary results ledger but 35.0% in the statistical summary, and the source names the ledger value as primary and treats the summary value as evidence of an aggregation defect rather than as a second measurement (source: platform_hybrid/paper/main_eai_body.tex).

The genuinely same-stack evidence in the repository points the same way, and it is worth stating plainly because it is a negative. The E1 audit takes four GRPO-family variants (DAPO, GSPO, Dr. GRPO, AERO) plus the GRPO reference, runs five arms across eight paired seeds for 40 units at a frozen stack fingerprint, and evaluates on 500 held-out GSM8K examples per unit. Every one of the four contrasts is inconclusive: DAPO $+0.00100$, 95% CI $[-0.00450, +0.00675]$, $p = 0.756$; GSPO $+0.00500$, CI $[-0.00125, +0.01200]$, $p = 0.210$; Dr. GRPO $-0.00200$, CI $[-0.00950, +0.00725]$, $p = 0.673$; AERO $-0.00075$, CI $[-0.00825, +0.00675]$, $p = 0.858$. The pre-audit aggregator's verdict that the differences "disappear" is itself declared invalid, because the locked exact noncentral paired-$t$ minimum detectable effect of $0.010115893$ exceeds the $0.010$ margin the study was designed around (source: autoresearch/deli-neurips-tmlr-260802/audits/statistical_refutation.md). The audit's permitted wording is deliberately narrow: across eight paired seeds no arm produced a BH-significant held-out difference from GRPO, and none met the locked exact-power equivalence rule — which is not the same as saying the methods are the same, nor that they fail to improve.

Taken together, these two halves of the example are the whole motivation for what follows. The mechanism half shows that a large fraction of the compute in a GRPO run is not doing what a casual reading of the loss curve suggests it is doing. The attribution half shows that the most natural comparative question one would ask about that mechanism — is this estimator better than the alternative? — cannot be answered from the label "GRPO" and "PPO" alone, and that the honest answer on the project's own evidence is a non-significant, underpowered, back-end-confounded contrast rather than a ranking. Neither observation is a defect of the GRPO estimator; both are defects of what gets measured and what gets reported.

## 1.3 Problem Statement

The project is organised around four problems, in decreasing order of how often the literature acknowledges them.

**Attribution is missing.** Comparative claims about RL post-training are routinely made across frameworks that differ in parser, sampler, KL coefficient, clip range, LoRA targets, precision and evaluator. A reported gain from "GRPO" over "PPO" is, on most published evidence, a gain from one complete stack over another complete stack, with no way to say which component produced it. The project's own variance decomposition puts the framework term at a magnitude comparable to the algorithm term (source: platform_hybrid/paper/main_eai_body.tex), and its matched Qwen contrast returns $p = 0.7605$ (source: platform_hybrid/paper/main_eai_body.tex). The problem is not that these numbers are surprising; it is that no paper in this area is obliged to report the information that would let a reader separate them.

**Signal starvation is invisible.** Standard training logs report reward, loss, gradient norm, entropy and completion length. None of these surfaces the fraction of a batch whose group advantage is exactly zero. A run can show a rising reward curve and a falling ZVF, or a rising reward curve with a ZVF pinned near 0.8, and the two look identical in a loss plot. The fraction of sampling budget buying no reward-relative gradient is, in the project's own measurements, the largest measured source of zero-advantage sampling on this fixed setup (source: platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex), and it is not part of any standard telemetry.

**There is no reporting or provenance standard.** Even where authors are willing to describe their stack, there is no machine-readable form in which to do it, no validator that checks whether a claim's recorded configuration is internally consistent, and no accepted minimum set of fields that a stack-conditioned result must carry. Consequently, results cannot be pooled, cannot be compared across papers, and cannot be audited after the fact.

**Evaluation results are rarely reported with their terminal states.** Benchmark suites in the agentic and reasoning evaluation space are frequently multi-scope (a public split versus a private original contract, a benign-utility scope versus an adversarial scope), quota-limited, or gated behind private payloads. Reports tend either to omit such suites or to collapse them into a single number, which conceals how much of the suite was actually graded. The project treats this as a first-class concern rather than a presentational one: a result that is genuinely partial should be recorded as partial, with its coverage denominator and its reopen condition, and a lane that cannot be run at all should be recorded as externally blocked rather than quietly dropped.

## 1.4 Objectives

The project pursues eight objectives. They are stated as they were pursued, not retrofitted to the results.

**O1 — Build a single harness that runs the same experiment across multiple back-ends.** Provide one control surface over Tinker, TRL, SkyRL, verl and OpenRLHF paths so that a configuration can be held fixed while the framework varies, and so that a framework difference is measurable rather than anecdotal.

**O2 — Instrument the training loop so that signal starvation is directly observable.** Emit per-step telemetry carrying ZVF, gradient utilisation, policy entropy, completion length and KL divergence, and persist the raw per-group reward tensors so that any derived statistic can be deterministically recomputed after the fact rather than trusted from a log line.

**O3 — Measure the zero-variance regime on real reward tensors and test the levers that might move it.** Characterise ZVF empirically on genuine GRPO reward tensors, and investigate re-baselining and curriculum interventions as ways of converting dark compute into contrast.

**O4 — Establish and hold a scientific protocol.** Multi-seed runs, matched baselines, held-out evaluation with explicit denominators, deterministic recomputation, pre-registered effect-size and power accounting, and honest reporting of negatives and nulls — including the cases where the answer is that the study was underpowered to answer.

**O5 — Scope the most original direction in the field.** A literature scan for white space, which in this project identified stack provenance and reporting standards as the unclaimed ground and produced the minimum reporting standard (P5) and the machine-readable stack registry (P6).

**O6 — Turn the diagnostic into an intervention.** Derive an adaptive group-size controller from the ZVF measurement and evaluate it as a training-time policy rather than a post-hoc observation (P7).

**O7 — Evaluate a trained actor against held-out suites under strict governance.** Run a single trained checkpoint against fourteen held-out agentic and reasoning suites, each with its own native evaluator at a pinned revision, under receipt, budget and decontamination controls, and record every lane's terminal state.

**O8 — Report under a deliberate asymmetry.** Where the evidence supports a structural or descriptive claim, state it; where it does not, withhold it. No cross-suite aggregate, no pooling of original-contract and replacement-scope numbers, and no claim of improvement over any baseline anywhere in the work.

## 1.5 Scope and Non-Goals

The scope of this report is measurement and diagnostics, not leaderboard performance. All training runs are on small-to-mid open models — the Qwen2.5, Qwen3, Qwen3.5 and Qwen3.6 families, from roughly 0.5B parameters up to the 35B sparse mixture-of-experts configuration used for the campaign actor. Verifiable rewards come from mathematics and code checkers, principally GSM8K exact-match and HumanEval-style unit tests. Held-out evaluation uses suites' own native evaluators at pinned revisions.

Several things are explicitly out of scope. This is not an attempt to set a state-of-the-art result on any benchmark, and no such claim is made anywhere in the report. It is not a study of frontier reasoning models. The held-out sets used in the controlled comparisons are intentionally small — 200 to 500 prompts in most cases — and every denominator is reported so that a reader can see how little is being claimed. Claims about novel algorithms are not made: the length-weighting intervention (P4) and the adaptive controller (P7) were designed during the work and are reported as designed and simulated systems with their evaluation limits attached, not as demonstrated improvements.

Three evidentiary rules apply throughout and are worth stating here because they constrain what the later chapters are allowed to say. First, every number in this report is traceable to a repository file that was actually read; where a figure could not be sourced, the finding is described qualitatively and the figure is attributed to its source rather than reconstructed. Second, original-contract and replacement-scope benchmark results are never pooled and never averaged across suites — the two are different measurements of different things under different licences, and treating them as one column would manufacture a number that no suite reported. Third, no improvement over a baseline or over a base model is claimed unless the source states it with a test; where the source reports a difference without a test supporting it, or reports an inconclusive test, that is what is written here.

## 1.7 Organisation of the Report

The report is organised in four parts.

**Part I — Foundations** contains the present chapter, followed by Chapter 2, the literature survey, which situates the work against the RLHF and RLVR literature, the critic-free line of estimators, the group-size and advantage-estimation literature, and the reproducibility and reporting literature on which the provenance argument depends; and Chapter 3, the system requirements specification, which states the functional and non-functional requirements the harness, the telemetry layer and the campaign infrastructure were built to satisfy.

**Part II — Design and Implementation** contains Chapter 4, the proposed methodology, which sets out the measurement design, the statistical protocol, the evidence rules and the study-to-evidence mapping; and Chapter 5, the implementation details, covering the multi-framework harness, the per-step telemetry and raw tensor persistence, the campaign launcher and its receipt and budget governance, and the registry and validator components.

**Part III — Results** contains four chapters. Chapter 6 reports the core GRPO studies — the cross-library and cross-scale scaling work, the ZVF diagnostic, the group-size and contrast-density sweep, and the length-bias and held-out generalisation study, corresponding to P1 through P4. Chapter 7 reports the reporting, registry and controller work, corresponding to P5 through P7, including the minimum reporting standard, the machine-readable stack registry, and the simulated behaviour of the adaptive group-size controller. Chapter 8 reports the applied fraud-detection study, corresponding to the archived P8. Chapter 9 reports the E1–E14 held-out benchmark campaign: the actor, the governance model, the strictly completed scopes, the original-contract scores, the incomplete and blocked lanes, the verification results and the campaign's terminal states.

**Part IV — Synthesis** contains Chapter 10, which draws the cross-study synthesis, states the threats to validity candidly, gives the conclusions the evidence will bear, and sets out future work and the reopen conditions under which the blocked lanes could be completed.

Three appendices follow. Appendix A gives the complete run registry. Appendix B documents reproducibility and provenance, including the inherited-versus-individual boundary and the environment records needed to reproduce the reported results.
