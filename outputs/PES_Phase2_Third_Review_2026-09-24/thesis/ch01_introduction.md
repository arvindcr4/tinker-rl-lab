# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Background: critic-free RL post-training and the group-relative estimator

Post-training is now the stage at which a base language model is turned into something that reliably follows instructions and solves problems. For several years the dominant recipe was reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), in which a learned reward model is trained on human preference comparisons and then used as the reward signal for a policy-optimisation loop. RLHF works, but it introduces a second learned model whose errors the policy can exploit, and it makes reward construction expensive and hard to audit. The alternative that has taken hold more recently is reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR): instead of a learned reward model, the reward comes from a programmatic checker — exact-match against a reference answer for grade-school mathematics, unit tests for code, a formal or semi-formal validator for structured output. The checker is deterministic, transparent, and free to run. What it gives up in generality it gains in measurability: every reward is a fact about a generated string rather than a prediction from a model (source: platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex).

The workhorse optimiser for both settings has been proximal policy optimisation (PPO), which optimises a clipped surrogate objective against a value function critic. The critic is a second network, usually of comparable size to the policy, trained to predict the expected return of the current state; its predictions supply the baseline that the advantage estimator subtracts. This works, but it is expensive and fragile. The critic consumes memory that could hold policy parameters, it has its own learning rate and its own failure modes, and its errors propagate directly into the policy gradient as biased advantages.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) removes the critic entirely. Rather than learn a value function, GRPO samples a group of $G$ completions for the same prompt, scores all of them with the verifier, and derives the advantage of each completion from the group's own reward statistics. Concretely, for a group with rewards $r_1, \dots, r_G$, the advantage assigned to completion $i$ is a within-group standardisation,

$$A_i = \frac{r_i - \mu_g}{\sigma_g + \varepsilon}, \qquad \mu_g = \frac{1}{G}\sum_j r_j, \qquad \sigma_g^2 = \frac{1}{G}\sum_j (r_j - \mu_g)^2,$$

which in the original GRPO formulation is then used in a clipped, KL-regularised policy-gradient update. The runners used in this project are simpler: none has a KL term or a reference policy, and the Tinker runners, including the one that trained the campaign actor, have no ratio or clipping either, so they are REINFORCE with a group-mean baseline (Table 4.2 gives the loss form of every study). The empirical baseline is the group mean; there is no value network to train, no value loss to weight, and no critic to keep in sync with the policy. The trade is a much simpler optimiser for a much smaller effective batch, since $G$ samples of the same prompt now carry one learning signal rather than $G$ independent ones.

That trade has a sharp consequence, and it is the observation this project is organised around. Because the advantage is a *within-group* standardisation, its numerator is a deviation from the group mean and its denominator is the group's own spread. When every completion in a group receives the same verifier outcome — all correct, or all incorrect — the group contributes nothing: $\sigma_g = 0$, every numerator is zero, and every advantage $A_i$ is exactly zero. The group has been fully sampled and fully scored, at full token cost, and the reward-relative part of the update is identically zero. In an objective with a KL penalty, that penalty would still pull the policy toward the reference; in this project's runners there is no such term, so the group contributes exactly zero gradient. We call the fraction of a training batch that falls into this state the **Zero-Variance Fraction (ZVF)**, and it is the single measurement that most of this report's diagnostics are built around (source: platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex).

ZVF is not a rare edge case, but its size depends on the regime. The Phase-1 main configuration (Qwen3.5-4B, GSM8K, 40 steps, $G = 8$) records a ZVF of approximately 0.72–0.77, meaning that roughly three quarters of that rollout batch contributes no reward-relative gradient (source: platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex). Other measured runs span 0.16 (Qwen3-8B on held-out GSM8K at $G = 8$) to 0.84 (Qwen2.5-0.5B arithmetic at $G = 2$), so no single value is typical; the measured values are collected in the canonical-numbers table (Table 1.1). The fraction responds to the one knob that most directly controls it: on the Qwen2.5-0.5B arithmetic sweep, mean ZVF falls from 0.838 at $G = 2$ to 0.631 at $G = 16$, a decline of 0.207 across that range (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/group_size.tex; source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/zvf_iter122.tex). Larger groups buy more contrast, with diminishing returns that saturate well before the group is small enough to be cheap.

There is a second and less comfortable observation in the background, and it is about attribution rather than mechanism. "GRPO" is not a single algorithm. It is a label that covers implementations in Tinker, TRL, SkyRL, verl, OpenRLHF and a growing number of research forks, each of which chooses its own answer parser, sampling temperature, top-$p$, KL coefficient, clip range, LoRA target set, precision and evaluation harness. Two runs that both say "GRPO" in their titles may differ in a dozen ways that plausibly move the outcome, and the reported headline number does not distinguish between them. The project's own stack-sensitivity probe makes this concrete: a nominally matched configuration (Qwen3-8B, seed 42, $G = 8$, learning rate $1\times10^{-5}$, GSM8K-500, 30 steps, visible hyperparameters held equal, although the managed Tinker run used the Qwen3-8B-Base checkpoint and TRL the instruction-tuned Qwen3-8B) was run across four framework paths, two of which completed and two of which are dry-run placeholders, with results recorded in `experiments/results/framework_comparison.json` (source: platform_hybrid/paper/main_eai_body.tex). Four separate marginal one-way ANOVAs over 42 experiments give $\eta^2 = 0.546$ for library or framework, $\eta^2 = 0.558$ for training algorithm, $\eta^2 = 0.471$ for model family and $\eta^2 = 0.322$ for model scale — with the important caveat, stated by the source itself, that these factors are confounded with one another and with back-end, reward implementation, hardware and task (source: platform_hybrid/paper/main_eai_body.tex). Because each value is a marginal per-factor $\eta^2$ on a collinear corpus, the four sum to 1.897 and are not a variance decomposition; none estimates a factor's independent contribution (Chapter 4 §4.1; source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/_shared_methods.tex). The framework factor is nonetheless not a nuisance parameter to be wished away: marginally, outcomes vary with it about as strongly as with the algorithm label.

## 1.2 A Motivating Example

Consider a single training step with a group size of $G = 8$ on an easy GSM8K prompt that the current policy already solves. All eight sampled completions produce the correct final answer, so each receives reward $r_i = 1$. The group mean is $\mu_g = 1$ and the group standard deviation is $\sigma_g = 0$, so every advantage $A_i = 0$. The compute spent on that group is real — eight full forward-decode passes at whatever temperature the sampler is configured with — and the group supplies no task-learning signal. In this project's runners, which carry no KL term, that group contributes exactly zero gradient. Now imagine a batch of 16 such prompts in which 12 are already solved and 4 are not: $12/16 = 0.75$ of the batch is "dark" compute (source: platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex). What the Semester 4 work adds to this picture is that 0.75 is not a worst case: it is close to the 0.72–0.77 measured on the Phase-1 main configuration. It is not a universal value either, since the measured runs range from 0.16 to 0.84 depending on model, task and $G$ (Table 1.1), so any claim that GRPO is learning efficiently has to be read against the ZVF of its own regime.

Now suppose, having noticed this, that one wants to answer the obvious follow-up question: is the group-relative estimator actually a better choice than a learned critic? The natural experiment is a matched comparison — same model, same training prompts, same verifier, same step budget, same evaluation, with PPO and GRPO as the only difference. The project's first attempt at that contrast is recorded in a historical paper source under the label "Qwen3-8B", with a Welch $t$-test of $p = 0.7605$ computed over the final ten steps of a single run per arm (source: platform_hybrid/paper/main_eai_body.tex). Those ten steps are autocorrelated observations of one run, not ten independent runs, and the project's later methods note re-examined the row and marks it "provenance conflict; not estimable" (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/_shared_methods.tex). No effect size, confidence interval or power figure is therefore quoted for it here, and the Welch value is carried only as a provenance record (Chapter 4 §4.1). The project's own rule is that single-seed Tinker experiments have "zero statistical power" and are "treated as descriptive only and are not subject to significance testing" (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/_shared_methods.tex).

Two further observations from the same historical source make the example sharper rather than cleaner. First, the same table reports the PPO-versus-GRPO contrast on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct in the opposite direction; it is also one run per arm, so again no inferential statistic is quoted, but the direction alone shows that the apparent answer is not stable across model families (source: platform_hybrid/paper/main_eai_body.tex). Second, the Qwen arms were not executed on a common back-end — the GRPO arm ran through Tinker-managed infrastructure and the PPO arm on a Modal H100 cluster — and the source states this explicitly and declines to draw the algorithmic conclusion it invites: "We therefore treat the PPO/GRPO rows as stack-conditioned evidence, not as an algorithm diagnostic baseline", and "We therefore withhold a directional Qwen algorithm claim" (source: platform_hybrid/paper/main_eai_body.tex). The same section records an aggregation gap that illustrates why such care is warranted: the Qwen PPO last-10 GSM8K figure is 22.5% in the primary results ledger but 35.0% in the statistical summary, and the source names the ledger value as primary and treats the summary value as evidence of an aggregation defect rather than as a second measurement (source: platform_hybrid/paper/main_eai_body.tex).

The genuinely same-stack evidence in the repository is also a null, and its scope has to be stated with it. The same-stack PPO/GRPO run (Qwen2.5-0.5B, 40 steps, five paired seeds) is a two-digit addition task on which both arms are at ceiling: evaluation accuracy is 0.990 for GRPO and 0.992 for PPO, the paired difference is −0.002 with p = 0.374, and the 200 evaluation prompts are fresh random draws from the same generator used in training rather than a disjoint split (sources: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/samestack_ppo_grpo.json; platform_hybrid/experiments/modal/modal_samestack_ppo_grpo.py). At that ceiling a null cannot separate "no effect" from "no headroom", so the result is that no difference was detectable on a saturated task, not that the estimators are equivalent; a paired TOST at a ±0.005 margin gives p = 0.10 (Table 4.2 gives the exact loss form of both arms). The variant audit (not to be confused with campaign lane E1) takes four GRPO-family variants (DAPO, GSPO, Dr. GRPO, AERO) plus the GRPO reference, runs five arms across eight paired seeds for 40 units at a frozen stack fingerprint, and evaluates on 500 held-out GSM8K examples per unit. Every one of the four contrasts is inconclusive. The figures below are paired-$t$ 95% intervals and paired-$t$ $p$-values over the eight seeds, recomputed from `zvf-program/audit/results/full/*.json`: DAPO $+0.00100$, CI $[-0.00632, +0.00832]$, $p = 0.756$; GSPO $+0.00500$, CI $[-0.00357, +0.01357]$, $p = 0.210$; Dr. GRPO $-0.00200$, CI $[-0.01272, +0.00872]$, $p = 0.673$; AERO $-0.00075$, CI $[-0.01029, +0.00879]$, $p = 0.858$. The audit's own percentile-bootstrap intervals are narrower, for example $[-0.00950, +0.00725]$ for Dr. GRPO, and undercover at $n = 8$, so they are not used. After Benjamini–Hochberg correction every adjusted $p$ is at least 0.84. The pre-audit aggregator's verdict that the differences "disappear" is itself declared invalid, because the locked exact noncentral paired-$t$ minimum detectable effect of $0.010115893$ exceeds the $0.010$ margin the study was designed around (source: autoresearch/deli-neurips-tmlr-260802/audits/statistical_refutation.md). The audit's permitted wording is deliberately narrow: across eight paired seeds no arm produced a BH-significant held-out difference from GRPO, and none met the locked exact-power equivalence rule — which is not the same as saying the methods are the same, nor that they fail to improve. That audit runs on Qwen3-8B GSM8K with 500 evaluation examples per unit (Appendix A, P11).

Taken together, these two halves of the example are the whole motivation for what follows. The mechanism half shows that a large fraction of the compute in a GRPO run is not doing what a casual reading of the loss curve suggests it is doing. The attribution half shows that the most natural comparative question one would ask about that mechanism — is this estimator better than the alternative? — cannot be answered from the label "GRPO" and "PPO" alone, and that the answer on the project's own evidence is a non-significant contrast that is either back-end-confounded or measured at a performance ceiling, rather than a ranking. Neither observation is a defect of the GRPO estimator; both are defects of what gets measured and what gets reported.

## 1.3 Problem Statement

The project is organised around four problems, in decreasing order of how often the literature acknowledges them.

**Attribution is missing.** Comparative claims about RL post-training are routinely made across frameworks that differ in parser, sampler, KL coefficient, clip range, LoRA targets, precision and evaluator. A reported gain from "GRPO" over "PPO" is, on most published evidence, a gain from one complete stack over another complete stack, with no way to say which component produced it. The project's own marginal per-factor $\eta^2$ values put the framework factor at a magnitude comparable to the algorithm factor, although they are not a partition of the variance (source: platform_hybrid/paper/main_eai_body.tex; source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/_shared_methods.tex), and its same-stack PPO/GRPO contrast, run on a saturated 0.5B addition task (evaluation accuracy 0.990 GRPO, 0.992 PPO), returns a paired difference of −0.002 with $p = 0.374$ (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/samestack_ppo_grpo.json). The problem is not that these numbers are surprising; it is that no paper in this area is obliged to report the information that would let a reader separate them.

**Signal starvation is invisible.** Standard training logs report reward, loss, gradient norm, entropy and completion length. None of these surfaces the fraction of a batch whose group advantage is exactly zero. A run can show a rising reward curve and a falling ZVF, or a rising reward curve with a ZVF pinned near 0.8, and the two look identical in a loss plot. The fraction of sampling budget buying no reward-relative gradient is, in the project's own measurements, the largest measured source of zero-advantage sampling on this fixed setup (source: platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex), and it is not part of any standard telemetry.

**There is no reporting or provenance standard.** Even where authors are willing to describe their stack, there is no machine-readable form in which to do it, no validator that checks whether a claim's recorded configuration is internally consistent, and no accepted minimum set of fields that a stack-conditioned result must carry. Consequently, results cannot be pooled, cannot be compared across papers, and cannot be audited after the fact.

**Evaluation results are rarely reported with their terminal states.** Benchmark suites in the agentic and reasoning evaluation space are frequently multi-scope (a public split versus a private original contract, a benign-utility scope versus an adversarial scope), quota-limited, or gated behind private payloads. Reports tend either to omit such suites or to collapse them into a single number, which conceals how much of the suite was actually graded. The project treats this as a first-class concern rather than a presentational one: a result that is genuinely partial should be recorded as partial, with its coverage denominator and its reopen condition, and a lane that cannot be run at all should be recorded as externally blocked rather than quietly dropped.

## 1.4 Objectives

The project pursues eight objectives. They are stated as they were pursued, not retrofitted to the results.

**O1 — Build a single harness that can dispatch the same experiment to multiple back-ends.** Provide one control surface over Tinker, TRL, SkyRL, verl and OpenRLHF paths so that a configuration can be held fixed while the framework varies. The harness is the deliverable; it is not a completed multi-framework benchmark. Only Tinker and TRL produced completed runs, and those two used different base checkpoints (Qwen3-8B-Base and Qwen3-8B Instruct), so no framework comparison is claimed (Chapter 5 §5.2).

**O2 — Instrument the training loop so that signal starvation is directly observable.** Emit per-step telemetry carrying ZVF, gradient utilisation, policy entropy, completion length and KL divergence, and persist the raw per-group reward tensors so that any derived statistic can be deterministically recomputed after the fact rather than trusted from a log line.

**O3 — Measure the zero-variance regime on real reward tensors and test the levers that might move it.** Characterise ZVF empirically on genuine GRPO reward tensors, and investigate re-baselining and curriculum interventions as ways of converting dark compute into contrast.

**O4 — Establish and hold a scientific protocol.** Multi-seed runs, matched baselines, held-out evaluation with explicit denominators, deterministic recomputation, declared (not pre-registered) effect-size and power accounting, and reporting of negatives and nulls — including the cases where the answer is that the study was underpowered to answer.

**O5 — Scope the most original direction in the field.** A literature scan for white space, which in this project identified stack provenance and reporting standards as the unclaimed ground and produced the minimum reporting standard (P5) and the machine-readable stack registry (P6).

**O6 — Turn the diagnostic into an intervention.** Derive an adaptive group-size controller from the ZVF measurement and evaluate it as a training-time policy rather than a post-hoc observation (P7).

**O7 — Build and exercise an evaluation-governance protocol on one trained actor.** Run a single trained checkpoint against fourteen agentic and reasoning suites that were not part of its training data, each with its own native evaluator at a pinned revision, under receipt, budget and decontamination controls, and record every lane's terminal state. The contribution is the governance protocol and the receipt-bound record it produces. The campaign is not evidence about GRPO: the actor's training run was short (40 steps, Chapter 9 §9.1.1), and the only trained-versus-base comparison is the small-sample Table 9.B.

**O8 — Report under a deliberate asymmetry.** Where the evidence supports a structural or descriptive claim, state it; where it does not, withhold it. No cross-suite aggregate, no pooling of original-contract and replacement-scope numbers, and no claim of improvement over any baseline anywhere in the work. Baseline comparisons are reported where they exist, and none is claimed as an improvement. They are the paired trained-versus-base small-scale comparison of Chapter 9 (Table 9.B), which finds no significant difference in any lane, and the pre-to-post held-out comparisons of the controlled studies in Chapter 6.

The objectives are what the project set out to build. The results chapters answer four research questions that the objectives make testable. Each is mapped to the table that answers it, and §10.3 gives a one-line answer to each.

- **RQ1.** Is the Zero-Variance Fraction recomputable from stored reward tensors, and does it carry information about training outcome? Answered by the ZVF rows of Table 1.1 and §6.3.
- **RQ2.** With the training stack held fixed, do algorithm labels (PPO vs GRPO, GRPO vs Dr. GRPO) or group size separate on evaluation accuracy? Answered by the same-stack rows of Table 1.1, the loss-form table in §4.3, and §6.2.3, §6.4 and §6.5.
- **RQ3.** Can a machine-readable stack manifest flag label-flip risk between two runs from their records alone? Answered by §7.2 (registry health and `stackdiff`).
- **RQ4.** What does a governance-first evaluation of one trained actor yield, and what can it not establish? Answered by Table C.3 and Tables 9.A and 9.B.

## 1.5 Scope and Non-Goals

The scope of this report is measurement and diagnostics, not leaderboard performance. The controlled studies and the campaign actor use small-to-mid open models — the Qwen2.5, Qwen3, Qwen3.5 and Qwen3.6 families, from roughly 0.5B parameters up to the 35B sparse mixture-of-experts configuration used for the campaign actor. The P1 cross-scale roster is wider, 0.6B to 1T total parameters, but its frontier-scale anchors are single-seed runs through a managed API and are read descriptively (Chapter 6 §6.1; source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p1_abstract.tex). Verifiable rewards come from mathematics and code checkers, principally GSM8K exact-match and HumanEval-style unit tests. Held-out evaluation uses suites' own native evaluators at pinned revisions.

Several things are explicitly out of scope. This is not an attempt to set a state-of-the-art result on any benchmark, and no such claim is made anywhere in the report. It is not a study of frontier reasoning models. The evaluation sets used in the controlled comparisons are small, and their sizes differ by study: 200 fresh prompts for the 0.5B arithmetic runs, 200 per seed for the 1.5B GSM8K chain-of-thought runs, 500 for the variant audit, and only 8 to 20 in several Phase-1 openings (the layer-freeze result rests on n = 8). Table 1.1 gives the n behind each headline number. Claims about novel algorithms are not made: the length-weighting intervention (P4) and the adaptive controller (P7) were designed during the work and are reported as designed and simulated systems with their evaluation limits attached, not as demonstrated improvements.

The report states negative, null and withdrawn results alongside positive ones; this is its one general statement about reporting practice, and later chapters report the results without repeating it. Three evidentiary rules apply throughout and are worth stating here because they constrain what the later chapters are allowed to say. First, every number in this report is traceable to a repository file that was actually read; where a figure could not be sourced, the finding is described qualitatively and the figure is attributed to its source rather than reconstructed. Second, original-contract and replacement-scope benchmark results are never pooled and never averaged across suites — the two are different measurements of different things under different licences, and treating them as one column would manufacture a number that no suite reported. Third, no improvement over a baseline or over a base model is claimed unless the source states it with a test; where the source reports a difference without a test supporting it, or reports an inconclusive test, that is what is written here.
