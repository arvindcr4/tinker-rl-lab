# 2. Literature Survey

This chapter surveys the prior work on which the project is built and the gap it occupies. It revises the survey of the Phase-1 report (source: `platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex`), retaining that survey's seven-part structure and updating each part with the material the Semester 4 work added: the P1–P8 paper series and the E1–E14 held-out evaluation campaign. Two rules of construction carry over from Phase 1. The first concerns proportion. The contribution of this project is overwhelmingly a contribution of measurement and reporting practice rather than of a new training algorithm, so the literature that establishes measurement practice receives more room here than the literature that establishes methods. The second concerns evidence. A figure is quoted only where a repository source actually states it; where a source states a finding without giving a number, the finding is described qualitatively and the chapter says so. Where the project's own documents state the same quantity differently, the divergence is reported rather than silently reconciled, and Section 2.5 records one such divergence in full.

## 2.1 From RLHF and PPO to Group-Relative Methods

Policy-gradient post-training of language models begins with the RLHF pipeline. Christiano et al. (2017) established the three-stage pattern of supervised fine-tuning, reward-model training on human pairwise preferences, and reinforcement learning against that reward model; Ziegler et al. (2019) adapted it to language, collecting approximately 60,000 comparisons and introducing the per-token KL penalty against a frozen reference policy that has been standard ever since. Stiennon et al. (2020) scaled the recipe to a 6.7B summarisation model with 64,832 comparisons, demonstrated transfer to an out-of-distribution summarisation set (CNN/DailyMail), and produced the first empirical evidence of reward over-optimisation: continued optimisation against a frozen reward model eventually degrades the true objective. Ouyang et al. (2022) then built InstructGPT, whose three-stage pipeline used roughly 40 contractors to produce about 13,000 supervised fine-tuning prompts, about 33,000 reward-model prompts, and about 31,000 PPO prompts. Two details of that work matter for this project. First, the reward model was 6B rather than 175B because the larger reward model proved unstable during training — a reminder that the reward model is itself a trained component with its own failure modes. Second, the preference data were collected as rankings of K ∈ {4, …, 9} responses, which yields C(K, 2) pairwise comparisons from a single annotation batch; this is precisely the within-prompt comparative structure that group-relative methods would later exploit directly. InstructGPT's headline results were that a 1.3B InstructGPT was preferred to the 175B GPT-3 base model, that the 175B InstructGPT was preferred to GPT-3 in 85% of evaluations, that hallucination on a closed-domain task fell from 41% to 21%, that toxicity was reduced by roughly 25%, and that TruthfulQA performance roughly doubled; inter-annotator agreement was approximately 73%. The same source records four limitations — annotation cost, reward-model over-optimisation, the difficulty of aligning to user intent rather than to annotator preference, and the residual capability costs of alignment (source: `capstone-literature-survey/section_2_2_rlhf.tex`).

The optimiser for that pipeline was PPO. PPO's clipping mechanism, with ε ≈ 0.2, restricts each update's policy ratio, and generalised advantage estimation with its standard defaults (λ = 0.95, γ = 0.99) provides the advantage estimate. Recasting language generation as an RL problem makes the state the prompt plus generated prefix, the action the next token, the policy the language model, and the reward sparse and terminal. The practical consequence is a four-model architecture — policy, reference, reward, and value — whose mixed-precision memory footprint is roughly four times that of supervised fine-tuning alone; PPO-ptx mitigates capability regression by mixing pretraining gradients into the update. Zheng et al.'s "Secrets of RLHF" study found that policy constraints, not algorithmic choice, are the single most critical factor in whether RLHF succeeds: without them, training exhibits reward hacking, length explosion, and catastrophic pattern collapse (source: `capstone-literature-survey/section_2_3_ppo.tex`). That finding is the intellectual ancestor of the zero-variance analysis in this project, because it locates the failure mode in the constraint structure rather than in the objective.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), introduced with DeepSeekMath (Shao et al., 2024), removes the learned value network altogether. For each prompt it samples a group of G completions, scores them with a reward function, and standardises each completion's reward against the group mean and standard deviation; the resulting z-score serves as the advantage for every token of that completion. In the DeepSeekMath configuration G = 64, β = 0.04, and ε = 0.2. Eliminating the value model reduces the number of trained and served models from four to three and cuts memory requirements by approximately 25%. DeepSeekMath-RL 7B reached 51.7% top-1 accuracy on MATH against a 46.8% supervised fine-tuning baseline, 60.9% with self-consistency over 64 samples, GSM8K improved from 82.9% to 88.2%, and CMATH improved from 84.6% to 88.8% despite no Chinese data in the RL training set (source: `capstone-literature-survey/section_2_5_grpo.tex`). The paper's iterative variant retains 10% of historical data in a replay buffer to prevent catastrophic forgetting in the co-evolving reward model. RLOO (Ahmadian et al., 2024) is the closely related leave-one-out estimator that baselines each sample against the mean of the *other* samples in the group; it already performs a within-group baseline, and is therefore directly relevant to the signal-starvation analysis this project conducts (source: `platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex`).

DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025) then demonstrated that pure outcome-reward RL can elicit chain-of-thought behaviour from a base model without supervised cold-start data. R1-Zero was trained on DeepSeek-V3-Base, a 671B mixture-of-experts model, using rule-based rewards only and no neural reward model. AIME 2024 pass@1 rose from 15.6% to 71.0% over training — the revised *Nature* version reports 77.9% after extended training — with consensus decoding over 64 samples (cons@64) reaching 86.7% and MATH-500 reaching 95.9%. The source states the training configuration as 16 sampled outputs per question, a group size of 512, a learning rate of 3 × 10⁻⁶, a KL coefficient of 0.001, and approximately 10,400 policy update steps. The paper documents spontaneous chain-of-thought, self-verification, and the widely reported "aha moment" as emergent rather than taught behaviours. The full R1 model, trained with a four-stage pipeline that includes a language-consistency reward, reports 79.8 on AIME, 97.3 on MATH-500, and 71.5 on GPQA, against 79.2 / 96.4 / 75.7 for o1-1217. Distillation into six students from 1.5B to 70B produced Distill-Qwen-1.5B at 28.9% AIME and 83.9% MATH-500, and Distill-Qwen-32B at 72.6% and 94.3%, against 47.0% and 91.6% for Qwen2.5-32B-Zero trained from scratch; the 7B and 16B base models failed to show meaningful reasoning gains from direct RL (source: `capstone-literature-survey/section_2_6_deepseek_r1.tex`).

For this project, the significance of that lineage is narrower than the headline numbers suggest. GRPO's defining operation is a within-group standardisation, and a standardisation is undefined when the group's rewards are all identical. That degenerate case is not an edge case: it is the modal case early in training on a hard task and late in training on an easy one. The rest of this chapter and the empirical chapters that follow are concerned with what that degeneracy does to the training signal, and with how honestly the field currently reports it.

## 2.2 Direct Preference Optimisation as a Contrast

An orthogonal branch of post-training removes on-policy sampling entirely. Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO; Rafailov et al., 2023) exploits the fact that the RLHF objective with a KL constraint admits a closed-form optimal policy, which permits the reward to be reparameterised in terms of the policy itself. The result is a simple classification loss over a fixed dataset of preference pairs, with no reward model, no value model, and no sampling loop. Reported savings are 40–60% in both GPU memory and wall-clock time relative to PPO. Four limitations are documented in the same source: the training data are static and offline, so there is no online exploration; the loss can displace likelihood on responses outside the preference pairs; and the method has weaker guarantees for long-horizon alignment, where credit must propagate across many steps (source: `capstone-literature-survey/section_2_4_dpo.tex`).

The contrast is structurally instructive for this project. DPO cannot suffer zero-variance collapse, because it never standardises within a sampled group — its contrast is supplied by the dataset rather than constructed at training time. But it equally cannot exploit a verifier that is available in the loop, which is the property that makes RLVR attractive on mathematics. GRPO's zero-variance pathology is therefore the price of its on-policy, verifier-in-the-loop design, and measuring that price is exactly what makes the pathology worth quantifying. The DPO family has since diversified into inverse-preference and non-paired variants — IPO, KTO, ORPO, SimPO, and SLiC among them — which the project's cross-library protocol treats as no-rollout baselines (sources: `capstone-literature-survey/section_2_4_dpo.tex`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`).

The boundary between the two families is also less sharp than it first appears. Wu et al. prove that GRPO is "secretly DPO" — the group-level advantage constitutes an implicit contrastive objective — and report that 2-GRPO retains 98.1% of the performance of 16-GRPO at 12.5% of the rollout budget (source: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`). That reduction is the theoretical counterpart of the PCD statistic this project develops: if a group is exactly as informative as the correct/incorrect pairs it contains, then the pairwise-contrast density PCD = ((G − 1)/G) · E[p(1 − p)] is the natural magnitude-aware summary of a group's usable signal, and the group-size question becomes a question about how much contrast a given sampling budget buys (source: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p7_intro.tex`). The present project is careful about what it inherits from that argument: the reduction is cited as prior theory, while the empirical claims resting on it are scoped to this project's own measurements, reported in Chapter 6.

## 2.3 Fixing GRPO's Biases and Collapse

A dense and fast-growing literature targets specific GRPO pathologies. Dr. GRPO (Tong et al., 2025) removes two normalisation terms from the standard objective — the per-response 1/L factor and the question-difficulty amplification — restoring the sequence-level gradient up to a global scale, and is motivated explicitly as a fix for reward hacking through length (sources: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_intro.tex`, `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p6_intro.tex`). DAPO (Yu et al., 2025, from ByteDance) changes four things in one release: asymmetric Clip-Higher clipping, dynamic sampling that resamples until the batch carries signal, token-level loss normalisation, and over-long filtering; the reported result is 50 points on AIME 2024 with a 32B model (source: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`). GSPO (Qwen team, 2025) moves the importance ratio from the token level to the sequence level and was credited with stabilising the Qwen3 series, particularly its mixture-of-experts variants. Production-side variants include VAPO and Dr. SAC, the latter arguing that carefully implemented REINFORCE with a value baseline matches GRPO on several RLHF tasks (source: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`).

A third thread attacks the zero-variance failure mode directly, and this is the thread the project's own diagnostic sits in. GRESO pre-screens prompts before sampling so that uninformative prompts are never paid for. CPPO prunes completions after rollout, reporting up to 7.98× speedup on GSM8K. NGRPO adds advantage calibration and asymmetric clipping so that all-incorrect groups still carry gradient. Scaf-GRPO injects tiered scaffolding hints when learning stagnates, reporting a 44.3% relative improvement on AIME 2024. MO-GRPO addresses vanilla GRPO's collapse to a single reward component under multiple objectives through variance-based reward re-weighting. SSPO operates at sentence granularity to balance GSPO's stability against token-level GRPO's expressiveness, improving by 3.56 points on mathematics benchmarks. AERO / RL-ZVP takes the opposite approach to filtering: it extracts signal *from* zero-variance groups through entropy-guided advantage shaping (sources: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`, `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p7_intro.tex`). GVPO re-weights by within-group variance (source: `platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex`). The tail continues with λ-GRPO, MC-GRPO (median-centred advantage), G²RPO, ConSPO, and Goldilocks RL.

One act of bookkeeping in this literature deserves emphasis, because it is a claim about what may *not* be inferred. AVSPO, query recycling, ConSPO, and Goldilocks RL are described in the project's own related-work source as prior or concurrent interventions, not as evidence for this project's runner, and the paper explicitly claims neither priority nor an intervention effect over them (source: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`). The present thesis inherits that disclaimer without qualification.

Against this background, the project's own contribution to the area is deliberately meagre in algorithmic ambition and deliberately demanding in evidential standard. The Zero-Variance Fraction (ZVF) — the fraction of groups whose completions are all correct or all incorrect — was measured across libraries, model scales, and training phases in the companion ZVF paper, and that paper stopped at description: ZVF tracks collapse, drifts upward, and is mechanically entangled with accuracy and group size, so it declined to promote ZVF to a predictive or causal statistic (source: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p7_intro.tex`). A minimal binomial model of group degeneracy was then proposed, in which the expected ZVF indicator is h_G(p) = p^G + (1 − p)^G and the usable signal is S = p(1 − p)(1 − h_G(p)); three regularities follow in closed form, each paired with an empirical check drawn from a 368-run Weights & Biases audit that counts all logged runs and is therefore a superset of the benchmark's 70+ core training runs. Because h_G(p) is symmetric under p ↔ 1 − p, ZVF assigns the same value to a mastered prompt and to a hopeless one, producing the U-shape measured across seven models; and because ZVF is an existence statistic, adding reward jitter of order 10⁻⁴ drives measured ZVF to zero while shifting the measured within-group contrast by less than 10⁻⁶. The pairwise-contrast density is proposed as the magnitude-aware replacement, and the toy-scale validation reports a gradient-norm correlation of corr = +0.71, marked directional only. The word *controller* in that work names the implemented policy, not an established improvement: the decisive static-G = 16 matched-token bakeoff remains unrun (source: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p7_intro.tex`).

The one genuinely measured GRPO-variant comparison in the repository is a Dr. GRPO versus GRPO control. On Qwen2.5-0.5B at group size 8, 40 steps, and 5 seeds, held-out accuracy was indistinguishable between arms: GRPO 0.987 ± 0.003 against Dr. GRPO 0.992 ± 0.003, paired difference +0.5 percentage points, t = 1.05, df = 4, p = 0.35; mean completion length was 4.74 against 4.71 tokens. In a second regime — Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct on GSM8K with a 200-token chain-of-thought cap and 3 seeds — GRPO moved from 20.2% to 26.3% and Dr. GRPO from 20.5% to 25.5%; per-seed McNemar tests were significant in 2 of 3 seeds for GRPO (p = 0.071, 0.036, 0.016) and 1 of 3 for Dr. GRPO (p = 0.122, 0.017, 0.122), and the pooled p < 10⁻³ is expressly rejected as overstating significance. Neither arm inflated length over 30 steps (GRPO 195 → 180 tokens; Dr. GRPO 194 → 187). The same source contains head-to-head and rank-correlation tables whose values are *projections*, not measurements: the GRPO baseline row is a synthetic baseline produced by a dry run of `platform_hybrid/experiments/variance_mitigation_integration.py`, the file states that it makes no measurement claim for any row of that table, the correlation coefficients (0.78 / 0.66 / 0.63 / 0.31) carry the same caveat, and the configuration proxies under `platform_local/unified/` are explicitly not faithful reimplementations. Those numbers are reported here only to mark them as unusable as evidence (sources: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/variance_mitigation_comparison.tex`; `platform_hybrid/experiments/results/drgrpo_vs_grpo.json`; `platform_hybrid/experiments/results/drgrpo_gsm8k_cot.json`). The nearest thing to a headline cross-library effect in the project is also the one that must be read most carefully: holding the visible group size, learning rate, data, seed, and label fixed while changing stacks moved final GSM8K reward from 85.6% to 5.0%, a 17× swing; the audit that followed found the managed stack had also pinned a different base checkpoint, so the source describes this as an under-specification exhibit rather than a backend-only causal effect (source: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p6_intro.tex`). The complementary telemetry fact is that running "DAPO" on an open Colab trainer whose dynamic sampling demonstrably filters zero-variance groups produced mean ZVF 0.00, while running "DAPO" on a closed managed stack where only an asymmetric-clip surrogate is user-settable produced mean ZVF 0.58 — the same label, opposite telemetry (source: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p6_intro.tex`).

## 2.4 Verifiable Rewards, Frameworks and Reporting Practice

RLVR — reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards — was formalised for open post-training in TÜLU 3 (Lambert et al., 2024), which is the source this project follows in treating a programmatic checker rather than a learned preference model as the reward (source: `platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex`). The implementation landscape the project unifies is crowded and heterogeneous. On the training side it includes TRL, OpenRLHF, veRL (HybridFlow), NeMo-RL, DeepSpeed-Chat, trlX, Axolotl, and Tinker as a managed back-end; each makes different default choices about advantage normalisation, KL estimators, tokenisation loss masking, rollout batching, and importance-sampling granularity, and the project identifies those differences as the principal source of the cross-library gap it quantifies. On the inference side, vLLM and SGLang supply the rollout backends on which modern frameworks depend, with FlashAttention and LoRA as load-bearing kernels (sources: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`; `platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex`). Tinker is adopted here through its `message_with_tool` interface as the neutral substrate for tool-use extensions, and the same source explicitly cites tool-use and agentic RL settings as concurrent environments in which this project's ZVF measurements will need to be re-evaluated rather than assumed to transfer (source: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`).

The evaluation benchmarks are equally well established and equally awkward. GSM8K comprises 8,500 problems split 7,473 / 1,319, on which a fine-tuned GPT-3 verifier reached 35% at release while current systems exceed 95%; GSM8K-Platinum has since documented label noise in the original set, which is why the source describes the benchmark as necessary but not sufficient. MATH comprises 12,500 problems across seven subjects and five difficulty levels, on which the original results reached 6.9%; MATH-500 is the 500-problem subset introduced with the Lightman process-supervision work, on which current systems sit at roughly 99%. AIME comprises 15 problems per year with integer answers in 0–999; reported AIME 2024 accuracies include GPT-5 at 95.7% and Grok 4 at 94.3%. Because these benchmarks are close to saturation, the source frames chain-of-thought as the action space that makes them interesting at all, and identifies process reward models as the bridge from terminal outcome signals to step-level credit (source: `capstone-literature-survey/section_2_8_mathematical_reasoning_benchmarks.tex`).

Reporting practice is where the project's own contribution is anchored, and its lineage is explicit. Model Cards (Mitchell et al., 2019) introduced documentation of intended use, out-of-scope use, and per-slice evaluation results; Datasheets for Datasets (Gebru et al., 2021) extended the idea to datasets, covering composition, preprocessing, annotation process, and discriminatory-impact mitigation; Data Statements (Bender and Friedman, 2018) added the speaker-demographic axis; and Data Cards (Pushkarna et al., 2022) bridged research and industry practice with schema, provenance, sampling-distribution, and audit-transparency fields. A cross-coupling audit at iteration 109 maps these four works onto the project's legacy 18-field experimental manifest, showing that they cover 12 distinct fields, that the remaining six are either RL-specific (per-step ZVF, group-size schedule) or stack-coverage items with no direct analogue in the prior literature, and that items 9–12 — annotation process and annotator demographics — have the lowest evidence density in the live manifests. The same iteration verified all four entries against CrossRef metadata (4/4 pass on year, five-gram title, and author-family overlap ≥ 0.6) and retrofitted two entries that had been present in the bibliography but never cited (sources: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p5_related.tex`; `platform_hybrid/experiments/results/p5p8/p5_iter109_minreport_coupling.tsv`; `platform_hybrid/experiments/results/p5p8/p5_iter109_crossref_verify.tsv`; `platform_hybrid/experiments/results/p5p8/p5_iter109_bib_audit.tsv`). The resulting standard, MIN-REPORT-RL, is an eight-item list aligned at eight items on 2026-07-11, whose eighth item (held-out pass@k) is an evaluation-report field rather than run-start stack identity (source: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p5_related.tex`, `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p6_intro.tex`).

The reproducibility literature to which this standard is a deliberate echo is older and better established. Henderson et al. (2018) showed that deep-RL results depend on codebase, hyperparameters, and even the choice of random seeds, to the point that the same algorithm from two repositories behaves as two algorithms. Colas et al. (2019) and Agarwal et al. (2021, `rliable`) supplied the statistical repair kit — rigorous comparison protocols and small-sample-aware aggregate metrics with interquartile-mean reporting and performance profiles. Jordan et al. (2024) argued that most published RL comparisons lack statistical power. Dodge et al. (2019) proposed reporting expected validation performance as a function of compute budget; the project's run manifest is the RL-for-LLM analogue, replacing *budget* with *stack*. Pineau et al. (2020) formalised the NeurIPS reproducibility criteria, and the project draws the explicit lesson that self-attested checklists have limits, which motivates an artifact-computed badge rather than a declared one (sources: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p5_related.tex`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`). Statistical treatments of evaluation noise (Miller et al.; Zhang et al.) quantify the uncertainty floor against which the project's own within-noise findings should be read, and the evaluation-stack sensitivity documented by the LM Evaluation Harness effort, by audits of reasoning evaluations, and by contamination studies of the data side of the same coin, together establish that RL *training* inherits all of this — the verifier is an evaluator in the loop — plus the additional vectors unique to on-policy optimisation (source: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p5_related.tex`).

Three existing resources come closest to the project's registry and are the reason it exists in its present form. HELM aggregates evaluation across a large model and scenario matrix, but its unit is the (model, scenario) cell, not the training stack. The LM Evaluation Harness standardises evaluation by *executing* implementations, whereas the registry here standardises by *describing* stacks it cannot execute — many of them closed — which is precisely why the project required a null-versus-absent convention and an R5 rung that records un-auditability rather than converting it into a false value. Papers with Code catalogued paper–code–leaderboard linkage at scale, stopped at the repository boundary, and was discontinued in July 2025; the source treats its fate as a governance cautionary tale. The project's own claim to novelty is narrow and stated negatively: no existing resource provides the executed loss form at GRPO-variant granularity, per-component implementation status, or an unauditability marker (sources: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p6_related_resources.tex`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p6_intro.tex`).

## 2.5 Scaling and Layer-Adaptation Context

Compute-optimal pretraining is governed by well-characterised scaling laws (Kaplan et al.; Hoffmann et al., Chinchilla), but RL post-training is much less well characterised, and the sources the project builds on separate cleanly into those that predict and those that describe. Hilton et al. (2023) derived compute-efficiency frontiers for RL in games and robotics. For RLHF, Gao et al. (2023) characterised the over-optimisation curve of reward models — the point at which additional optimisation against a frozen reward model buys a decreasing true objective — and Rafailov et al. (2024) provided scaling laws for DPO and PPO that show a strong dependence on reward-model quality. Nimmaturi et al. (2025) derived an empirical scaling law specifically for GRPO, identifying three training phases (slow start, rapid improvement, plateau) and reporting that most of the benefit is exhausted by roughly 80% of a single epoch. Tan et al. (2025) treat group size as a budget knob in an empirical scaling study of GRPO post-training. Snell et al. treat inference-time compute as a scaling axis in its own right, and the project's own related-work source cites these as the works against which its measurements should be read (source: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`).

The project's scaling study covers 70+ runs across seven RL libraries and five model families. Its own documents do not agree on the upper end of the model-range: the P1 paper's introduction states the range as 0.6B–1T, while the P2, P3, and P4 introductions state 0.6B–~671B, and the related-work source states the cross-family data as spanning 0.6B to 235B across five families and four frameworks (sources: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p1_intro.tex`, `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p2_intro.tex`, `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p3_intro.tex`, `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_intro.tex`, `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`). This divergence is a reporting defect in the portfolio's own documents and is recorded here rather than harmonised, because harmonising it would mean asserting a range that no single source states. The principal empirical result of the scaling work is a null: across that range, no reliable scaling law is recovered from reward curves (sources: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/frontier_synthesis_scaling.tex`, `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p1_conclusion.tex`). The companion group-size study adds a second bounded negative: a single-seed sweep does not establish an optimum, trainability is non-monotone in group size, and the measured signal-to-noise ratio grows at only about 52% of the √G ideal, with G ∈ {2, 4, 8, 16} measured and G ∈ {4, 8, 16, 32, 64} explicitly reconstructed rather than run.

Layer adaptation supplies the second half of this section's context, and connects the project's white-box investigation to the parameter-efficient fine-tuning literature. LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) freezes the pretrained weights and injects trainable low-rank matrices into the attention projections, achieving a 128× reduction in trainable parameters at d = 4096 with rank r = 16, which is on the order of 0.1% of the model's parameters. QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023) extends this by quantising the frozen base to NF4 with double quantisation at 0.37 bits per parameter — approximately 3 GB for a 65B model, permitting 65B fine-tuning on a single 48 GB GPU. The motivating arithmetic is that Adam at mixed precision costs roughly 16N bytes, exceeding 100 GB for a 7B model. Prefix tuning, prompt tuning, and adapter layers are the earlier members of the same family, and LoRA's advantages for RL specifically are that the frozen base doubles as the reference policy and that the trainable parameter count is small enough for modest hardware (source: `capstone-literature-survey/section2_7_peft.tex`). The project's layer-adaptation pillar asks a narrower and orthogonal question: where inside the network GRPO-style adaptation concentrates, and whether the layers that will matter can be identified early enough to freeze the rest. The reported finding is moderate concentration of per-layer adaptation, with the important negative that step-1 predictability does *not* survive scaling. This connects directly to Liu et al. (2025), who found that RL fine-tuning updates only 5–30% of model parameters across seven algorithms and ten model families, suggesting that RL activates a sparse subnetwork (sources: `platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`). The length-bias pillar completes the scaling picture with its own bounded null: under a 200-token GSM8K generation cap, GRPO and Dr. GRPO produce held-out gains that are indistinguishable at the project's seed budget, and neither arm shows length inflation; the source is careful to state that the 200-token cap is part of the estimand rather than a nuisance detail, so the null bounds the capped, short-horizon regime and cannot adjudicate uncapped reasoning or long agent trajectories (source: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_intro.tex`).

## 2.6 Process-, Tree- and Stability-Aware Variants

Beyond the group-baseline fixes, a parallel line of work changes *what* signal drives the update. Process reward models supply denser, step-level feedback instead of a single terminal reward, which reduces the chance that a whole group collapses to identical terminal rewards. Let's Verify Step by Step (Lightman et al., 2023) showed that step-level PRMs substantially outperform outcome reward models on GSM8K and MATH; Math-Shepherd derives process rewards from automatically generated process annotations, avoiding costly human labels; PRM800K and PRMBench supply step-level evaluation data; OmegaPRM uses Monte-Carlo tree search to supervise process rewards without human-in-the-loop; ReasonEval introduces reasoning-specific reward models; and Implicit PRM learns process rewards implicitly from outcome labels, with VersaPRM extending process rewards to multi-domain tasks. Tree-of-thought search, self-consistency, and critic-style post-hoc verifiers all either benefit from or substitute for process supervision (source: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`). The same source documents the counter-current that matters most here: VerifyBench and "A Sober Look" warn that reward hacking and evaluation artefacts can inflate perceived gains on reasoning benchmarks, which is the project's stated motivation for insisting on binary verifiable rewards in its own mathematics work.

Stability-aware variants constrain the update itself. GSPO's sequence-level importance ratio, discussed above, is the clearest instance, credited with stabilising the Qwen3 mixture-of-experts series. SSPO's sentence-granularity objective is explicitly framed as balancing GSPO's stability against token-level GRPO's expressiveness. Entropy-guided advantage shaping in AERO / RL-ZVP regulates the update when the group carries no reward contrast, and GRESO's pre-screening is a sampling-side stability control. On the reasoning-RL side, GRPO-Lead, LCPO, and LIMR tune reasoning depth through length-aware rewards, Search-R1 trains reasoning agents to interleave search with chain-of-thought, and TTRL performs test-time RL against self-generated verifiers (source: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`).

The Phase-1 survey's judgement on this body of work is retained without amendment, because the Semester 4 evidence has not changed it. These methods propose *levers*; this project first builds the *instrumentation* needed to tell whether any such lever converts wasted rollouts into held-out accuracy — ZVF, group utilisation, pairwise-contrast density, and the collapse decomposition. The nulls reported in the results chapters are exactly the kind of evidence that instrumentation is designed to produce, and they constitute a standing caution against adopting a variant on the strength of a single-seed improvement. The project applies that caution to its own proposals: the adaptive group-size controller is described as an implemented policy rather than an established improvement, and its decisive matched-token comparison remains unrun (sources: `platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p7_intro.tex`).

## 2.7 Positioning the Portfolio

The portfolio's positioning against prior art is a per-pillar statement of what is claimed and, more importantly, what is not. Table 2.1 reproduces the Phase-1 positioning and updates it with the Semester 4 evidence.

| Pillar | Closest prior art | Narrowed axis claimed |
|---|---|---|
| P1 (scaling) | whole-model scaling and saturation laws; Liu et al. on sparse RL subnetworks | Per-layer adaptation is moderately concentrated; step-1 predictability does not survive scaling; no reliable reward-curve scaling law recovered |
| P2 (ZVF) | DAPO dynamic sampling, GVPO, AERO/RL-ZVP, GRESO, CPPO, NGRPO | *Measure* ZVF as an existence statistic; show it is entangled with accuracy and group size; propose PCD as the magnitude-aware replacement |
| P3 (group size) | integer-G sweeps; MC-GRPO; Tan et al. budget-knob treatment | Trainability is non-monotone in G; a single-seed sweep does not establish an optimum; measured SNR grows at ~52% of the √G ideal |
| P4 (length bias) | Dr. GRPO and λ-GRPO length fixes | A bounded null: under a 200-token cap, GRPO and Dr. GRPO held-out gains are indistinguishable with no length inflation |
| P5 (reporting standard) | Datasheets, Model Cards, Data Statements, Data Cards | An eight-item RL-run reporting standard targeting the stack rather than the label |
| P6 (registry) | static run-config registries; HELM; LM Evaluation Harness; Papers with Code | Machine-readable stack records with per-component implementation status and an unauditability marker |
| P7 (controller) | heuristic adaptive-G (G²RPO-A); DAPO dynamic sampling | Closed-form h_G(p) prediction of ZVF, with a controller described as implemented, not established |
| P8 (integrity auditing) | output-level reward-hack benchmarks | Infrastructure-telemetry integrity auditing; P8 was archived and absorbed into the portfolio |

: Positioning of the four de-confound pillars against closest prior art and the narrowed axis each claims.

The two crowded pillars are P2 and P4, where the literature is dense and the project accordingly narrows its claim to measurement rather than to a new algorithm. The widest open ground remains the systems and reporting side, where no GRPO-specific machine-readable standard and no infrastructure-level integrity benchmark exists — the gap Section 2.4 establishes from three directions: HELM's unit is the (model, scenario) cell rather than the training stack, the LM Evaluation Harness standardises evaluation by executing code while the registry must describe stacks it cannot execute, and Papers with Code stopped at the repository boundary and was discontinued in July 2025 (sources: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p6_related_resources.tex`; `platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex`).

The Semester 4 held-out campaign extends this positioning from method-comparison to benchmark practice. A single Tinker-trained actor — base `Qwen/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B` with the `pavlov-portfolio-qwen36-seed809-stepfinal` adapter, in bfloat16 — was evaluated against fourteen held-out agentic and reasoning suites under receipt, budget, and decontamination governance. The results are reported per suite and never pooled or averaged across suites. SWE-bench Pro recorded 2/731 = 0.274% pass@1 under its original contract, with all eighteen non-native outcomes retained in the denominator. VerilogEval recorded 129/312 = 41.35% pass@1 (67/156 code-completion and 62/156 spec-to-RTL), with a 129/311 sensitivity figure retained non-canonically. LAB-Bench's public split was completed at 1,967/1,967 across eight categories, with per-category accuracies ranging from 0.787 for TableQA to 0.000 for CloningScenarios. AgentDojo's benign-utility scope completed at 97/97. Omni-MATH closed with all 4,428 dispositions recorded and the official accuracy of 2,271/4,428 = 51.31% reproduced, with two parser exclusions verified to have no score effect. The remaining lanes are recorded as partial, quota-blocked, or externally blocked: E2 FrontierSWE at 1/17 tasks with a normalised replay of 0.8628, E4 BankerToolBench at 1/100 tasks with a recovery metric of 0.3115, E5 APEX-Agents at 7/480 natively scored with a prefix mean of 0.050505, E9 MLE-bench at 40/75 competitions natively graded with a null suite score, E13 BALROG at 13/255 episodes, and six lanes (E3, E7, E8-original, E10-original, E12, E14-original) closed as externally blocked with no provider response and named reopen conditions. Structural claims were re-verified by deterministic code checks on 19 September 2026 at 11/11 PASS (source: `outputs/E1_E14_FINAL_RESULTS_2026-09-19.md`). No claim of improvement over a baseline or over the base model is made anywhere in the campaign, and the agentic-suite results in particular — E4's mean reward of 0.0 across 100 base-model trials, with 27 or more trajectories ending in role-token degeneration — are reported as evidence about tool-dialogue collapse rather than as evidence about the tasks the suites nominally measure (source: `outputs/E1_E14_FINAL_RESULTS_2026-09-19.md`).

Two gaps in this survey should be stated plainly, since the chapter's purpose is to locate the work rather than to flatter it. First, the primary papers for the fourteen evaluation suites are not surveyed here at the depth the algorithmic literature receives; the suites are discussed as instruments, sourced to the campaign's own results document, and their native protocols are documented in Chapter 9 and its receipt trail rather than in this chapter. Second, the survey's treatment of tool-use and agentic RL is inherited from the project's own related-work source and is explicitly flagged there as a concurrent setting in which the ZVF measurements will need to be re-evaluated rather than assumed to transfer (source: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`). What the chapter does establish is that the project's contribution is a measurement and reporting contribution, that the literature it sits in is dense enough that claim-narrowing rather than claim-extension is the honest move, and that the specific gap the portfolio occupies — machine-readable, per-component, executed-loss-form disclosure for group-relative RL runs — is not filled by any of the resources surveyed above.
