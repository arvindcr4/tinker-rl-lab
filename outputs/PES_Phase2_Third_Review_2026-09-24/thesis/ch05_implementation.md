# 5. Implementation Details

This chapter describes what was built. Its organising claim is that the engineering contribution of the project is a **dispatch and evidence layer** rather than a training algorithm: the algorithmic content of GRPO is inherited from the literature, but the work of making six reinforcement-learning frameworks and six compute backends addressable through one Tinker-style interface — and of making every number that comes out of them traceable to a sealed, hash-pinned artefact — is where the implementation effort sits. The chapter proceeds from the abstraction (the adapter architecture), through the concrete training loop, to the two engineering episodes that most shaped the codebase (the parallel logging defect and the Colab white-box path), and finally to the evaluation harness and the recovery re-implementations undertaken after an execution-source loss in the final phase of the project.

## 5.1 Two Rosters, One Dispatch Surface

A reader approaching the project's comparison material will encounter two different framework rosters, and conflating them is the single most likely misreading of the implementation. The paper's methods note states the distinction explicitly: the cross-launcher LLM-RL scaffold referenced in the framework-gap discussion is a seven-library roster — Tinker, TRL, SkyRL, veRL, OpenRLHF, Atropos, and HF reference launchers — that targets LLM post-training launchers specifically, while the cross-RL-library evidence reported elsewhere uses a different roster. Critically for this chapter, the same note records that "only Tinker and TRL produced completed runs in this release; veRL and OpenRLHF entries in the `framework_comparison.json` artefact are dry-run placeholders" (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/_shared_methods.tex). The veRL and OpenRLHF entries in that artefact carry peak and last-ten-average reward values, but they are the output of the harness resolving a launch plan, not the output of a training run, and they must never be pooled with real results or read as framework comparison evidence.

Atropos occupies a different position again. Rather than being a cell in the dispatch matrix, it is integrated through a dedicated package under `platform_tinker/atropos/`, a `tinker_atropos` module containing `config.py`, `api.py`, `trainer.py`, `types.py`, `dataset.py`, `stats_utils.py`, and an `environments/` directory with per-task environment servers (source: platform_tinker/atropos/). Its integration model is the Atropos managed-server pattern: a three-terminal workflow in which `run-api` starts the Atropos API, `launch_training.py --config configs/default.yaml` starts Tinker training against a `TINKER_API_KEY`, and an environment server — a built-in one or any external Atropos environment pointed at the same config file — serves rollouts. The README's quickstart example runs a fifty-step training run on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct against a GSM8K environment, and the environment contract is that the `env` and `openai` sections are consumed by the environment server while the `tinker` section is consumed by the trainer and ignored by the environment (source: platform_tinker/atropos/README.md). This separation is what allows an arbitrary existing Atropos environment to be reused against the Tinker backend without modification to the environment.

## 5.2 The Adapter Architecture: What versus Where

The dispatch surface is built on one orthogonal decomposition, stated in the backends module docstring: "A `Backend` knows **where** to run one experiment cell (local GPU, Modal, Colab, vast.ai, GCP, HF Spaces); the framework dimension (trl/tinker/verl/openrlhf/skyrl) is the **what**. `plan()` resolves a cell into a concrete `LaunchPlan` without spending compute, so `--dry-run` and the matrix test work uniformly" (source: platform_local/unified/backends/base.py). The `LaunchPlan` dataclass carries `backend`, `framework`, `command`, `driver_file`, `output`, `env`, and `notes`, with a `format()` method for printing; the default `_execute` runs `subprocess.run(plan.command, shell=True, check=True)`. Because plan resolution is separated from execution, the entire matrix can be validated without a GPU, an API key, or a dollar of spend — a property that the dry-run placeholders discussed above depend on, and which is also the reason those placeholders must be labelled as such.

The matrix itself ships as data. `platform_local/unified/matrix.json` declares schema `tinkerrl-framework-backend-matrix-v1` with thirty cells — five frameworks (trl, tinker, verl, openrlhf, skyrl) crossed with six backends (local, modal, colab, vast, gcp, hfspaces), five cells per row and per column — and a `canonical_experiment` block that pins the single configuration every cell is meant to instantiate: `Qwen/Qwen3-8B` on GSM8K with GRPO, thirty training steps, `num_generations` 8, per-device batch size 2, gradient accumulation 8, learning rate `1e-06`, LoRA rank 16 and alpha 32 over all linear modules, `beta` 0.0, `epsilon` 0.2, maximum completion length 1024, and seed 211 (source: platform_local/unified/matrix.json). Each cell is a concrete plan; a representative one reads `{"framework":"trl","backend":"local","command":"python -m platform_local.unified --backend local --framework trl --model Qwen/Qwen3-8B","driver_file":"platform_local/trl_integrations/trainer.py","output":"./checkpoints/","env":[],"notes":"in-process framework dispatch"}`.

The launcher implements the dispatch. `UnifiedLauncher` holds three registries: `FRAMEWORKS` mapping `skyrl`, `tinker`, `verl`, `openrlhf`, and `trl` to human-readable labels; `BACKENDS` mapping `local`, `modal`, `colab`, `vast`, `gcp`, and `hfspaces` to labels; and `ALGORITHMS` covering `grpo`, `ppo`, `reinforce`, `dapo`, and `dpo` (source: platform_local/unified/launcher.py). Dispatch is outer-on-backend and inner-on-framework, which is why the per-framework handlers can each be written against the framework's own idioms. `_run_tinker` shells to `python -m platform_tinker.tinkerrl.grpo_cli --preset <spec.task>`; `_run_openrlhf` delegates to `platform_modal/openrlhf/trainer.py:run_openrlhf_training({}, output_dir="./checkpoints/openrlhf")`, which drives `openrlhf.cli.train_ppo_ray --advantage_estimator group_norm`; `_run_trl` generates a resumable GRPO training script through `trl_integrations.trainer.generate_trl_train_script` into a temporary directory and executes it; `_run_verl` imports `verl.config` and `verl.trainer` directly; and `_run_skyrl` requires a `SKYRL_CHECKOUT` environment variable and emits `uv run --extra gpu --extra tinker -m skyrl.tinker.api` (source: platform_local/unified/launcher.py). For the `local` and `colab` backends the launcher runs `dispatch_framework()` in-process, a path the code comments identify as the one that breaks the Colab entry's self-recursion — an implementation detail worth recording because it indicates that the backend and framework dimensions are not perfectly orthogonal in practice, and the launcher carries framework-specific knowledge precisely at the points where they intersect.

Fairness across frameworks is addressed by configuration auditing rather than by assertion. The `platform_hybrid/experiments/framework_config_dumps/` supplement ships one exact hyperparameter dump per framework for the canonical Qwen3-8B + GSM8K matched-configuration run, and the accompanying README recomputes the field partition from those dumps: of 44 tracked leaf fields, 25 are identical across all four frameworks (LoRA rank, alpha, target modules and dropout; GRPO group size, rollouts per group and max new tokens; sampling temperature, top-p, top-k and do-sample; optimizer name, learning rate, weight decay, betas and epsilon; reward type; and the task, seed and source-git-sha metadata), while 11 are Tinker-managed — serialised as `null  # managed_by_tinker` in the Tinker dump — covering `kl.beta`, `kl.surrogate`, importance-sampling granularity, reference-model offload and recompute, tokens per optimizer step, reward broadcast precision, gradient checkpointing, micro-batch size, gradient accumulation, and tensor-parallel size (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/framework_config_dumps/README.md). The same README records two disclaimers that matter more than the identical count: `model` and `model_sha256` are *not* identical, because the Tinker run uses `Qwen/Qwen3-8B-Base` while TRL, veRL and OpenRLHF use the instruction-tuned `Qwen/Qwen3-8B`, and `model_sha256` is a placeholder rather than a verified checksum. Remaining fields are framework-specific and documented rather than harmonised — KL beta is 0.04 for TRL and veRL against 0.02 for OpenRLHF, importance-sampling granularity is token-level for TRL and veRL against sequence-level for OpenRLHF, and reference-model placement differs across all four (`on_device`, `managed`, `separate_process`, and `sharded_ray`). The appendix's original "byte-identical" framing is retracted on the strength of this partition; the honest statement is that a majority of tracked fields are matched, a known Base-versus-Instruct confound survives, and eleven fields are not under user control at the Tinker API level at all.

## 5.3 The Tinker GRPO Loop

The reference implementation of the training loop lives in `platform_tinker/tinkerrl/grpo.py` and is exposed through `grpo_cli.py`. The loop follows the standard four-stage shape recorded in the Phase-1 report under "The Tinker GRPO Loop": sample a group of `G` completions per prompt, compute group-relative advantages, form the loss, and take an optimiser step (source: platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex). Advantages are normalised within the group to mean zero and unit standard deviation, and the loss is the clipped surrogate with a KL penalty to a frozen reference policy,

`L(θ) = -E[min(ρ A, clip(ρ, 1-ε, 1+ε) A)] + β KL(π_θ ‖ π_ref)`

where `ρ` is the importance ratio and `ε` the clipping range. The implementation uses the Tinker SDK's `ServiceClient` with `create_lora_training_client`, `forward_backward_custom` with `loss_type_input="logprobs"` so that per-token log-probabilities flow into a custom loss, `optim_step` with `AdamParams`, and `sample()` for rollouts; resumption uses `create_training_client_from_state_with_optimizer` (source: platform_tinker/tinkerrl/grpo_cli.py).

The single most consequential property of this loop for the project's research questions is the degenerate case. When the group's reward standard deviation `σ_g` is zero, every advantage `A_i` is zero, the surrogate term vanishes, and only the KL term survives — so the step is a pure pull toward the reference policy with no learning signal at all. This is the mechanism behind the zero-variance-fraction diagnostic that runs through the project; it is recorded in the Phase-1 report as the observation that motivated the collapse analysis, and it is the reason `zero_loss` and `zero_reward` are first-class outputs of the training CLI rather than derived quantities. Zero reward is counted as an average reward equal to zero; zero loss is counted as a step loss below `1e-6` (source: platform_tinker/tinkerrl/grpo_cli.py).

The CLI exposes eight presets — `tooluse_synth`, `tooluse_baseline`, `tooluse_heldout`, `tooluse_xlam`, `gsm8k`, `math100`, `pavlov_xlam`, and `pavlov_portfolio` — each resolving a dataset factory and a reward function through a `DATASET_FACTORIES` registry and a `REWARD_MAP` (`ExactMathReward` for GSM8K, `StrictToolCallReward` for `pavlov_xlam`, `PavlovNonXLAMReward` for `pavlov_portfolio`). Tracking and campaign gates are enforced before a run starts, through `config.validate_tracking()` and `config.validate_campaign_gate()`, and the CLI refuses to launch without a `TINKER_API_KEY`. Each seed's result is printed as a structured record containing `run_id`, `sampler`, `avg_first5`, `avg_last10`, `peak_reward`, `zero_loss`, `zero_reward`, `heldout_reward`, and `reward_trace`, so that a run's evidence is emitted at the point of execution rather than reconstructed afterwards. The eight presets replaced an earlier collection of standalone `grpo_*.py` scripts, with `legacy_main` retained to translate the old flag surface (source: platform_tinker/tinkerrl/grpo_cli.py).

## 5.4 The Parallel Runner and a Real Logging Defect

Sweep execution is handled by `parallel_sweep.py` and `campaign.py`, with the runner's design shaped directly by a defect found during development. The defect is documented in the group-size sweep findings and is worth quoting in full, because it is the clearest illustration in the project of why logging infrastructure deserves the same scrutiny as training code:

> The threaded runner trains all G in parallel correctly, BUT `wandb` uses a thread-unsafe global `run` — with 4 threads calling `wandb.init(reinit=True)`, the G8/G16 **summary** writes failed ("Run is finished") after another thread finished its run. Per-step `wandb.log` mostly survived; the summary dict didn't. **Fix for next parallel batch:** isolate each run in its own PROCESS (multiprocessing / subprocess per config), not a thread — or use `wandb-core` service mode with explicit per-run objects. Training itself was unaffected (all step data is in the run log).
> (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/p3_groupsize/FINDINGS.md)

Two aspects of this record are instructive. First, the failure was silent with respect to training: gradients were correct, steps completed, and per-step metrics were written. Only the run summary — the aggregated dictionary from which a results table would be built — was lost, which is precisely the class of failure that corrupts downstream reporting while leaving the primary artefact intact. Second, the remedy adopted was structural rather than retry-based: isolating each run in its own process removes the shared mutable global entirely, whereas retrying the summary write would have left the race in place. The finding also notes the alternative of `wandb-core` service mode with explicit per-run objects, which would preserve in-process execution at the cost of depending on service-mode semantics that the project chose not to rely on.

The same findings file documents the group-size sweep that exposed the defect, and its numbers are reported here with the qualifications the source attaches to them. The sweep ran `G ∈ {2, 4, 8, 16}` on Qwen3.5-4B for six steps on GSM8K at seed 0 under the W&B group `p3-groupsize-sweep`, with total token counts of 9,663, 19,964, 34,084, and 68,055 respectively. At `G = 2` the run showed a 50% batch-level collapse with a `+0.000` gain; at `G = 4` a 17% collapse with a `+0.125` gain. The source flags the `G = 4` gain explicitly as supported by one example at n = 8 on a single seed and therefore *suggestive rather than proven*, and records that the independent verification of the sweep returned the verdict SUSPECT (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/p3_groupsize/FINDINGS.md). These are not results this thesis claims; they are the observations that motivated the group-size investigation and that the later controlled work supersedes, and they are reported in the register in which they were recorded.

## 5.5 The Colab White-Box Path (Per-Layer Gradient Profile)

The per-layer gradient profile — numbered P1 in the Phase-1 series, and distinct from the P1 cross-scale study of Chapter 6 — asks a question the managed Tinker backend cannot answer. Per-layer gradient norms require access to the model's internals during the backward pass, and the managed service exposes no such interface. The implementation therefore leaves the managed path entirely and runs on a Colab L4 GPU in `platform_hybrid/experiments/openings/p1_layer_profile.py`, using Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct with LoRA adapters on the q/k/v/o projections, five GRPO-style advantage-weighted steps, and per-layer LoRA gradient norms recorded at each step (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/p1_layerfreeze/FINDINGS.md; platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex). The white-box path is thus a deliberate architectural exception: the project accepts the loss of managed-scale training in exchange for gradient visibility, and confines the exception to a question that only gradient visibility can address.

The first-pass result appeared strong. Across 28 layers, the top-k layer set identified at step 1 overlapped the final top-k set perfectly, with a measured overlap of 1.0; the top 25% of layers accounted for a 0.476 share of gradient mass; layer 0 carried a gradient norm of 63 against 6–17 elsewhere, and a secondary mid-late band appeared at layers 17–23. A scaled re-test disciplined this result. The re-run used `p1_layer_profile_scaled.py` on Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, with 24 real GSM8K problems, ten steps, `G = 4`, 36 layers, and seeds {0, 1}. The step-1-to-final top-k overlap fell to 0.111 — chance level — and the top-25% concentration measured 0.39 with a standard deviation of 0.013. The source's verdict is unambiguous: the original predictability finding "was an artifact of the toy 1.5B/hardcoded-arithmetic setup" (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/p1_layerfreeze/FINDINGS.md). A later four-seed pass at the same configuration (seeds {0, 1, 2, 3}) supersedes the two-seed figure and gives a mean overlap of 0.083 ± 0.048, again approximately chance, with concentration 0.39 (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/p1_layerfreeze/scaled_4seed_result.json).

A companion freeze test asked whether the layer profile could be exploited: if a small band of layers dominates the gradient, freezing the rest should preserve the benefit at lower cost. It did not. The full 28-layer configuration trained 2.18M parameters and produced a mean held-out gain of `+0.0625` (seed 0 `+0.125`, seed 1 0.0). Freezing to a hot band comprising layer 0 plus layers 12–27 — 17 layers, 1.32M parameters, or 0.61 times the full count — produced `+0.000`. The source concludes that "aggressive freezing loses the benefit… The freeze lever does not work at this scale", and records that the comparison is underpowered at n = 8 held-out examples over 2 seeds (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/p1_layerfreeze/FREEZE_FINDINGS.md). Both P1 outcomes are negatives: the apparent structure did not survive scaling, and the intervention it suggested did not survive testing. They are reported as findings rather than suppressed, because a layer-importance claim that collapses under a scaled re-test is exactly the kind of result the project's evidence discipline exists to surface.

## 5.6 Software Modules

The module inventory recorded in the Phase-1 report remains the accurate map of the codebase. The unified launcher, `platform_local/unified/launcher.py`, maps one configuration onto a chosen backend; `platform_tinker/tinkerrl/grpo.py` is the reference GRPO loop against the managed backend; `platform_local/trl_integrations/` and the `verl/` integration hold the per-framework adapters, with `platform_modal/openrlhf/` and `platform_hybrid/skyrl/` completing the roster, and `platform_tinker/atropos/` carrying the Atropos integration described above. Analysis code is separated from training code by intent: `p2_collapse_analysis.py` recomputes zero-variance fraction and collapse statistics from stored reward tensors, so the diagnostic is reproducible from evidence rather than from a live run; `p1_layer_profile.py` implements the white-box gradient probe; `parallel_sweep.py` and `campaign.py` drive sweeps; and `p8_detector.py` is an exploratory method-trace classifier evaluated by row-wise cross-validated AUROC (source: platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex).

The p8 detector deserves an explicit caveat in an implementation chapter. It is an exploratory artefact evaluated by row-wise cross-validation, and its AUROC is a measure of separability on the traces it was given, not a benchmark result against an external suite; it should not be read alongside the E-lane scores reported later in this thesis. Beyond these modules, the `platform_local/unified/` package carries `canonical.py` for the canonical configuration and `peft_utils.py` for adapter handling, and the backends package implements one module per backend (`local.py`, `modal.py`, `colab.py`, `vast.py`, `gcp.py`, `hfspaces.py`) behind the common `base.py` interface (source: platform_local/unified/).

## 5.7 The E1–E14 Evaluation Harness

Its `LANE_SPECS` table binds each lane to a replacement scope, a task count, and the runner or adapter that produces its evidence: E2 FrontierSWE at 17 tasks (`pavlov_frontier_swe_eval_adapter.py`, `frontier_swe_eval.py`), E3 SDAB at 80, E4 BankerToolBench at 100 (`run_harbor_via_tinker_bridge.py`), E5 APEX-Agents at 480, E7 BinaryAudit at 46 (`modal_non_e11_readiness.py`), E9 MLE-bench at 75 (`e9_mle_bench_streaming.py`), E12 AppBench at 6, and lanes E6, E8, E10, E13 and E14 with their respective runners. Reporting a mean reward of 0.0 with its mechanism attached, rather than reporting a low score and moving on, is the standard this chapter's evidence rules are meant to enforce.

## 5.8 The Bridge and Sampler Path

Runners reach a Tinker-trained model through `zvf-program/flagship/modal_tinker_openai_bridge.py`, a Modal-deployed OpenAI-compatible bridge whose own header states that "the deployed bridge is infrastructure, not a benchmark result. Its default paid-inference budget is zero" (source: zvf-program/flagship/modal_tinker_openai_bridge.py). The bridge declares app name `pavlov-tinker-openai-bridge`, model alias `pavlov-qwen36-tinker` over model id `Qwen/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B`, and pins its sampler at `tinker://cf0ad8c1-1f1b-5ff3-8bd7-2a0bf232657b:train:0/sampler_weights/seed809_final`, with the Hugging Face side pinned by commit `64444133c55d88c3f1bf0df8a2f5d7ac646125c8`. Runtime limits are 16,384 maximum completion tokens against a 65,536-token model context, with an 8,192-token context prefix reserved; `DEFAULT_MAX_USD` is `Decimal("0.00")`, so every paid path must be opted into explicitly at launch. The pinned dependency set is `fastapi` 0.116.1, `huggingface-hub` 1.27.0, `tinker` 0.30.0, `transformers` 5.5.4, and `wandb` 0.21.0 (source: zvf-program/flagship/modal_tinker_openai_bridge.py).

The architectural point of the bridge is that it converts a Tinker sampler into an OpenAI-compatible endpoint. A harness written against the OpenAI chat-completions API — which most agentic benchmark harnesses are — can therefore be pointed at a Tinker-trained actor without modification, and the E4 rerun described above used exactly this path in its `/v1/responses` form. The same pattern appears in the actor-side configuration of the E1 recovery wave, where the actor is addressed as an OpenAI-compatible chat-completions service at `http://127.0.0.1:18015/v1` (container port 8000) under model name `pavlov-public-portfolio-bf16`, with `max_tokens` 8192, temperature 0, seed 809, top-p 0.95, and `chat_template_kwargs {"enable_thinking": false}` (source: zvf-program/e1_wave10/README.md). The bridge and the local actor endpoint are two deployments of the same interface contract, and keeping that contract stable across deployment modes is what allows one harness to serve both.

## 5.9 Recovery Re-implementations under `zvf-program/`

In the final phase of the project an execution-source loss — deletion of the `.codex-run` directory — removed the finish-era recovery and build code, confined in scope to the E1 wave10 controller, the E9 restored dependencies, and the E6 validator, while the replacement-lane native runners survived under `zvf-program/flagship/` (source: outputs/E1_E14_FINAL_RESULTS_2026-09-19.md). The project's lost-source policy is that sealed requests, validation receipts and test logs pin any faithful re-implementation. Four such re-implementations are worth describing, because together they show what the evidence discipline looks like when it is expressed as code rather than as convention. None of them produced a benchmark number; all four are launch-ready or design-complete artefacts whose value is that their safety properties are testable offline.

**E1 wave10.** The wave-10 recovery package rebuilds the sealed v6 controller for sixteen tasks — nine `google__gson`, five `hashicorp__terraform`, and two `immutable-js` — selected from `resource_request_v6.json` at SHA256 `7adc6e2616f61bc7ef4d08533422a5df83ed1253f2fea507ce831890b2bb972a`. Four safety properties are enforced in code. Selection is sealed-only: the driver may act only on the hash and the embedded sixteen-task id list. Lane evidence stays behind a boundary, in that requests are built only by `public_swe_multilingual_native.build_actor_request` and receipts use the `collect_attempts` schema. The v6 process-group contract is enforced with unconditional finalisation on both success and failure, with the result JSON read only after `killpg(pgid, 0)` reports ESRCH, and with success requiring four conditions jointly — worker exit zero, direct worker reaped, the original group absent, and the controller's remaining deadline not yet expired. Finally, failures are never fabricated: `GENERATION_FAILED` is reserved for genuinely completed responses that lack a valid unified diff, receipts are write-once and immutable, and there is no automatic retry, resume, or replay. The package carries an eight-item pre-dispatch checklist, and the reservation records `public0919-e1-actor11` at 8 USD over 3600 seconds and `public0919-e1-runtime11` at 8 USD over 10800 seconds with status `RESERVED_NOT_DISPATCHED` (source: zvf-program/e1_wave10/README.md). The re-implementation verifies 10 of 10 targeted regressions offline, matching the lost suite's record: `test_process_group.py` at 4/4 and `test_cleanup_regressions.py` at 6/6 (source: outputs/E1_E14_FINAL_RESULTS_2026-09-19.md).

Read together, these four artefacts are the implementation answer to a question the results chapters raise: how a project maintains evidential integrity when its execution environment is partly lost and partly unreachable. The answer taken here is to re-derive the controllers from the sealed requests and the surviving test records, to keep every re-implementation offline-testable, and to make the no-retry, no-replay, hash-pinned, fail-closed properties explicit in code so that they can be demonstrated rather than asserted. The cost of that discipline is visible: four controllers, three of them complete and one a deliberate stub, produced no new benchmark numbers in this phase. That is the intended trade.
