# 6. Results I: Core GRPO Studies (P1–P4)

This chapter reports the four core GRPO studies that make up the first half of the paper series: the cross-library and cross-scale behaviour of GRPO training reward (P1), the Zero-Variance Fraction as a diagnostic of signal starvation (P2), the effect of group size on trainability (P3), and the length-bias and held-out-generalisation behaviour of GRPO against its Dr. GRPO variant (P4). Each study is reported here in the form the evidence supports, which in every case is more conservative than the hypothesis that motivated it. Three of the four pillars return a negative or an explicitly bounded result, and the fourth returns a diagnostic whose measured association with the outcome it is meant to predict is weaker than the literature would suggest.

## 6.1 Scope, Sources and Evidence Boundary

The four pillars share one methodological commitment, which is the organising principle of the whole project: every comparative claim is made with the entire training stack held fixed except the one factor under test. Where that was not possible, the result is reported as an association rather than a contrast, and the confound is named. The reader should treat this chapter as the primary result surface for P1–P4; the corresponding LaTeX sources are the canonical artefacts, and every figure quoted below is taken from one of them.

Each study draws on a different evidence base, and the differences matter. P1 pools 70+ runs across seven reinforcement-learning libraries and five model families spanning 0.6B to 1T parameters, but the frontier-scale anchors within that pool are single-seed runs executed through a managed training and inference API, so the cross-scale analysis is descriptive and its uncertainty is wide (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p1_abstract.tex). P2's measured magnitudes come from a controlled five-seed panel plus a matched-budget rollout budget check; a portion of its comparative table is a declared simulation projection rather than a set of independent library deployments, and that distinction is preserved below (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/zvf.tex). P3's measured sweep covers G ∈ {2, 4, 8, 16}; every G = 32 and above number in the pillar is a joint-fit reconstruction, not a trained arm (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p3_intro.tex). P4's controlled comparison is sixteen runs across two algorithms and two tasks; its roster-level audit is drawn from a separate, larger pool that mixes models, tasks and libraries (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex).

Throughout this chapter, "statistically indistinguishable" means that a formal equivalence or difference test failed to separate the arms at the stated level, not that the arms were demonstrated to be equivalent. Where an equivalence test was run and returned a null, that is stated explicitly.

## 6.2 P1 — Cross-Library and Cross-Scale GRPO Behaviour

The question P1 sets out to answer is whether the benefit of GRPO post-training scales with model size. The framing is deliberate: the hypothesis of interest is a Chinchilla-style power law in parameter count N, and the paper's declared claim boundary is that it reports a descriptive limits and identifiability audit rather than a positive scaling law (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p1_abstract.tex). The short answer is that no reliable scaling law is recovered from the reward curves, and that the evidence base is too small and too heterogeneous to falsify a plausible scaling hypothesis even if one were present.

### 6.2.1 The anchor pool and the fitted saturation forms

The fitted anchor pool spans roughly 2.4 orders of magnitude in parameter count. This is a subset of the full 0.6B–1T roster: for the cross-scale fits, only anchors with a usable per-step reward trace and a known parameter count were retained. The frontier-scale anchors — Qwen3-235B-MoE, DeepSeek-V3.1 at the 685B class, Kimi-K2 at 1T, and Nemotron-120B — were obtained through the Tinker managed training and inference API in a single-seed, descriptive-only regime, since full base weights are not released for those anchors and the project does not own frontier hardware (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p1_abstract.tex).

Each trace was fitted with a saturating-exponential form R(t) = R_max(1 − e^{−λt}), with the saturation rate λ constrained by the optimiser to the interval [0, 10]. The fitted results are the first place the negative result becomes visible. Across the five well-characterised frontier-class anchors, the ordinary-least-squares trend slope on the raw reward trace, β_OLS, satisfies |β_OLS| ≤ 0.0037 in every case — that is, the traces are flat to within a few thousandths of a reward unit per step (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/scaling_laws.tex). Qwen3.5-4B fits a mean reward of 0.817 with a peak of 1.000 and λ pinned at the bound of 10.0; Qwen3-8B fits 0.285 with a peak of 0.625; Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct fits 0.869 with a peak of 1.000; DeepSeek-V3.1 fits 0.844 with a peak of 1.000. Four of these five traces have λ pinned at the fit boundary — that is, the optimiser wanted a faster saturation than the constraint permitted — and the paper's verdict is that for those traces no single saturation exponent is identifiable at all (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p1_abstract.tex).

The exception is Nemotron-120B, which fits R_max = 0.182 with λ = 0.99, well inside the bound, and which the pipeline classifies as a collapse rather than a saturation. The autopsy table records a peak at step 3, a fitted R̂_max of 0.875, a late-phase mean R̄_late of 0.208, a drop of −0.667 from peak to late phase, a zero-reward step fraction of 0.55, and a post-peak OLS slope of −0.0036 per step (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/scaling_laws.tex). Across the rest of the pool, the zero-reward step fraction is at most 0.067. The Nemotron run is therefore better described as a distinct collapse phase than as a point on a smooth trend, and this is the reason the pillar's headline object is a local, stack-conditioned taxonomy of endpoint ceilings rather than a functional form in N (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p1_abstract.tex).

### 6.2.2 The cross-scale test and its nulls

Three cross-scale statistics were computed against log₁₀ N: the slope of the mean reward, the slope of the peak reward, and the slope of the reward variance. None is distinguishable from zero. The mean-reward slope is −0.024 per decade with a bootstrap mean of −0.117 and a 95% interval of [−0.796, +0.313], Spearman ρ = −0.07; the peak-reward slope is +0.037 with a bootstrap mean of −0.010 and an interval of [−0.623, +0.197], ρ = +0.22; the variance slope is −0.002 with an interval of [−0.063, +0.027], ρ = −0.14 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/scaling_laws.tex). A residual-resampling bootstrap with 1,000 draws applied to the Nemotron anchor returns R̄_max = 0.310, λ̄ = 3.26, P(λ ≥ 9.5) = 0.277 and a t₈₀ of 17.87 steps with an interval of [0.16, 161.4] — an interval wide enough to contain almost any behavioural claim. A 70/30 anchor holdout returned slope estimates of +0.0000, +0.0000, −0.0000, −0.0000 and +0.0016 across the five holdout configurations (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/scaling_laws.tex). Fitting a power law over the full twelve-anchor set from 4B to 1T returns p ≥ 0.46 for every exponent tested.

Two model-selection checks were run to see whether the saturation story was at least the best available description of the traces. Fitting constant, linear and saturating forms to each trace, AIC and BIC select the constant model on every single trace, with a ΔAIC of at most 2 over the runner-up (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/scaling_laws.tex). That is, a flat line describes these reward curves at least as well as any saturating curve does. A changepoint test with a permutation null estimated change locations τ̂ of 27, 28, 5, 15 and 18 steps across five traces, with permutation p-values of 0.493, 0.789, 0.742, 0.909 and 0.137; none is significant. An effective-plateau-horizon analysis at tolerance ε = 0.20 found that only two of the five traces — Qwen3.5-4B and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct — reach a horizon at all, both at 27 steps, because the others never satisfy the tolerance (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/scaling_laws.tex).

A compute-adjusted proxy, which folds an estimated token budget into the scale axis, returns a slope of +482 ± 490 per decade. The point estimate is positive and the interval is an order of magnitude wider than the estimate, so the honest reading is that the compute axis does not rescue the scaling story either.

### 6.2.3 Where the geometric template fails

The three-phase learning template used throughout the project — a slow start, a period of rapid improvement, and a plateau, with collapse as a degenerate tail — was tested directly against the anchor pool as an internal consistency check. Only 2 of the 12 anchors match the template's geometric prediction (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p1_abstract.tex). The template itself survives as a descriptive vocabulary: it names the failure modes the pipeline detects, and the collapse criteria (peak ≥ 0.4, late mean below 40% of peak, zero-reward fraction ≥ 0.30) are used in the pillar's flagging throughout. What does not survive is the claim that these traces realise the template as a progression.

One further contrast deserves note because it is our own measurement rather than a synthesis. Under a strictly matched same-stack comparison of PPO and GRPO, the paired algorithm difference is −0.002 with p = 0.374 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/frontier_synthesis_scaling.tex). This is the cleanest de-confound the project has: the entire training stack held fixed except the estimator, and the two estimators are statistically indistinguishable. It is reported here rather than in P2 or P3 because it is the algorithmic counterpart of the P1 null — the cross-scale story is not the only axis on which GRPO's advantage fails to separate from its alternatives.

### 6.2.4 A withdrawn architectural contrast

An earlier version of this analysis reported a MoE-versus-dense gap across six pairs of anchors, with a mean gap of +0.338 and a permutation p = 0.023. That claim has been withdrawn. The defect is architectural: the Nemotron-120B anchor is a LatentMoE, not a dense model, so the original six-versus-six allocation misclassified it. With the classification corrected, the same test returns a one-sided permutation p ≈ 0.178. A leave-Nemotron-out recomputation returns a gap of +0.278 with p ≈ 0.049, and this too is withdrawn, because removing the misclassified anchor from one side of the comparison does not repair the design (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/scaling_laws.tex; provenance in autoresearch/deli-neurips-tmlr-260802/state/nemotron_architecture_resolution.md). The withdrawal is recorded here because the project's convention is that retired claims remain visible with the reason for retirement attached.

A related cross-anchor statistic is retained, with its own caveat. Across the twelve anchors, seven have |Δ_1T| ≤ 0.2, where Δ_1T measures how far the anchor's endpoint deviates from the three-phase template's terminal prediction; the largest deviation is Qwen3.5-27B at +0.562 and the largest negative is Nemotron at −0.50 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/scaling_laws.tex). A Cohen's κ analysis of the categorical assignments is also reported in the source. The honest summary is that categorical capability structure carries more signal than log₁₀ N, which is what the paper's abstract says and what the numbers support.

## 6.3 P2 — The Zero-Variance Fraction

The second pillar asks a mechanistic question: when GRPO stops learning, is the cause visible in the reward statistics of the groups it samples? The diagnostic proposed is the Zero-Variance Fraction.

### 6.3.1 Definition and measurement

For a prompt x sampled from the training distribution, GRPO emits G completions and scores each with a verifier. Centring those rewards within the group produces the advantage signal. If all G completions receive the same reward — all correct, or all incorrect — every centred reward is zero and the group contributes no contrastive signal to that step's update. The Zero-Variance Fraction is the fraction of sampled groups in a step for which this occurs, and the gradient utilisation is defined as GU = 1 − ZVF (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/zvf.tex).

One clarification from the source is load-bearing and is repeated here because it is easy to misread: a zero-variance group does not mean the total gradient is zero. The policy-gradient term can still carry weight through other paths. What ZVF measures is the fraction of groups contributing zero centred reward contrast (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/zvf.tex). The strongest statement the pillar supports is about signal starvation in the group-relative term, not about a vanished update.

The statistic is validated by recomputation. Three stored GSM8K group tensors were re-read from disk and their ZVF recomputed from the raw per-group rewards; the recomputed values are 0.130, 0.190 and 0.155, pooled 0.1583, matching the reported values exactly (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p2_abstract.tex). This is the mechanical check that the quantity in the tables is the quantity the code computes.

### 6.3.2 Measured magnitudes across libraries

The headline measurement is a nine-row panel run at K = 8 with five seeds and a 100-step rollout budget, spanning vanilla GRPO and eight named variance-mitigation methods. Vanilla GRPO records a mean ZVF of 0.481 against a mean last-10 accuracy of 0.379, with zero of five seeds collapsing, zero of five drifting, and five of five plateauing. The remaining rows are: AERO 0.220 ZVF with 0.399 accuracy; CPPO 0.295 with 0.392; NGRPO 0.300 with 0.387; SCAFGRPO 0.317 with 0.409; MCGRPO 0.146 with 0.322; GIFT 0.114 with 0.326; AREAL 0.121 with 0.328; and ES 0.114 with 0.316. The four lowest-ZVF rows each record one of five seeds drifting and four of five plateauing (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/zvf.tex).

The variance-mitigation rows require a caveat that the source states explicitly and that this chapter preserves: they are a declared simulation projection used for sensitivity analysis, and they "should be read as synthetic, not as independent real-library deployments" (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/zvf.tex). The vanilla GRPO row and the collapse/drift/plateau counts are the measured content. Anyone citing the nine-row ordering as evidence that a named method mitigates failure would be citing a projection.

Two further measured results sharpen the picture. First, across 60 measured runs spanning the nine variance-mitigation methods and the group-size sweep, the late-phase ZVF of GRPO runs sits in the range 0.87–0.97, while early-phase ZVF for variance-mitigation GRPO and for the largest group size sits in the range 0.04–0.13; smaller group sizes start higher, between 0.25 and 0.61. The lag-1 autocorrelation of the ZVF trace is approximately 0.94, meaning the quantity moves smoothly rather than jittering, which is what makes it usable as a warning indicator at all (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p2_results_intro.tex).

Second, and more directly relevant to practice, the diagnostic rises *after* the reward plateau rather than before it. Measuring first-passage times, ZVF crosses its warning threshold only 1 to 3 steps ahead of the plateau. The recommended operating rule from the pillar is therefore not predictive but custodial: report ZVF alongside mean reward, and treat a rise of more than 0.3 sustained over ten consecutive rollout steps as a warning (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/zvf.tex). The project reports this limitation against its own proposed diagnostic rather than in a footnote.

### 6.3.3 The all-correct wall and the mastery–incapacity aliasing

The mechanism behind large ZVF values on easy tasks is a saturation effect that the project calls the all-correct wall. A matched-budget panel was run at 2,560 rollouts per arm — G = 2 over 160 steps against G = 16 over 20 steps — on Qwen3-8B with GSM8K, LoRA rank 4 and batch size 8. The late G = 2 arms reach a mean training reward of approximately 1.0 with ZVF between 0.75 and 1.0; the G = 16 arms hold ZVF at or below 0.25 at the same rollout budget (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/zvf.tex). The reading is that when the policy solves the task, nearly every group becomes all-correct, nearly every centred reward is zero, and the estimator loses its contrast even though the reward signal is at its maximum. Signal starvation in this regime is driven by ease, not by difficulty — which is the opposite of the intuition that motivates variance-mitigation work.

This produces the pillar's most important structural caveat. ZVF is unsigned and symmetric: an all-wrong group (K_x = 0) and an all-correct group (K_x = G) score identically. The statistic therefore cannot distinguish mastery from incapacity, and this aliasing is the explanation the project gives for the weak association between ZVF and final held-out accuracy (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p2_conclusion.tex). Under an i.i.d.-Bernoulli model the closed form is ZVF(p_x, G) = p_x^G + (1 − p_x)^G, and the derivative with respect to log G at p = ½ is −ln 2. The project attributes this closed form and the aliasing analysis to a frontier-model synthesis exchange rather than to its own derivation — the document carrying it is explicitly headed as reasoning contributions and proposed theorems from ChatGPT Pro Extended and Gemini Deep Think, "NOT experiments this paper ran" (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/frontier_synthesis_zvf.tex) — and this chapter marks that provenance accordingly. What the project itself measured is the empirical ZVF against the closed form: at G = 2 the mean measured ZVF is 0.838 against a theory value of 0.964 (residual −0.126); at G = 4, 0.764 against 0.954 (−0.191); at G = 8, 0.691 against 0.923 (−0.232); at G = 16, 0.631 against 0.704 (−0.073), with gradient utilisation rising across the same range from 0.162 to 0.369 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/group_size.tex). The measured ZVF consistently falls below the i.i.d. prediction, which is consistent with the reward distribution being less extreme than independent Bernoulli draws with a common rate.

### 6.3.4 Prognostic value

The pillar's central claim is not that ZVF is large but that it is informative, and here the results split cleanly. Pooling the measured runs, the naive correlation between mean ZVF and last-10 accuracy is r = 0.22 with a confidence interval of [−0.29, +0.94] over n = 23 — not significant. Residualising ZVF against mean reward raises the correlation to r = +0.80 with an interval of [+0.54, +1.00] (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/zvf.tex). In other words, the raw diagnostic is confounded by the reward level, and the residualised version is strong; the pillar reports both and does not claim the raw version works.

Against catastrophic collapse, ZVF performs much better than it does against graded outcome. Mean ZVF correlates with catastrophic collapse at Spearman ρ = 0.56 and point-biserial 0.62, but with final held-out accuracy at only ρ ≈ 0.27 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p2_results_intro.tex). A three-channel risk index built on the diagnostic — magnitude, critical slowing down via a rolling lag-1 autocorrelation with window 15, and drift slope, blended 0.3/0.5/0.2 with a max-fusion variant — was evaluated over a 52-row cross-experiment panel comprising 45 variance-mitigation seeds, 2 tool-use collapse anchors and 5 scaling-law collapse anchors, with 11 failures and 41 safe runs. Cross-experiment AUROCs are 0.663 for magnitude, 0.758 for critical slowing down, 0.579 for drift, 0.763 for the blended risk index and 0.929 for the max-fusion index; within-methods, the same values fall to 0.073, 0.335, 0.293, 0.396 and 0.805 respectively (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/zvf.tex). The gap between the cross-experiment and within-method columns is a warning about the index's transportability: most of the cross-experiment discrimination is coming from the fact that collapse clusters by experimental family, not from a within-family predictive signal. The declared thresholds are a risk above 0.55 to treat as failure-positive, below 0.30 as safe, and the interval between as requiring investigation.

The pillar's own conclusion names three load-bearing limitations: the diagnostic is mechanically coupled to reward sparsity, group size and baseline accuracy; its strong association holds only for catastrophic collapse and not for the graded held-out outcome; and the unsigned statistic aliases mastery with incapacity (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p2_conclusion.tex). A proposed replacement, the pairwise contrast density, is introduced in the synthesis document with a target association of ρ ≳ 0.45; the project states that this bar is not yet met by the replacements and that the head-to-head protocol remains future work (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/frontier_synthesis_zvf.tex).

## 6.4 P3 — Group Size and Contrast Density

The third pillar studies G, which plays two roles simultaneously in GRPO: it is a variance-reduction knob, like a Monte-Carlo baseline, and it is a preference-density knob, since centring rewards within a group induces implicit pairwise contrasts — nonzero only for mixed-reward groups. Practitioners often treat larger G as strictly better by analogy to variance reduction, which conflates the two roles and ignores cost. The pillar's conclusion is that the measured evidence does not justify a group-size optimum and that the dependence of trainability on G is non-monotone.

### 6.4.1 The measured sweep

The primary sweep measures G ∈ {2, 4, 8, 16} on a near-ceiling arithmetic task with Qwen2.5-0.5B, three seeds and 40 steps. Held-out accuracy traces an inverted U with its apex at G = 8: 0.990 ± 0.003, against 0.982 ± 0.004 at G = 2 and 0.978 ± 0.006 at G = 16. At n = 3 seeds these three arms are statistically indistinguishable (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/group_size.tex). A separate check run at B = 4000 with seed 20260702 records G = 2 and G = 16 at 0.982 and 0.978 with a difference of +0.003 and an interval of [−0.008, +0.015]; G = 4 and G = 8 add +0.010 and +0.012 respectively, and G = 4 versus G = 32 was not measured on that sweep (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/group_size.tex).

The retention figures are the cleanest way to state the same result. G = 2 retains 100.3% of G = 16 (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/group_size_effect_dpo_check.tsv). A later unified synthesis at the same configuration reports retention ratios and signal-to-noise ratios with intervals: G = 2 retains 1.0000 [0.998, 1.002] with SNR 1.46 [1.22, 1.81]; G = 4, 0.9941 [0.992, 0.997] with SNR 1.76 [1.46, 2.23] and a TOST p < 10⁻⁹; G = 8, 0.9960 [0.989, 1.004] with SNR 1.94 [1.58, 2.54] and p < 10⁻⁹; G = 16, 1.0068 [0.998, 1.013] with SNR 2.16 [1.72, 2.86] and p < 10⁻⁴. All four retention ratios sit above the Wu et al. 97.6% reference bar (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/group_size_iter27.tex).

The signal-to-noise ratio grows sublinearly with G: it reaches about 52% of the √G ideal, and about 48% above a flat baseline. Reward itself is statistically flat across the sweep, in the range 0.971–0.984 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/group_size.tex). The marginal ZVF gain from G = 8 to G = 16 is 0.6906 → 0.6312, a fall of 0.059, which is smaller than the G = 2 → G = 4 fall of 0.8380 → 0.7635, a fall of 0.075 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/group_size.tex). Diminishing returns on the contrast axis are visible in the measured data, which is what the reconstruction later formalises.

The pillar's own framing of this regime is important and is stated in its abstract: near the ceiling, variance is compressed, so the finding is "no detectable G effect under saturation", not evidence of equivalence on harder, unsaturated tasks. That is, the flat retention curve is a property of an easy task, and the pillar explicitly declines to generalise it. The measured equivalence tests extend only to G = 16; there is no directly trained, matched-budget G = 32 cell in the primary sweep (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p3_abstract.tex). The retention-versus-budget axis is where the picture changes: R(1M) = 0.976, R(4M) = 0.833, R(16M) = 0.750 and R(64M) = 0.727, fitted by R(T) = 0.729 + 0.247·e^{−T/5.76M} with R² = 0.948 and an asymptote of 0.729 [0.712, 0.745]. The Wu reference line and this reconstructed curve cross at T = 1M and diverge by 25 percentage points at T = 64M (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/group_size_iter27.tex). The pillar's operative conclusion is that the present evidence supports G = 2 on the measured easy near-ceiling sweep and makes no universal recommendation for hard tasks.

### 6.4.2 The same-stack four-axis audit and the reconstructed budgets

The strongest de-confound for P3 is a four-axis factorization audit of the same training stack. On the algorithm axis, the residual effect is small: η² = 0.023, with a paired GRPO−PPO difference of −0.002 and permutation p = 0.62. On the group-size axis, the effect dominates: η² = 0.54 for terminal accuracy and 0.63 for gradient norm, with Cohen's d = −1.47 for the G = 2 → 16 comparison (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/berkeley/unpacking_dpo_ppo_factorization.json, as summarised in platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p3_conclusion.tex). The substantive reading is that G is a real lever and the algorithm label is nearly inert on this stack.

Two of the pillar's four headline contrasts survive a Miller-style error-bars audit and two do not. The gradient-utilisation ratio at T = 1M has a confidence interval of [4.16, 4.82] and is graded precise. The negative retention-versus-training-token slope has an interval of [−0.237, −0.038] and is precise. The SNR-versus-√G slope has an interval of [+0.148, +0.583], which contains the ideal value of +0.500 and is therefore graded NULL. The native Wu-style equivalence claim that G = 2 ≈ G = 16 is also graded NULL (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/berkeley/adding_error_bars_summary.json, as summarised in platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p3_conclusion.tex). This chapter reports those verdicts as they stand: two of the four P3 headlines are precise and two are not, and the pillar's own summary is that the data do not justify a universal optimum.

Everything at or above G = 32 in the pillar comes from an explicitly reconstructed token-budget grid, retained only as a hypothesis generator. The reconstruction table gives, for G = 2 versus G = 32, a T = 1M comparison of 0.41 against 0.42 with a difference of −0.01, a 97.6% retention ratio and a "generalizes" verdict of yes; at T = 4M, 0.55 against 0.66 with −0.11, 83.3% and no; at T = 16M, 0.63 against 0.84 with −0.21, 75.0% and no; at T = 64M, 0.64 against 0.88 with −0.24, 72.7% and no (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/group_size.tex). The source labels these as conditional reconstruction intervals, not fresh-run confidence intervals. The T = 64M accuracy row across G = 4, 8, 16, 32 and 64 reads 0.64, 0.80, 0.85, 0.88 and 0.87 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/group_size.tex) — the apex moves with budget, which is precisely the pattern that makes a universal G optimum incoherent, and precisely the pattern the project declines to claim as measured. A fit of the critical-budget form |Δ(T)| = a(1 − e^{−T/τ}) gives â = 0.30 and τ̂ ≈ 12.1M tokens, with a 5% critical budget of 2.21M tokens and an interval of [1.29M, 4.77M] (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/group_size_iter23.tex). A difficulty-gated adaptive rule derived from this analysis reports a mean selected group size of 7.0 and a projected 56.2% rollout saving against always using G = 16, with 5 of 20 cells under-predicting by more than 5 percentage points (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p3_conclusion.tex). These are projections, and the chapter marks them as such.

### 6.4.3 The curriculum and matched-token null results

Two interventions that P3's design space naturally suggests were run and both returned nulls. They are reported here rather than elsewhere because they are group-size interventions, and because their negative results are load-bearing for the chapter's overall argument.

The first is a difficulty curriculum that filters collapsed groups during training. The intervention works as designed on its own terms: it drives the zero-gradient waste fraction from 0.50 to 0.00, eliminating wasted rollouts completely. It does so at approximately 4.81 times the sampling cost, and its held-out gain is identical to the baseline's — both arms move from 0.80 to 0.85 on the held-out set, a gain of +0.05 in each case (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/curriculum_opening/FINDINGS.md, as summarised in platform_hybrid/paper/sections/robustness_smallscale_nulls.tex). At a matched 30k-token budget the curriculum ties the baseline at +0.028 against +0.028, and at five times the cost it loses. The verdict recorded is Null. An important provenance note attaches to this result: an independent verification pass conducted on 6 July 2026 flagged the curriculum claim, along with a single-seed G = 4 "win" of +0.125, as SUSPECT and overclaimed, before multi-seed re-testing confirmed both as noise (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/curriculum_opening/FINDINGS.md). The group-size sweet-spot result is itself a null for the same reason — the +0.125 advantage at G = 4 rested on a single held-out example and did not survive multi-seed re-testing, and G = 2 under-trains with a 50% batch collapse and a +0.0 gain (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/robustness_smallscale_nulls.tex).

The second null family concerns loss formulations intended to remove the length bias. On the held-out metric, the standard sum formulation is the best of the three tested: +0.125 against +0.000 for mean-normalisation and +0.042 for the surprise formulation, with the length change under the standard arm recorded as Δlen = −16 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/robustness_smallscale_nulls.tex). The verdict is Null. A fourth null, on a step-1 predictive layer-freeze, is reported in the same table because it was proposed as a cheap way to stabilise group-relative training: it achieves a per-step overlap of 1.0 on a 1.5B hardcoded-arithmetic toy, but only 0.11 — approximately chance, on both seeds — at 3B on real GSM8K, while the concentration statistic still holds at approximately 0.39. Verdict: Null, and a reversal of its own toy-scale positive under scaling (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/robustness_smallscale_nulls.tex).

The pattern across all four nulls is consistent and worth stating plainly: the interventions that target the mechanism the diagnostic identifies do not improve held-out outcomes at matched cost. That is a negative result about the diagnostic's prescriptive power as much as about the interventions themselves, and it is one of the reasons P2 is reported as a monitoring tool rather than an optimisation objective.

## 6.5 P4 — Length Bias and Held-Out Generalisation

The fourth pillar tests the most widely repeated specific criticism of GRPO: that it rewards verbosity, inflating completion length in a way that Dr. GRPO corrects. The pillar's headline is a scoped null, and the scope is the point.

### 6.5.1 The controlled null

Sixteen controlled runs were analysed: ten on an arithmetic task with Qwen2.5-0.5B over 40 steps, and six on GSM8K chain-of-thought with Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct over 30 steps, split evenly between GRPO and Dr. GRPO. At these 30–40-step horizons, neither algorithm exhibits the verbosity-trap signature on either task; every one of the sixteen runs records a trap flag of zero (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex).

The summary correlations are as follows. On arithmetic, the rank correlation between training step and completion length is −0.405 ± 0.176 for GRPO and −0.307 ± 0.181 for Dr. GRPO; on GSM8K-CoT it is −0.622 ± 0.301 and −0.355 ± 0.255. The step-versus-reward correlations are +0.709 ± 0.082 and +0.753 ± 0.074 on arithmetic, and +0.046 ± 0.162 and +0.025 ± 0.091 on GSM8K-CoT. The length-versus-reward correlations are −0.419 ± 0.155 and −0.293 ± 0.182 on arithmetic, and −0.501 ± 0.258 and −0.618 ± 0.118 on GSM8K-CoT. Mean reward over the last ten steps is 0.988 for both algorithms on arithmetic, and 0.265 for GRPO against 0.252 for Dr. GRPO on GSM8K-CoT (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex). The direction of the length trend is negative everywhere: all sixteen length slopes are negative, with the arithmetic GRPO slope at −0.029 tokens per step and the GSM8K GRPO slope at −0.481, and the count of runs with a positive horizon is zero (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex). On the harder task, GRPO's final-ten-step mean completion length is 184.4 tokens against Dr. GRPO's 189.3 — a difference of +2.7% in the direction opposite to the critique (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex).

The pillar's abstract states the scope of this null explicitly. The claim is bounded by the 200-token cap used in that regime, and the summary sentence is that "the verbosity-trap signature is absent in this regime" (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_abstract.tex). The pillar is not claiming that GRPO never inflates length; it is claiming that within a capped, short-horizon regime it does not, and it names the length-confounded regime in which a Dr. GRPO advantage should appear as future work (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_results_intro.tex).

### 6.5.2 The roster-level verbosity-trap audit

Set against the controlled null is a descriptive audit over the whole experiment roster, and here the picture differs. The audit defines four statistics per trace with at least five steps: an instability index σ(r_tail)/|μ(r_tail)| over the final 50% of training; a monotonicity score, the fraction of consecutive step-pairs with strictly improving reward; a regression severity, the largest normalised drop from peak; and a binary verbosity-trap flag, set when the peak occurs before 65% of training and the terminal reward falls below 90% of peak (source: platform_hybrid/paper/archive/absorbed/U01_main_compendium/main.tex).

On that definition, the eleven GRPO runs in the roster have a mean instability index of 0.267 ± 0.335, against 0.785 ± 0.297 for the seventeen PPO-labelled runs. Four of the eleven GRPO runs — 36% — exhibit the verbosity-trap pattern, including both Qwen3-8B runs and the Nemotron-120B run. The Nemotron run records the highest instability index in the pool at 1.194, with a regression severity of 1.0 and a terminal-to-peak reward ratio of 0.57. The two DeepSeek-V3.1 GRPO runs, by contrast, show markedly lower instability, with a mean of 0.143 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/archive/absorbed/U01_main_compendium/main.tex; the same rates appear in platform_tinker/reports/combined/combined_capstone.tex, where the trap rates are given as GRPO 36% and PPO 71%).

Two caveats on that comparison must travel with it. First, the PPO pool is dominated by classic-RL library runs (SB3, CleanRL, Tianshou) that failed to train at all, with last-10 rewards below 5%; a near-zero reward trajectory mechanically produces a high instability index. Restricted to LLM-native PPO, the PPO Llama instability is 0.105, lower than GRPO Llama's 0.228, and the source therefore explicitly declines to claim that GRPO is more or less stable than PPO at the algorithmic level (source: platform_hybrid/paper/archive/absorbed/U01_main_compendium/main.tex; platform_tinker/reports/combined/combined_capstone.tex). Second, the audit's reward metric is the in-training reward, not a held-out one, and the audit pool is single-seed per configuration: each Tinker configuration was run once and is reported descriptively. The project's only matched base-versus-RL held-out control is the five-seed Qwen3-8B comparison discussed below.

The task specification for this chapter attributes to P4 the claim that Dr. GRPO is the only algorithm without the verbosity-trap pattern. That specific claim could not be located in any source read for this chapter. What the sources state is that 36% of the audit's GRPO runs carry the flag; the audit does not report a per-algorithm flag rate for Dr. GRPO, and the controlled sixteen-run comparison reports zero flags for both algorithms. The finding recorded here is therefore the sourced one: the trap pattern is present in a minority of roster GRPO runs and absent in the controlled comparison for both algorithms, and the project makes no claim that Dr. GRPO is uniquely free of it. The narrowest related statement the sources do support is a length-drift one — that Dr. GRPO is the only algorithm whose length keeps drifting upward after the initial compression phase (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias_iter48.tex) — which is a different statistic and is not the verbosity-trap flag.

### 6.5.3 Elevated analyses

Because the controlled 30–40-step null is a null, the pillar runs a sequence of progressively more sensitive analyses to establish whether the trap is present but invisible at that horizon. Four of these deserve report.

A sliding-window trap-onset analysis with window W = 10 relaxes the flag from "peak before 65% of training" to "local peak followed by decay within a window", and finds traps in 2 of 10 arithmetic runs (20%), 4 of 6 GSM8K runs (67%) and 6 of 16 runs overall (37.5%) — every one of which is a local re-expansion rather than a terminal divergence (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex). This is the analysis that most nearly matches the roster-level trap rate, and the pillar treats it as the honest upper bound on trap prevalence in the controlled data.

A paired bootstrap on the Dr. GRPO minus GRPO difference in rank correlations gives, on arithmetic, Δρ(step, len) = +0.098 with an interval of [+0.073, +0.119] — excluding zero, so Dr. GRPO reliably compresses length relative to GRPO on the easy task; Δρ(step, reward) = +0.043 with an interval of [−0.024, +0.112], which is not significant; and Δρ(len, reward) = +0.126 with an interval of [+0.045, +0.201], again excluding zero. On GSM8K the same three quantities are +0.267 [+0.164, +0.419], −0.020 [−0.069, +0.065] and −0.117 [−0.265, +0.015] (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex). The reading is that Dr. GRPO's length attenuation is real but task-dependent, and that on the harder task it deepens rather than weakens the length–reward coupling.

A decile-binned coupling analysis on GSM8K shows the mechanism directly: completions in the first length decile, spanning 153–180 tokens, earn a mean reward of 0.351, while completions in the eighth decile, spanning 194–195 tokens, earn 0.233. Longer completions are rewarded less, which is the opposite of the verbosity-trap prediction (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex). A 100-step cross-validation on the arithmetic metrics file returns ρ(step, len) = +0.41 and ρ(step, reward) = +0.27, but the absolute length range across that run is 0.08 tokens against a cap of 5 tokens — a positive correlation over a range too small to be a length effect (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex).

The length–ZVF coupling analysis at iteration 12 is the most mechanistically interesting of the elevated results, because its sign flips between tasks. On arithmetic, ρ(len, zvf) is −0.449 [−0.584, −0.324] for GRPO and −0.291 [−0.428, −0.139] for Dr. GRPO; on GSM8K it is +0.286 [+0.231, +0.348] and +0.187 [+0.148, +0.225]. The Dr. GRPO minus GRPO difference is +0.158 [+0.075, +0.239] on arithmetic and −0.099 [−0.132, −0.041] on GSM8K (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex). Conditioning ZVF on length bins makes the arithmetic structure concrete: the shortest bin has ZVF 0.92 and the longest 0.62, a drop of −0.30 [−0.34, −0.26], whereas on GSM8K the corresponding values are 0.30 and 0.41, a rise of +0.11 [+0.10, +0.13] (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex). On the easy task, shorter completions are more often all-same-reward; on the hard task, longer ones are.

The mechanism analysis at iteration 20 fits a regression of length on reward and finds that Dr. GRPO reduces the slope magnitude on arithmetic — −3.65 ± 0.08 against −3.22 ± 0.15, a difference of −0.43 [−0.58, −0.28] with p = 0.036; the token-surprisal component moves by −1.11 [−1.59, −0.63] with p = 0.047; the rank correlation moves by −0.10 [−0.18, −0.03] with p = 0.087; and the lag-1 autocorrelation is essentially unchanged, +0.01 with p = 0.92. On GSM8K the same differences reverse sign, with an OLS difference of +4.41 whose interval [−4.58, 19.84] is far too wide to interpret, p = 0.57 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex). A mediation analysis attributes 42% of the arithmetic effect to ZVF under GRPO and 55% under Dr. GRPO, against 28% and 12% on GSM8K.

The drift analysis at iteration 24 closes the sequence. Windowed ρ drift on arithmetic is +0.326 for GRPO with p = 0.035, decomposing into an early −0.330 and a late −0.003, against +0.187 for Dr. GRPO with p = 0.399; on GSM8K the corresponding values are +0.019 with p = 0.905 and −0.084 with p = 0.769 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex). The sign-flip analysis of late windows gives fractions of 0.527 (p = 0.468), 0.382 (p = 0.562), 0.048 (p = 0.011) and 0.000 (p < 0.001) across the four algorithm-task cells. The one fully significant cell is the GSM8K Dr. GRPO arm, in which not a single late window flips sign.

### 6.5.4 Held-out generalisation and the scale extension

The pillar's held-out results are the part of the chapter closest to a conventional capability claim, and they are reported with corresponding care. The only matched base-versus-RL held-out control in the project is a five-seed comparison on Qwen3-8B-Instruct, which moves from 82.0% to 83.3% with p = 0.26 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/archive/absorbed/U01_main_compendium/main.tex). Stated as a mean improvement over the pre-RL checkpoint, the gain is +0.013 with a paired p = 0.256 at n = 5 — small and not statistically significant, and a matched pre-RL control for the Qwen3-8B-Base checkpoint is not available (sources: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_results_intro.tex`, `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_intro.tex`). The honest summary is that on this near-ceiling configuration, GRPO does not produce a held-out improvement that can be separated from noise at the available seed budget, and that GRPO and Dr. GRPO produce held-out gains that are indistinguishable from each other at that budget (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_abstract.tex).

The lower-accuracy configuration behaves differently and more conventionally. On Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct with GSM8K-CoT, both algorithms do produce significant pre-to-post held-out gains: +6.2 percentage points with McNemar exact p = 1.1 × 10⁻⁴, and +5.0 percentage points with p = 7.6 × 10⁻⁴ (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_results_intro.tex). This is the project's clearest evidence that the pipeline trains something real; it is reported alongside the near-ceiling null precisely because the contrast between the two configurations is what the pillar's bounded-null interpretation rests on. Across the same comparison, the pooled mean completion length drifts from 194.4 to 183.5 tokens, that is downward (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_abstract.tex).

A scale extension tests whether the null survives when the cap is lifted. Qwen3-8B with LoRA rank 4 was run on GSM8K for 30 steps at G = 8 and batch size 4, three seeds per algorithm, with the completion cap raised fivefold to 1,024 tokens. All six arms compress: GRPO moves 1004 → 905, 981 → 944 and 996 → 900 tokens, and Dr. GRPO moves 999 → 931, 972 → 902 and 1000 → 878 — declines of between 3.8% and 12.2% in every arm. Late ZVF is 0.45, 0.70 and 0.72 for Dr. GRPO against 0.47, 0.47 and 0.55 for GRPO, and step-0 completions are approximately 98% of the cap (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex). The step-0 saturation is the caveat that limits this extension: with completions already at 98% of the ceiling at the first step, the run starts truncated, and length compression under a binding cap is not the same phenomenon as length control in an uncapped regime. The pillar reports the extension as consistent with the null, not as an independent confirmation of it.

Finally, the P4-facing headline in the Miller-style error-bars audit is the iteration-136 arithmetic late-training-efficiency contrast: paired, n = 5, Cohen's d = +2.68, one-sided parametric p = 0.031 and two-sided permutation p = 0.063. It is graded DECISIVE (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/berkeley/adding_error_bars_summary.json; platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_conclusion.tex). The audit's three NULL verdicts fall on Pillar-1 and Pillar-3 headlines rather than on this one. The pillar also records its own limitations plainly: a single-benchmark held-out evaluation, a single-seed top-10-checkpoint frontier comparison, weak length identifiability, and marginal paired tests (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_conclusion.tex).

## 6.6 Cross-Pillar Observations and Limitations

Three observations span the four pillars and are worth stating together, because the chapter's overall contribution is better captured by their pattern than by any single number.

First, the project's strongest results are its de-confounds, and its de-confounds mostly return nulls. The matched same-stack PPO-versus-GRPO contrast returns a paired difference of −0.002 with p = 0.374; the four-axis factorization audit attributes η² = 0.023 to the algorithm axis and η² = 0.54 to the group-size axis; the controlled length-bias comparison returns zero trap flags across sixteen runs. In each case the entire stack was held fixed except the one factor under test, and in each case the expected algorithmic advantage failed to appear. The single exception is the group-size axis, which is a hyperparameter rather than an algorithm choice. A reader looking for the project's central empirical claim should take this one: on the stacks measured here, the algorithm label matters much less than the sampling configuration.

Second, the diagnostics and interventions are less prescriptive than their motivating literature suggests, and the project says so. ZVF measures signal starvation reliably but predicts catastrophic collapse well (ρ = 0.56) and graded held-out accuracy poorly (ρ ≈ 0.27), because it is unsigned and therefore cannot distinguish mastery from incapacity. The four interventions built on it — the difficulty curriculum, the group-size sweet spot, the length-bias loss formulations and the step-1 layer freeze — all return nulls at matched cost, and two of them (the curriculum and the G = 4 win) were flagged as SUSPECT by an independent verification pass before multi-seed re-testing reclassified them as noise. Reporting an intervention as a null after an independent reviewer flagged it as overclaimed is, in this project's convention, part of the result.

Third, the single-seed regime is the dominant shared limitation. The cross-scale anchors are single-seed and descriptive. The roster-level verbosity-trap audit is single-seed per configuration, and its in-training reward metric is not a held-out one. Only the P3 sweep, the P4 controlled comparison, the five-seed Qwen3-8B held-out control and the P4 scale extension operate at n ≥ 3, and only the Qwen3-8B control is a matched base-versus-RL held-out comparison. Every headline in this chapter is therefore stated at the strength that its own evidence base supports, which is in several cases weaker than the strength at which it would need to be stated to generalise beyond the named stack. The reconstructions (G = 32 and above in P3, the T = 64M grid, the difficulty-gated saving projection) are held at hypothesis-generator status for the same reason: they are joint-fit extrapolations, not trained arms, and the project declines to let them carry a recommendation.

Taken together, the four pillars describe a training method whose measured behaviour is dominated by signal availability and sampling configuration rather than by algorithmic identity, and whose most widely repeated specific failure mode — length inflation — is absent in the regimes where it was tested directly and present in only a minority of the roster runs where it was measured descriptively. That is a narrower and more cautious set of conclusions than the paper series set out to establish, and the chapter reports it as such.
