### Summary
This manuscript presents a literature survey of 60+ papers (2023–2025) on reinforcement learning (RL) for agentic AI workflows, alongside an empirical study of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) using LoRA/QLoRA across small-to-medium open-weight models (0.5B to 30B-MoE). The survey organizes the literature into a 19-category taxonomy across six domains and compares PPO, DPO, and GRPO. The experimental section proposes a five-experiment ablation suite across SWE-bench, WebArena, and OSWorld, and reports initial GRPO training runs on GSM8K, MATH, HumanEval, and synthetic/Glaive tool-calling tasks using the Tinker/Atropos framework.

Despite the timely topic, the manuscript is not ready for publication at a top ML venue. It reads as an unedited university capstone report (complete with student ID numbers, a missing logo placeholder, and internal team progress notes). It suffers from a completely broken bibliography (`[? ]` throughout the text), severe internal contradictions in the reported experimental metrics, flawed statistical analysis (treating training-batch rollout rewards as evaluation metrics and misapplying statistical tests), and a major disconnect between the proposed agentic experimental framework (§5.1–5.2) and the actual experiments run (§5.3–5.7).

---

### Strengths
- **Timely and Comprehensive Scope:** The survey captures the recent transition from PPO/DPO to critic-free group-normalized methods (GRPO) for reasoning and tool use, organizing methods into a structured 19-category taxonomy (Ch. 2–4).
- **Candid Reporting of Negative and Null Results:** In Section 5.6, the authors honestly report that held-out GSM8K improvements (+1.3pp, $p=0.26$) and HumanEval subset improvements (+8pp, $p=0.53$) are statistically indistinguishable from baseline, and they explicitly identify model-family/scale confounds in Section 5.5.
- **Concrete Taxonomies of Failure Modes:** The categorization of six RL training instabilities (Table 6.1: Echo Trap, Entropy Collapse, Reward Instability, Gradient Spikes, Tool Hallucination, Credit Assignment Failure) and four agent safety risks (p. 48–49) provides useful practical structure.

### Weaknesses
- **Broken Bibliography and Unresolved Citations:** Every in-text citation renders as `[? ]` or `? ]` (e.g., p. 2, 16, 20, 24, 29, 37, 52), and the References section is entirely missing from the document. Only partial BibTeX keys appear in Appendix E (p. 99).
- **Severe Internal Contradictions in Experimental Results:** The manuscript contradicts itself across tables and sections regarding the exact same experiments. For example, Qwen3-8B on GSM8K is reported as reaching `100%` final accuracy in Table 5.2 (p. 68), `0.9437` in Table 5.3 (p. 70), `0.275–0.350` in Table 5.10 (p. 78), and `83.3%` held-out in Finding 5 (p. 75). Furthermore, the reward definition shifts between a sum in $[0, 2.0]$ (p. 67) and a binary pass rate $k/128 \in [0, 1]$ (p. 69).
- **Conflation of Training Rollout Rewards with Evaluation Accuracy:** The training curves (Fig. 5.2, 5.3) and statistical tests (§5.4) analyze on-policy *training batch* rewards over 50 steps rather than held-out evaluation benchmarks. Claiming "100% GSM8K accuracy" (p. 53, 68) based on stochastic training batches is misleading, as the authors later admit in Finding 5 (p. 75) that held-out accuracy only moved from 82.0% to 83.3% ($p=0.26$).
- **Disconnect Between Proposed Design and Actual Execution:** Sections 5.1–5.2 propose a 5-experiment, 57-run suite comparing PPO, DPO, and GRPO on SWE-bench, WebArena, and OSWorld (Table 5.1). Section 5.3 abruptly switches to 8 GRPO-only runs on static math/coding datasets (GSM8K, MATH, HumanEval) and single/multi-turn tool-call formatting. Neither PPO, DPO (except a 5-example toy run in §5.7), SWE-bench, WebArena, nor OSWorld were actually evaluated.
- **Flawed Statistical Methodology:** Section 5.4 applies i.i.d. binomial Z-tests to grouped completions (16 rollouts per prompt for 8 prompts are correlated within prompts, violating independence), runs a Chow structural-break test *post-hoc* on the visually selected maximum breakpoint without multiple-testing correction, and applies Mann-Whitney U tests to autocorrelated time-series steps before and after the selected breakpoint.
- **Unedited Course Report Artifacts and Informal Tone:** The manuscript retains university course metadata (p. 1), first-name attributions in section headers (p. 67, 76), multiple contradicory GitHub URLs (p. 66, 74, 81), a fake/illustrative plot presented as a numbered figure in the experiments chapter (Fig. 5.1, p. 62), and colloquial phrasing ("stuff", "screws up", "chokes").

- `[CRITICAL] p. 70: "Each step provides 128 independent binary outcomes, making the large-sample z-test appropriate."` - The 128 outcomes per step are generated by sampling $G=16$ completions for each of 8 prompts (p. 69). Completions for the same prompt share the same problem difficulty and are not independent binary trials; treating $n=128$ as i.i.d. artificially inflates the effective sample size and $z$-statistics. **Fix:** Compute variance and confidence intervals by clustering at the prompt level (across the 8 prompts per step) or evaluate on the standard held-out GSM8K test set ($N=1,319$ distinct problems).
- `[CRITICAL] p. 71: "We used an exhaustive Chow F-test to detect the step index at which the reward trajectory undergoes a structural change ... Step 25, F = 32.35, p < 10^-10"` - Running an exhaustive search over all possible breakpoints maximizes the $F$-statistic, which invalidates the standard $F$-distribution $p$-value (this requires the Andrews/Quandt supremum-Wald distribution). Similarly, Table 5.6 (p. 71) runs a Mann-Whitney U test comparing steps before and after the data-selected breakpoint on an autocorrelated time series, which guarantees an artificially tiny $p$-value ($1.3 \times 10^{-9}$). **Fix:** Use a proper structural change test for unknown breakpoints (e.g., `sup-F` / Bai-Perron test) and remove the post-hoc pre- vs. post-breakpoint Mann-Whitney U test.
