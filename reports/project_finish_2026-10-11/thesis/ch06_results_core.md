# 6. Results I: Core GRPO Studies (P1–P4)

This chapter reports the four core GRPO studies that make up the first half of the P1–P8 internal reports: the cross-library and cross-scale behaviour of GRPO training reward (P1), the Zero-Variance Fraction as a diagnostic of signal starvation (P2), the effect of group size on trainability (P3), and the length-bias and held-out-generalisation behaviour of GRPO against its Dr. GRPO variant (P4). Each study is reported here in the form the evidence supports, which in every case is more conservative than the hypothesis that motivated it. Three of the four pillars return a negative or an explicitly bounded result, and the fourth returns a diagnostic whose measured association with the outcome it is meant to predict is weaker than the literature would suggest.

## 6.1 Scope, Sources and Evidence Boundary

The four pillars share one methodological commitment, which is the organising principle of the whole project: each comparison names the intended contrast, the controls actually held fixed and any coupled changes that limit attribution. Where checkpoint, prompt exposure, sampling or budget differ, the result describes those executed configurations rather than an isolated algorithm effect. The reader should treat this chapter as the primary result surface for P1–P4; the corresponding historical LaTeX sources are preserved, while this chapter distinguishes their reported outputs from corrections recomputed from the named data and inspected analysis code.

Each study draws on a different evidence base, and the differences matter. P1 pools 70+ runs across seven reinforcement-learning libraries and five model families spanning 0.6B to 1T parameters, but the frontier-scale anchors within that pool are single-seed runs executed through a managed training and inference API, so the cross-scale analysis is descriptive and its uncertainty is wide (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p1_abstract.tex). P2's measured magnitudes come from a controlled five-seed panel plus a matched-budget rollout budget check; a portion of its comparative table is a declared simulation projection rather than a set of independent library deployments, and that distinction is preserved below (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/zvf.tex). P3's measured sweep covers G ∈ {2, 4, 8, 16}; every G = 32 and above number in the pillar is a joint-fit reconstruction, not a trained arm (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p3_intro.tex). P4's controlled comparison is sixteen runs across two algorithms and two tasks; its roster-level audit is drawn from a separate, larger pool that mixes models, tasks and libraries (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex).

The corpus-level motivation for the fixed-stack commitment (Chapter 4 §4.1) is a variance decomposition computed as four separate marginal one-way ANOVAs, each run against the same 42 experiments with valid performance observations: library/framework η² = 0.546, training algorithm η² = 0.558, model family η² = 0.471, model scale η² = 0.322 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/_shared_methods.tex). These four values sum to 1.897. A set of shares that sums above one is not a partition of a common variance budget, and on a corpus where the factors are collinear a marginal η² absorbs the variance of each factor's correlates, so the values show that framework and algorithm vary a great deal across this corpus and do not estimate either factor's independent contribution. The historical twenty-test multiple-comparison family that accompanies the decomposition silently includes single-seed rows whose Welch statistics are mathematically undefined, and reduces to a single surviving comparison under the restricted family; it is retained as a reproducibility audit trail and not as a corrected survival claim (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/_shared_methods.tex).

Throughout this chapter, "statistically indistinguishable" means that the stated difference test did not detect a difference at its declared level; it does not mean demonstrated equivalence. Equivalence is reported only when both one-sided tests reject differences outside a stated margin. Failure to establish equivalence and failure to detect a difference are separate outcomes, and can occur together.

## 6.2 P1 — Cross-Library and Cross-Scale GRPO Behaviour

**Estimand boundary.** P1 is a short-horizon reward-trace audit. Absolute training reward combines pretrained starting capability and adaptation, and does not estimate the benefit of post-training. A future gain estimand is $\Delta(N,B)=\mathrm{Acc}_{post}(N,B)-\mathrm{Acc}_{pre}(N)$. The historical zero-intercept saturation curve is a descriptive fit; a proposed alternative would include a starting level, $R(t)=R_0+(R_\infty-R_0)(1-e^{-\lambda t})$. Adding that parameter would not by itself establish identifiability in these short, heterogeneous traces or establish a scaling law.


The question P1 sets out to answer is whether the benefit of GRPO post-training scales with model size. The framing is deliberate: the hypothesis of interest is a Chinchilla-style power law in parameter count N, and the P1 report's declared claim boundary is that it reports a descriptive limits and identifiability audit rather than a positive scaling law (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p1_abstract.tex). The short answer is that no reliable scaling law is recovered from the reward curves, and that the evidence base is too small and too heterogeneous to falsify a plausible scaling hypothesis even if one were present.

### 6.2.1 The anchor pool and the fitted saturation forms

The fitted anchor pool spans roughly 2.4 orders of magnitude in parameter count. This is a subset of the full 0.6B–1T roster: for the cross-scale fits, only anchors with a usable per-step reward trace and a known parameter count were retained. Two properties of that pool limit what the fits can support, and both are stated here rather than left to the reader. First, the anchors are not measured on a common footing: five of the twelve are interrupted runs whose recorded values are means over three to five logged steps, against twenty to thirty for the remaining seven, so the dependent variable is a short-horizon snapshot for part of the pool. Second, scale is collinear with training recipe across these anchors rather than independent of it: the learning rate, LoRA rank and related settings change together and only at the scale boundary, so a fit through the pool cannot separate an absent scale effect from recipe changes offsetting scale changes. The null reported below is therefore a statement about this pool as configured, and not an estimate of how reward scales with parameter count in general. The frontier-scale anchors — Qwen3-235B-MoE, DeepSeek-V3.1 at the 685B class, Kimi-K2 at 1T, and Nemotron-120B — were obtained through the Tinker managed training and inference API in a single-seed, descriptive-only regime, because this project accessed them through the managed service rather than operating their full base weights on its own hardware (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p1_abstract.tex).

Each trace was fitted with a saturating-exponential form $R(t) = R_{\max}(1 - e^{-\lambda t})$, with the saturation rate λ constrained by the optimiser to the interval [0, 10]. The fitted results are the first place the negative result becomes visible. Across the five characterised anchors, the ordinary-least-squares trend slope on the raw reward trace, β_OLS, satisfies |β_OLS| ≤ 0.0037 in every case — that is, the traces are flat to within a few thousandths of a reward unit per step (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/scaling_laws.tex). Qwen3.5-4B fits a mean reward of 0.817 with a peak of 1.000 and λ pinned at the bound of 10.0; Qwen3-8B fits 0.285 with a peak of 0.625; Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct fits 0.869 with a peak of 1.000; DeepSeek-V3.1 fits 0.844 with a peak of 1.000. Four of these five traces have λ pinned at the fit boundary — that is, the optimiser wanted a faster saturation than the constraint permitted — and the P1 report's verdict is that for those traces no single saturation exponent is identifiable at all (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p1_abstract.tex).

The exception is Nemotron-120B, which fits $R_{\max} = 0.182$ with $\lambda = 0.99$, well inside the bound, and which the pipeline classifies as a collapse rather than a saturation. The autopsy table records a peak at step 3, a fitted R̂_max of 0.875, a late-phase mean R̄_late of 0.208, a drop of −0.667 from peak to late phase, a zero-reward step fraction of 0.55, and a post-peak OLS slope of −0.0036 per step (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/scaling_laws.tex). Across the rest of the pool, the zero-reward step fraction is at most 0.067. The Nemotron run is therefore better described as a distinct collapse phase than as a point on a smooth trend, and this is the reason the pillar's headline object is a local, stack-conditioned taxonomy of endpoint ceilings rather than a functional form in N (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p1_abstract.tex).

### 6.2.2 The cross-scale test and its nulls

Three cross-scale statistics were computed against log₁₀ N: the slope of the mean reward, the slope of the peak reward, and the slope of the reward variance. None is distinguishable from zero. The mean-reward slope is −0.024 per decade with a bootstrap mean of −0.117 and a 95% interval of [−0.796, +0.313], Spearman ρ = −0.07 (a later statistical audit, `results/berkeley/adding_error_bars_summary.json`, re-bootstraps the same anchors to a slope of −0.023 and [−0.796, +0.323]; neither interval excludes zero); the peak-reward slope is +0.037 with a bootstrap mean of −0.010 and an interval of [−0.623, +0.197], ρ = +0.22; the variance slope is −0.002 with an interval of [−0.063, +0.027], ρ = −0.14 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/scaling_laws.tex). A residual-resampling bootstrap with 1,000 draws applied to the Nemotron anchor returns R̄_max = 0.310, λ̄ = 3.26, P(λ ≥ 9.5) = 0.277 and a t₈₀ of 17.87 steps with an interval of [0.16, 161.4] — an interval wide enough to contain almost any behavioural claim. A 70/30 anchor holdout returned slope estimates of +0.0000, +0.0000, −0.0000, −0.0000 and +0.0016 across the five holdout configurations (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/scaling_laws.tex). Fitting a power law over the full twelve-anchor set from 4B to 1T returns p ≥ 0.46 for every exponent tested.

Two model-selection checks were run to see whether the saturation story was at least the best available description of the traces. Fitting constant, linear and saturating forms to each trace, AIC and BIC select the constant model on every single trace, with a ΔAIC of at most 2 over the runner-up (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/scaling_laws.tex). That is, a flat line describes these reward curves at least as well as any saturating curve does. A changepoint test with a permutation null estimated change locations τ̂ of 27, 28, 5, 15 and 18 steps across five traces, with permutation p-values of 0.493, 0.789, 0.742, 0.909 and 0.137; none is significant. An effective-plateau-horizon analysis at tolerance ε = 0.20 found that only two of the five traces — Qwen3.5-4B and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct — reach a horizon at all, both at 27 steps, because the others never satisfy the tolerance (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/scaling_laws.tex).

The source reports a compute-adjusted proxy slope of +482 ± 490 per decade. Its proxy is parameter count multiplied by estimated steps to saturation, $N\,t_{80}$; per-step token counts were unavailable, so it is not a measured token or FLOP budget. The ± term is described as a wide interval in the source but its precise construction is not specified in that paragraph. With four saturation-bound traces, the source regards the proxy as uninformative, and this thesis does not use it to make a compute-scaling claim (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/scaling_laws.tex, lines 306–318).

### 6.2.3 Where the geometric template fails

The three-phase learning template used throughout the project — a slow start, a period of rapid improvement, and a plateau, with collapse as a degenerate tail — was tested directly against the anchor pool as an internal consistency check. Only 2 of the 12 anchors match the template's geometric prediction (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p1_abstract.tex). The template itself survives as a descriptive vocabulary: it names the failure modes the pipeline detects, and the collapse criteria (peak ≥ 0.4, late mean below 40% of peak, zero-reward fraction ≥ 0.30) are used in the pillar's flagging throughout. What does not survive is the claim that these traces realise the template as a progression.

One further contrast deserves note because it is our own measurement rather than a synthesis. Under a same-stack comparison of PPO and GRPO on a saturated Qwen2.5-0.5B two-digit addition task, evaluation accuracy is 0.990 for GRPO and 0.992 for PPO, and the paired algorithm difference is −0.002 with p = 0.374 (paired t on n = 5 seeds, each seed's held-out accuracy on 200 items; source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/frontier_synthesis_scaling.tex; recomputed from platform_hybrid/experiments/results/samestack_ppo_grpo.json). The 200 evaluation items are fresh random draws from the training generator, not a disjoint split. The pairing is by seed only, not by item: each arm drew its evaluation set from the shared random stream after training, and because GRPO consumes 16 problem draws per step against PPO's 128, the two arms of a seed were evaluated on almost entirely different items. Replaying the stream for the five seeds, the GRPO and PPO evaluation sets share only 6, 6, 4, 7 and 3 of 200 items, and the evaluation items that had also been drawn for training number 12, 16, 13, 15 and 13 of 200 for GRPO against 90, 86, 80, 83 and 74 for PPO, roughly 7% against 40% (corrected; see Table E.1; replay of `platform_hybrid/experiments/modal/modal_samestack_ppo_grpo.py`). The paired difference is therefore a difference of per-seed accuracies on different item sets with unequal train–evaluation overlap, not an item-paired contrast. At a 0.98–1.00 ceiling this does not change the null reading, but it is a further reason the null cannot be read as equivalence. This is the project's closest de-confound: the runner, clipped surrogate and step budget are shared, but the arms also differ in prompts per step (16 prompts × 8 completions for GRPO against 128 distinct prompts for PPO; Table 4.2). The two estimators are statistically indistinguishable, and because both sit at the ceiling the null cannot separate no effect from no headroom. They are not shown to be equivalent. The five per-seed differences are −0.005, +0.005, −0.005, 0.000 and −0.005, each one item of 200, at a 0.98–1.00 accuracy ceiling. A paired TOST with a ±0.005 margin gives p = 0.104, so equivalence at that margin is not established, although it is at ±0.010 (p = 0.008). The exact sign-flip permutation p on the same five pairs is 0.625. It is reported here rather than in P2 or P3 because it is the algorithmic counterpart of the P1 null — the cross-scale story is not the only axis on which GRPO's advantage fails to separate from its alternatives. The project's historical effect-size table also carries a Qwen3-8B PPO-versus-GRPO row. It is marked a provenance conflict and not estimable, and its Welch test, recorded in the historical twenty-test family at p = 0.7605, is carried as a provenance record rather than as an estimate, so no statistic is quoted for it here (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/_shared_methods.tex; Chapter 1 §1.2).

### 6.2.4 A withdrawn architectural contrast

An earlier version of this analysis reported a MoE-versus-dense gap across six pairs of anchors, with a mean gap of +0.338 and a permutation p = 0.023. That claim has been withdrawn. The defect is architectural: the Nemotron-120B anchor is a LatentMoE, not a dense model, so the original six-versus-six allocation misclassified it. With the classification corrected, the same test returns a one-sided permutation p ≈ 0.178. A leave-Nemotron-out recomputation returns a gap of +0.278 with p ≈ 0.049, and this too is withdrawn, because removing the misclassified anchor from one side of the comparison does not repair the design (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/scaling_laws.tex; provenance in autoresearch/deli-neurips-tmlr-260802/state/nemotron_architecture_resolution.md). The withdrawal is recorded here because the project's convention is that retired claims remain visible with the reason for retirement attached.

A related cross-anchor statistic is retained, with its own caveat. Across the twelve anchors, seven have |Δ_1T| ≤ 0.2, where Δ_1T measures how far the anchor's endpoint deviates from the three-phase template's terminal prediction; the largest deviation is Qwen3.5-27B at +0.562 and the largest negative is Nemotron at −0.50 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/scaling_laws.tex). A Cohen's κ analysis of the categorical phase assignments is also reported in the source: across three labelling methods, the historical output records κ = +1.000 on the five-anchor set because the helper returns 1.0 when the denominator is zero. Both methods label every trace "plateau", so observed and chance agreement are each 1 and Cohen's κ = (1 − 1)/(1 − 1) is mathematically undefined; only raw agreement of 5/5 is supportable there. On the twelve-anchor set the reported heuristic–AIC κ is +0.625, while the changepoint comparisons lie between −0.029 and +0.077 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/scaling_laws.tex, Table `tab:scaling-iter17-kappa`). The five-anchor result therefore establishes agreement on a constant label, not validation of a phase classifier. Neither this agreement nor the cross-scale null identifies categorical capability structure as a better predictor than log₁₀ N; such a claim would require a common predictive comparison (source: platform_modal/scripts/scaling_law_iter17.py, lines 442–456).

## 6.3 P2 — The Zero-Variance Fraction

The second pillar asks a mechanistic question: when GRPO stops learning, is the cause visible in the reward statistics of the groups it samples? The diagnostic proposed is the Zero-Variance Fraction.

### 6.3.1 Definition and measurement

For a prompt x sampled from the training distribution, GRPO emits G completions and scores each with a verifier. Centring those rewards within the group produces the advantage signal. If all G completions receive the same reward — all correct, or all incorrect — every centred reward is zero and the group contributes no contrastive signal to that step's update. The exact-tie fraction records how often this occurs; the default sample-variance tolerance in §4.3 also counts near-ties. They agree for the binary rewards below. Gradient utilisation is defined descriptively as GU = 1 − ZVF (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/zvf.tex).

What a zero-variance group does to the update depends on the loss form. The source states the general case: in an objective with a KL penalty or auxiliary loss, a zero-variance group does not make the total gradient zero, and ZVF measures only the fraction of groups contributing zero centred reward contrast (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/zvf.tex). An exact-tie group contributes zero to a verified group-centred policy surrogate, but the full update can include KL, critic or other auxiliary losses and optimiser state. Table 4.2 contains both nonzero-KL configuration evidence and unknown managed settings; neither may be relabelled zero. Near-ties counted by the ε = 10⁻⁶ diagnostic need not have exactly zero policy gradients. ZVF therefore describes reward contrast, not measured useful compute or the whole update.

The statistic is validated by recomputation. Three stored GSM8K group tensors (Qwen3-8B, G = 8, sampling only, 200 problems per seed) were re-read from disk and their ZVF recomputed from the raw per-group rewards; the recomputed values are 0.130, 0.190 and 0.155, pooled 0.1583, matching the reported values exactly (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p2_abstract.tex). This is the mechanical check that the quantity in the tables is the quantity the code computes. The earlier ≤0.4% ε-sensitivity statement did not distinguish exact ties from thresholded variance and is replaced here by a reproducible sweep over these same 600 groups. With independent seeded δ ~ U(0,10⁻⁴) jitter, exact ties fall from 95/600 to 0/600. At sample-variance ε = 10⁻⁹, 71/600 groups remain below threshold; at ε = 10⁻⁸ through 10⁻², including the declared 10⁻⁶, all 95/600 remain. Unperturbed binary rewards retain 95/600 across this range. At G = 8, jitter alone has sample variance at most 2.86 × 10⁻⁹, so it cannot cross 10⁻⁶ in an originally tied group. PCD changes by +2.984 × 10⁻⁷. The example diagnoses exact-tie sensitivity, not failure of tolerance-based ZVF; it does not establish robustness to larger perturbations or other reward scales (sources: platform_modal/scripts/pcd_vs_zvf.py; platform_hybrid/experiments/results/pcd_vs_zvf_tolerance_analysis.json; platform_hybrid/experiments/results/pcd_vs_zvf_epsilon_sensitivity.tsv).

### 6.3.2 Source-reported magnitudes across libraries

The synthetic sensitivity panel has nine rows at K = 8 with five simulated seeds and a 100-step rollout budget, spanning vanilla GRPO and eight named variance-mitigation methods. Vanilla GRPO records a mean ZVF of 0.481 against a mean last-10 accuracy of 0.379, with zero of five seeds collapsing, zero of five drifting, and five of five plateauing. The remaining rows are: AERO 0.220 ZVF with 0.399 accuracy; CPPO 0.295 with 0.392; NGRPO 0.300 with 0.387; SCAFGRPO 0.317 with 0.409; MCGRPO 0.146 with 0.322; GIFT 0.114 with 0.326; AREAL 0.121 with 0.328; and ES 0.114 with 0.316. The four lowest-ZVF rows each record one of five seeds drifting and four of five plateauing (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/zvf.tex).

The variance-mitigation rows require a caveat that the source states explicitly and that this chapter preserves: they are a declared simulation projection used for sensitivity analysis, and they "should be read as synthetic, not as independent real-library deployments" (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/zvf.tex). The caveat covers the whole panel. The vanilla GRPO row and its collapse/drift/plateau counts come from the same dry-run file, `experiments/results/variance_mitigation.tsv`, as the other eight rows (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/zvf_by_library.tsv), and Chapter 2 §2.3 already records that GRPO baseline row as synthetic. None of the nine rows is a measured deployment. Anyone citing the nine-row ordering as evidence that a named method mitigates failure would be citing a projection.

Two further results sharpen the picture, and the first mixes measured and projected rows. First, across 60 runs the late-phase ZVF of GRPO runs sits in the range 0.87–0.97, while early-phase ZVF for variance-mitigation GRPO and for the largest group size sits in the range 0.04–0.13; smaller group sizes start higher, between 0.25 and 0.61. The lag-1 autocorrelation of the ZVF trace is approximately 0.94, describing smoothness in this mixed pool without establishing a measured early-warning capability (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p2_results_intro.tex). Of those 60 runs, 45 are the five-seed variance-mitigation projection rows above (corrected; see Table E.1). The measured part is the remainder, which includes the 12-run group-size sweep. The source does not report the ranges or the autocorrelation separately for the measured subset, so these figures describe a pool that is mostly synthetic.

Second, the historical source reports ZVF crossing a warning threshold only 1 to 3 steps ahead of the plateau. This short lead from the pooled source does not establish measured-only early-warning validity. Its proposed monitoring rule remains unvalidated: report ZVF alongside mean reward, and treat a rise of more than 0.3 sustained over ten consecutive rollout steps as a warning (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/zvf.tex). The project reports this limitation against its own proposed diagnostic rather than in a footnote.

### 6.3.3 The all-correct wall and the mastery–incapacity aliasing

The mechanism behind large ZVF values on easy tasks is a saturation effect that the project calls the all-correct wall. A matched-budget panel was run at 2,560 rollouts per arm — G = 2 over 160 steps against G = 16 over 20 steps — on Qwen3-8B with GSM8K, LoRA rank 4 and batch size 8. The late G = 2 arms reach a mean training reward of approximately 1.0 with ZVF between 0.75 and 1.0; the G = 16 arms hold ZVF at or below 0.25 at the same rollout budget (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/zvf.tex). The reading is that when the policy solves the task, nearly every group becomes all-correct, nearly every centred reward is zero, and the estimator loses its contrast even though the reward signal is at its maximum. Signal starvation in this regime is driven by ease, not by difficulty — which is the opposite of the intuition that motivates variance-mitigation work.

This produces the pillar's most important structural caveat. ZVF is unsigned and symmetric: an all-wrong group ($K_x = 0$) and an all-correct group ($K_x = G$) score identically. The statistic therefore cannot distinguish mastery from incapacity, and this aliasing is a theoretical limitation on its interpretation; the historical pooled correlation does not separately test the claimed held-out association (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p2_conclusion.tex). Under an i.i.d.-Bernoulli model the closed form is $\mathrm{ZVF}(p_x, G) = h_G(p_x) = p_x^G + (1-p_x)^G$, and it follows in one line. If the G rewards of a group are independent Bernoulli(p) draws, the group has zero variance exactly when all G rewards are equal. That happens when all are 1, with probability $p^G$, or all are 0, with probability $(1-p)^G$. The two events are disjoint, so $P(\text{zero variance}) = p^G + (1-p)^G$. At $p = \tfrac12$ this is $h_G = 2^{1-G}$, so $\ln h_G$ falls by $\ln 2$ for each unit increase in G. The project's source document for this closed form and the aliasing analysis is headed as reasoning contributions and proposed theorems from ChatGPT Pro Extended and Gemini Deep Think, "NOT experiments this paper ran" (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/frontier_synthesis_zvf.tex). That provenance is kept on record, and AI assistance is declared in the front matter, but the result does not rest on it: the derivation above is elementary and is the argument this thesis relies on. What the project itself measured is the empirical ZVF against the closed form, evaluated at the per-step training reward rate because ZVF is a training-batch quantity (corrected; see Table E.1). Recomputed from the per-step training rewards in `platform_hybrid/experiments/results/groupsize_zvf_sweep.json` (3 seeds × 40 steps per arm), the per-step-average null is 0.828, 0.709, 0.597 and 0.522. The measured mean ZVF is 0.838, 0.764, 0.691 and 0.631, so the residuals are +0.010, +0.055, +0.094 and +0.109: measured ZVF sits *above* the null at every G, which is compatible with heterogeneous prompt difficulty. Jensen's inequality bounds expected degeneracy at true latent success probabilities, not the sign of a finite-sample residual against an estimated plug-in baseline [@nie2026starvation]. Gradient utilisation rises across the same range from 0.162 to 0.369. The magnitude and interpretation of the gap depend on the baseline; averaging over heterogeneous prompt success probabilities is part of the formal null in §4.3. Evaluating the closed form at the pooled training rate understates the mean of the per-step plug-in predictions, because $h_G$ is convex: $h_G(\overline p_t) \le \overline{h_G(p_t)}$. The per-step-average plug-in baseline is $0.60$ at $G = 8$, rather than the $0.33$ obtained at the pooled mean. A held-out rate is a different quantity and has no corresponding ordering guarantee. Even the per-step baseline uses a batch-average estimate instead of the unknown per-prompt probabilities in §4.3, so prompt-difficulty heterogeneity and estimation error remain. Against the per-step-average baseline the measured ZVF at $G = 8$ ($0.69$) \emph{exceeds} the null by approximately $0.09$. The change from the pooled baseline to the per-step baseline is the Jensen effect; it does not by itself explain the remaining 0.09 residual. The source consequently "do[es] not read it as strong evidence that the independence null is a lower bound" (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/appendix_zvf_formalization.tex). This chapter uses only the per-step-average baseline. It is a weak consistency check on shape rather than evidence about dependence.

The formalisation's own summary of the same sweep, twelve runs and 480 logged steps, reports per-step observed ZVF correlating at r = 0.92 with the Bernoulli-null prediction $p^G + (1-p)^G$, with mean ZVF of 0.84, 0.76, 0.69 and 0.63 at G = 2, 4, 8 and 16 respectively (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/appendix_zvf_formalization.tex). That correlation is a shape check, not a validation: the null for each step is computed from the same step's mean reward, so a high r is partly mechanical. At larger scale the same instrument was run on Qwen3-8B against GSM8K test problems through the Tinker managed API at G = 8, temperature 1.0, over 200 problems and three seeds (sampling only, no training; these are the three stored tensors of §6.3.1), yielding a mean ZVF of 0.16 composed of 12.7% all-correct groups, 3.2% all-wrong groups and 84% mixed groups; the raw correlation with the mechanical null is approximately r = 0.93 with rank ρ ≈ 0.65, and the i.i.d. null over-predicts (approximately 0.28 against the observed 0.16) (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/appendix_zvf_formalization.tex). The direction of the deviation from the null is therefore opposite in the two runs: above the null on the 0.5B sweep, below it on this Qwen3-8B sampling run. The source does not state which baseline produced the 0.28, and neither deviation is read as evidence about dependence between completions.

A recomputation on the three stored Qwen3-8B tensors (600 groups, pooled success rate 0.694) separates the candidate explanations and does not reproduce the 0.28. At the pooled rate the homogeneous binomial null is $h_8(0.694) = 0.054$, against 0.158 observed, so the null *under*-predicts by a factor of about three. A beta-binomial fitted to the per-group success counts ($\alpha = 3.61$, $\beta = 1.62$, intraclass correlation 0.16) predicts ZVF 0.170, and the homogeneous binomial is rejected against it (likelihood-ratio statistic 282 on one degree of freedom). The fitted agreement shows that prompt-difficulty heterogeneity can explain the excess, without identifying it separately from conditional dependence or other latent variation: to a good approximation ZVF is the mean of $h_G(p_i)$ over prompts with heterogeneous success probabilities $p_i$, which is the Jensen argument above applied across prompts rather than across steps. A per-group plug-in residual of +0.1224 that appears in a P2 working note is withdrawn: it is non-negative by construction (the same sign-guaranteed quantity as the withdrawn "anti-herding" δ_div of §7.2, whose expectation is +0.105 under i.i.d. rollouts) and is not evidence of dependence (recomputation: 16 October 2026 audit of `results/tinker_gsm8k_zvf_s{42,123,456}.json`). Contemporaneous work studies the same quantity under other names — the Advantage Collapse Rate [@he2026acr] and gradient starvation in binary-reward GRPO [@nie2026starvation], the latter proving that heterogeneous prompt difficulty pushes the degeneracy rate above the i.i.d. Bernoulli prediction — so ZVF is not claimed here as a new diagnostic; the contribution is its recomputation from stored tensors and the reporting discipline around it.

### 6.3.4 Prognostic value

The historical residual analysis does not establish predictive validity on measured held-out data. It reports a naive correlation between mean ZVF and its outcome column of r = +0.22, with n = 17 per-experiment rows. After mean reward is regressed out of the outcome, the residual correlation with ZVF is r = +0.205 (historical p = 0.43, n = 17 experiments). The associated intervals are not used as inferential evidence here. These are retained as historical mixed-source calculations, not measured-only evidence (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/zvf_iter26_residual.tsv; corrected; see Table E.1). The source's own conclusion is that "we therefore do not treat ZVF as an incremental predictor above reward mean" (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/zvf.tex).

The source-lineage audit resolves two further defects. Of the seventeen finite cells, nine pool variance-mitigation simulation rows. Moreover, the residual script's outcome is `last10_avg`, mixed source outcomes including late training reward, despite its held-out wording. The remaining cells mix sampling, training and tool-use records. Residualising one variable against mean reward does not turn this mixture into an independent held-out test; it is also a semipartial calculation because ZVF itself is not residualised. Neither this coefficient nor its historical interval supports a measured-only predictive claim (sources: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/zvf_summary.tsv; platform_hybrid/experiments/results/zvf_iter26_residual.tsv; reports/project_finish_2026-10-11/colab_validation/result.json).

Pooling across task types shows why a pooled association cannot be read as a score. Over N = 15 experiments spanning five model families, the association between ZVF and performance is Pearson r = −0.769 (p = 0.0008) with Spearman ρ = −0.784 (p = 0.0005), but it is confounded by task type: tool-use experiments reach ZVF = 100% at approximately 0% reward while GSM8K experiments average ZVF = 8.5%. Within GSM8K the association weakens to r = +0.40 (p = 0.09, N = 19), and then to r = +0.13 (p = 0.79) when restricted to the seven model-varying runs. The N = 19 is a separate and larger pool of Tinker GSM8K runs, not a subset of the N = 15 pooled experiments, so the two analyses are not nested (source: platform_hybrid/paper/neurips_2026_variants/paper_P8_workshop.tex). A statistic whose sign is set by which tasks are included in the pool is not a score. On the instrumented runs ZVF also does not track final accuracy (r ≈ 0.09, n = 12) (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/appendix_zvf_formalization.tex).

The historical source reports stronger association with catastrophic collapse than with graded outcome. It states Spearman ρ = 0.56 (Fisher 95% CI [0.19, 0.79], n = 23 pooled cells) and point-biserial 0.62. It labels the outcome correlation as final held-out accuracy, ρ = 0.27, and the registry's bootstrap 95% CI is [−0.37, 0.88]; the Fisher CI is [−0.16, 0.61], p = 0.21, n = 23. These are historical reported values, not current inferential evidence (sources: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p2_results_intro.tex; platform_hybrid/experiments/results/claim_to_run/claim_to_run_table.tsv; Appendix A, claim P2-C2). The lineage audit identifies eighty source rows grouped into twenty-three cells. Six cells have missing ZVF and are dropped before the rank correlation, while the report prints the pre-drop count. Thus the displayed coefficient uses seventeen finite cells, including nine simulation cells. The outcome field is a mixture of stored source metrics, including late training reward; it is not a common held-out endpoint. The claimed sample size and measured-held-out interpretation are withdrawn, as are intervals justified with the pre-drop sample size; on the eight non-simulated finite cells alone, ρ = −0.37 (p = 0.36), the opposite sign. The new Colab audit preserves the source rows, exclusions and grouping in an executable report (source: reports/project_finish_2026-10-11/colab_validation/result.json). A three-channel risk index built on the diagnostic — magnitude, critical slowing down via a rolling lag-1 autocorrelation with window 15, and drift slope, blended 0.3/0.5/0.2 with a max-fusion variant — was evaluated over a 52-row cross-experiment panel comprising 45 variance-mitigation seeds, 2 tool-use collapse anchors and 5 scaling-law collapse anchors, with 11 failures and 41 safe runs. Cross-experiment AUROCs are 0.663 for magnitude, 0.758 for critical slowing down, 0.579 for drift, 0.763 for the blended risk index and 0.929 for the max-fusion index; within-methods, the same values fall to 0.073, 0.335, 0.293, 0.396 and 0.805 respectively (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/zvf.tex). None of these AUROCs is a measurement on real training runs, and this chapter reports them as a synthetic-panel exercise. All 45 variance-mitigation rows come from the declared simulation projection (§6.3.2). The seven collapse anchors enter with channel values fixed by the analysis script, not measured: mean ZVF 1.0 or an imputed 0.95, lag-1 0.95 or 0.85, and slope 0 or 0.015 (source: platform_modal/scripts/zvf_diagnostic_iter130.py). The 41 safe rows are therefore all synthetic. The max-fusion index was selected after the per-axis results were seen. For the 0.929 figure, a Hanley–McNeil 95% CI with 11 positives and 41 negatives is [0.82, 1.00]; that interval describes this constructed panel only. No AUROC is computed on the measured rows alone, because they contain too few failures to estimate one. The gap between the cross-experiment and within-method columns is a warning about the index's transportability: most of the cross-experiment discrimination is coming from the fact that collapse clusters by experimental family, not from a within-family predictive signal. The declared thresholds are a risk above 0.55 to treat as failure-positive, below 0.30 as safe, and the interval between as requiring investigation.

The pillar's own conclusion names three load-bearing limitations: the diagnostic is mechanically coupled to reward sparsity, group size and baseline accuracy; the reported stronger collapse association comes from the mixed-provenance table above and has not been established on a separately identified measured-only cohort; and the unsigned statistic aliases mastery with incapacity (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p2_conclusion.tex). A proposed replacement, the pairwise contrast density, is introduced in the synthesis document with a target association of ρ ≳ 0.45; the project states that this bar is not yet met by the replacements and that the head-to-head protocol remains future work (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/frontier_synthesis_zvf.tex).

## 6.4 P3 — Group Size and Contrast Density

The third pillar studies G, which plays two roles simultaneously in GRPO: it is a variance-reduction knob, like a Monte-Carlo baseline, and it is a preference-density knob, since centring rewards within a group induces implicit pairwise contrasts — nonzero only for mixed-reward groups. Practitioners often treat larger G as strictly better by analogy to variance reduction, which conflates the two roles and ignores cost. The measured evidence does not justify a group-size optimum. Accuracy point estimates are non-monotone in G, but the differences are not resolved statistically, so non-monotonic trainability is not established.

### 6.4.1 The measured sweep

The primary sweep measures G ∈ {2, 4, 8, 16} on a near-ceiling two-digit addition task with Qwen2.5-0.5B, three seeds and 40 steps, evaluated on 200 fresh draws from the training generator. Held-out accuracy (200 fresh problems per run) traces an inverted U with its apex at G = 8. Values are mean ± SE over n = 3 seeds: 0.9900 ± 0.0029 (SD 0.0050) at G = 8, 0.9817 ± 0.0044 (SD 0.0076) at G = 2, 0.9883 ± 0.0017 (SD 0.0029) at G = 4 and 0.9783 ± 0.0060 (SD 0.0104) at G = 16. At n = 3 seeds the arms are statistically indistinguishable: a one-way ANOVA across the four arms gives F(3, 8) = 1.82, p = 0.22, recomputed from `platform_hybrid/experiments/results/groupsize_zvf_sweep.json`. The minimum detectable effect at 3 versus 3 seeds is d = 3.07, so only very large differences could have been detected (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/group_size.tex). A reanalysis of the same measured sweep, using B = 4000 bootstrap resamples with analysis seed 20260702 rather than a new training run, records G = 2 and G = 16 at 0.9817 and 0.9783, a difference of +0.0033 (G = 2 minus G = 16) and an interval of [−0.008, +0.015]; G = 4 and G = 8 add +0.010 and +0.012 respectively, and G = 4 versus G = 32 was not measured on that sweep (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/group_size.tex).

The retention figures are the cleanest way to state the same result. G = 2 retains 100.3% of G = 16 (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/group_size_effect_dpo_check.tsv). A later unified synthesis at the same configuration reports "retention" ratios and signal-to-noise ratios with intervals (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/group_size_iter27.tex). The "retention" is a ratio of last-10 *training* reward, not of held-out accuracy, so this chapter calls it a training-reward ratio. The point values are G = 2 1.0000, G = 4 0.9941, G = 8 0.9960 and G = 16 1.0068, with SNR 1.46, 1.76, 1.94 and 2.16. All four ratios sit above the Wu et al. [@wu2025ittakestwo] 97.6% reference bar. At the seed level (paired TOST on per-seed last-10 training reward against G = 2, margin ±0.02, n = 3 seeds), the TOST p-values are 0.012 at G = 4, 0.043 at G = 8 and 0.056 at G = 16 (corrected; see Table E.1). Equivalence to G = 2 at that margin is therefore supported, narrowly, for G = 4 and G = 8, and is **not** established for G = 16.

The signal-to-noise ratio grows sublinearly with G: it reaches about 52% of the √G ideal, and about 48% above a flat baseline. Reward itself is statistically flat across the sweep, in the range 0.971–0.984 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/group_size.tex). The marginal ZVF gain from G = 8 to G = 16 is 0.6906 → 0.6312, a fall of 0.059, which is smaller than the G = 2 → G = 4 fall of 0.8380 → 0.7635, a fall of 0.075 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/group_size.tex). Diminishing returns on the contrast axis are visible in the measured data, which is what the reconstruction later formalises.

The pillar's own framing of this regime is important and is stated in its abstract: near the ceiling, variance is compressed, so the finding is "no detectable G effect under saturation", not evidence of equivalence on harder, unsaturated tasks. That is, the flat retention curve is a property of an easy task, and the pillar explicitly declines to generalise it. The measured equivalence tests were run up to G = 16, and at the seed level they establish equivalence only up to G = 8; there is no directly trained, matched-budget G = 32 cell in the primary sweep (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p3_abstract.tex). The retention-versus-budget axis is where the picture changes: R(1M) = 0.976, R(4M) = 0.833, R(16M) = 0.750 and R(64M) = 0.727, fitted by $R(T) = 0.729 + 0.247\,e^{-T/5.76\mathrm{M}}$ with $R^2 = 0.948$ and an asymptote of 0.729. The four points are themselves reconstructed (§6.4.2), and a three-parameter curve through four points leaves one residual degree of freedom. The fit is therefore illustrative only, and no confidence interval is reported for its asymptote (corrected; see Table E.1). The Wu reference line and this reconstructed curve cross at T = 1M and diverge by 25 percentage points at T = 64M (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/group_size_iter27.tex). The operative conclusion is scoped to the 0.5B arithmetic task: no G is distinguishable there, so G = 2 is the cheapest setting at statistically indistinguishable accuracy. It is not a recommendation for harder tasks, and it does not conflict with the single-seed Qwen3.5-4B observation of §6.4.3 that G = 2 under-trains with a 50% batch collapse, which is a different model and task.

### 6.4.2 The same-stack four-axis audit and the reconstructed budgets

The strongest de-confound for P3 is a four-axis factorization audit of the same training stack. On the algorithm axis, the residual effect is small: η² = 0.023, from the same five-seed same-stack contrast as §6.2.3. The GRPO−PPO difference is −0.002, paired by seed only: the two arms of each seed were evaluated on different items, with PPO's evaluation items overlapping its training draws far more (§6.2.3; corrected; see Table E.1). The factorization file reports a Monte-Carlo permutation p of 0.62; the exact sign-flip p over the five pairs is 0.625, and the paired t p of 0.374 in §6.2.3 is the primary test for this contrast. Equivalence on the algorithm axis is not established, because the TOST at ±0.005 in §6.2.3 does not establish it (corrected; see Table E.1). On the group-size axis the file reports η² = 0.54 and 0.63, with Cohen's d = −1.47 for the G = 2 → 16 comparison (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/berkeley/unpacking_dpo_ppo_factorization.json, as summarised in platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p3_conclusion.tex). The 0.54 is `eta2_G_last10`, a share of last-10 *training reward*, not of terminal held-out accuracy; 0.63 is for gradient norm. Recomputed on the 12 sweep runs (3 seeds per G), the training-reward share gives F(3, 8) = 3.08, p = 0.090. The corresponding held-out share is η² = 0.41, F(3, 8) = 1.82, p = 0.22. The source itself grades the G-axis result only SUGGESTIVE. The supportable reading is that the group-size axis shows a large but not statistically significant share of training-reward variance at n = 3 seeds per arm, and that the algorithm label is not shown to matter on this stack.

A Miller-style [@miller2024errorbars] error-bars audit covered the pillar's four headline contrasts. Two of them are held here at hypothesis-generator status rather than reported as precise (corrected; see Table E.1): the gradient-utilisation ratio at T = 1M, with a point estimate of 5.03 and no interval that n = 4 budgets can support, and the negative retention-versus-training-token slope, which is fitted to the four *reconstructed* budgets of the grid below, not to trained arms. The SNR-versus-√G slope has an interval of [+0.148, +0.583], which contains the ideal value of +0.500 and is therefore graded NULL. The native Wu-style equivalence claim that G = 2 ≈ G = 16 is also graded NULL (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/berkeley/adding_error_bars_summary.json, as summarised in platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p3_conclusion.tex). This chapter therefore reports none of the four P3 headlines as precise, and the pillar's own summary is that the data do not justify a universal optimum.

Everything at or above G = 32 in the pillar comes from an explicitly reconstructed token-budget grid, retained only as a hypothesis generator. The reconstruction table gives, for G = 4 versus G = 32, a T = 1M comparison of 0.41 against 0.42 with a difference of −0.01, a 97.6% retention ratio and a "generalizes" verdict of yes, a point-estimate criterion of retention at least 90% — the equivalence test at the 97.6% margin, with a reconstruction interval of [0.873, 1.092] at T = 1M, is not passed at any of the four budgets (sources: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/group_size_g4_vs_g32_broader_scale.tsv; platform_hybrid/experiments/results/findings_ledger.jsonl); at T = 4M, 0.55 against 0.66 with −0.11, 83.3% and no; at T = 16M, 0.63 against 0.84 with −0.21, 75.0% and no; at T = 64M, 0.64 against 0.88 with −0.24, 72.7% and no (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/group_size.tex). The source labels these as conditional reconstruction intervals, not fresh-run confidence intervals. The T = 64M accuracy row across G = 4, 8, 16, 32 and 64 reads 0.64, 0.80, 0.85, 0.88 and 0.87 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/group_size.tex) — the apex moves with budget, which is precisely the pattern that makes a universal G optimum incoherent, and precisely the pattern the project declines to claim as measured. A fit of the critical-budget form $|\Delta(T)| = a(1 - e^{-T/\tau})$ gives â = 0.30 and τ̂ ≈ 12.1M tokens, with a 5% critical budget of 2.21M tokens and an interval of [1.29M, 4.77M] (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/group_size_iter23.tex). A difficulty-gated adaptive rule derived from this analysis reports a mean selected group size of 7.0 and a projected 56.2% rollout saving against always using G = 16, with 5 of 20 cells under-predicting by more than 5 percentage points (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p3_conclusion.tex). These are projections, and the chapter marks them as such.

A separate conditional utility audit over 505 tasks, scoring each group size by (1 − ZVF)/√G, selects G = 4 over G = 5 by 0.00177 with a 95% bootstrap interval of [0.00082, 0.00270]. The margin is positive but small, and it is conditional on cohort and objective (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p3_abstract.tex).

### 6.4.3 The curriculum and matched-token null results

Two interventions that P3's design space naturally suggests were run and both returned nulls. They are reported here rather than elsewhere because they are group-size interventions, and because their negative results are load-bearing for the chapter's overall argument.

The first is a difficulty curriculum that filters collapsed groups during training. The intervention works as designed on its own terms: it drives the reported zero-gradient fraction from 0.50 to 0.00 in accepted training batches. Rejected candidates still consume sampling resources, so this does not eliminate wasted rollouts across the whole collection procedure. It does so at approximately 4.81 times the sampling cost, and its held-out gain is identical to the baseline's — both arms move from 0.80 to 0.85 on a held-out set of n = 20 (one seed, eight steps), a gain of +0.05 in each case, which is one item of 20 (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/curriculum_opening/FINDINGS.md, as summarised in platform_hybrid/paper/sections/robustness_smallscale_nulls.tex). At a matched 30k-token budget the curriculum ties the baseline at +0.028 against +0.028, and at five times the cost it loses. The verdict recorded is Null. An important provenance note attaches to this result: an independent verification pass conducted on 6 July 2026 flagged the curriculum claim, along with a single-seed G = 4 "win" of +0.125, as SUSPECT and overclaimed, before multi-seed re-testing failed to reproduce either apparent advantage reliably (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/curriculum_opening/FINDINGS.md). The group-size sweet-spot result is itself a null for the same reason — the +0.125 advantage at G = 4 rested on a single held-out example and did not survive multi-seed re-testing, and G = 2 under-trains with a 50% batch collapse and a +0.0 gain (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/robustness_smallscale_nulls.tex).

The second null family concerns loss formulations intended to remove the length bias. On the held-out metric, the standard sum formulation is nominally the best of the three tested: +0.125 against +0.000 for mean-normalisation and +0.042 for the surprise formulation. These are measured on held-out sets of n = 12–20 per arm, so each difference corresponds to about one or two items and none is distinguishable from noise. The length change under the standard arm is recorded as Δlen = −16 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/robustness_smallscale_nulls.tex). The verdict is Null. A fourth null, on a step-1 predictive layer-freeze, is reported in the same table because it was proposed as a cheap way to stabilise group-relative training: it achieves a per-step overlap of 1.0 on a 1.5B hardcoded-arithmetic toy, but only 0.11 on both seeds at 3B on real GSM8K, while the concentration statistic still holds at approximately 0.39. A later four-seed pass at 3B supersedes that two-seed figure with a mean overlap of 0.083 ± 0.048 (mean ± SD, n = 4 seeds), below the 0.25 expected overlap for independent uniform top-nine sets among 36 layers (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/p1_layerfreeze/scaled_4seed_result.json). The target is the aggregate across all steps, including step 1, rather than an independent final-step target (§5.5). Verdict: Null, and a reversal of its own toy-scale positive under scaling (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/robustness_smallscale_nulls.tex).

Across these four checks, no reliable held-out improvement was established in the tested configurations. The matched 30k-token comparison specifically supports the curriculum null; the other small-sample checks do not jointly constitute a matched-cost intervention study. These results leave the diagnostic's prescriptive value unresolved, which is why P2 is reported as a monitoring tool rather than a validated optimisation objective.

## 6.5 P4 — Length Bias and Held-Out Generalisation

The fourth pillar tests the most widely repeated specific criticism of GRPO: that it rewards verbosity, inflating completion length in a way that Dr. GRPO corrects. The pillar's headline is a scoped null, and the scope is the point.

### 6.5.1 The controlled null

Sixteen controlled runs were analysed: ten on an arithmetic task with Qwen2.5-0.5B over 40 steps, and six on GSM8K chain-of-thought with Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct over 30 steps, split evenly between GRPO and Dr. GRPO. The arithmetic half is saturated: evaluation accuracy is 0.987 for GRPO and 0.992 for Dr. GRPO (paired p = 0.35, five seeds), and completions average 4.7 tokens, so its accuracy and length nulls are nulls at a ceiling (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/drgrpo_vs_grpo.json). Only the GSM8K-CoT half has headroom. At these 30–40-step horizons, neither algorithm exhibits the verbosity-trap signature on either task: the pillar's length-bias flag, which fires when completion length grows while reward stays flat or falls, is zero on every one of the sixteen runs (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex). That flag is not the roster audit's peak-then-decay rule of §6.5.2. Recomputed from the per-step logs, the peak-then-decay rule (peak before 65% of training, last-10 mean reward below 90% of peak) fires on none of the ten arithmetic runs but on five of the six GSM8K-CoT runs, three GRPO and two Dr. GRPO, because the reward trace on that task is noisy and peaks early; length still falls in all five (sources: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/drgrpo_vs_grpo.json; platform_hybrid/experiments/results/drgrpo_gsm8k_cot_full.json).

The summary correlations are as follows, given as mean ± SD across seeds (n = 5 arithmetic, n = 3 GSM8K-CoT). On arithmetic, the rank correlation between training step and completion length is −0.405 ± 0.176 for GRPO and −0.307 ± 0.181 for Dr. GRPO; on GSM8K-CoT it is −0.622 ± 0.301 and −0.355 ± 0.255. The step-versus-reward correlations are +0.709 ± 0.082 and +0.753 ± 0.074 on arithmetic, and +0.046 ± 0.162 and +0.025 ± 0.091 on GSM8K-CoT. The length-versus-reward correlations are −0.419 ± 0.155 and −0.293 ± 0.182 on arithmetic, and −0.501 ± 0.258 and −0.618 ± 0.118 on GSM8K-CoT. Mean reward over the last ten steps is 0.988 for both algorithms on arithmetic, and 0.265 for GRPO against 0.252 for Dr. GRPO on GSM8K-CoT (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex). The direction of the length trend is negative everywhere: all sixteen length slopes are negative, with the arithmetic GRPO slope at −0.029 tokens per step and the GSM8K GRPO slope at −0.481, and the count of runs with a positive horizon is zero (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex). On the harder task, GRPO's final-ten-step mean completion length is 184.4 tokens against Dr. GRPO's 189.3 — a difference of +2.7% in the direction opposite to the critique (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex).

The pillar's abstract states the scope of this null explicitly. The claim is bounded by the 200-token cap used in that regime, and the summary sentence is that "the verbosity-trap signature is absent in this regime" (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_abstract.tex). The pillar is not claiming that GRPO never inflates length; it is claiming that within a capped, short-horizon regime it does not, and it names the length-confounded regime in which a Dr. GRPO advantage should appear as future work (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_results_intro.tex).

### 6.5.2 The roster-level verbosity-trap audit

Set against the controlled null is a descriptive audit over the whole experiment roster, and here the picture differs. The audit defines four statistics per trace with at least five steps: an instability index σ(r_tail)/|μ(r_tail)| over the final 50% of training; a monotonicity score, the fraction of consecutive step-pairs with strictly improving reward; a regression severity, the largest normalised drop from peak; and a binary verbosity-trap flag, set when the peak occurs before 65% of training and the terminal reward falls below 90% of peak (source: platform_hybrid/paper/archive/absorbed/U01_main_compendium/main.tex).

On that definition, the eleven GRPO runs in the roster have a mean instability index of 0.267 ± 0.335, against 0.785 ± 0.297 (mean ± spread across runs; the source does not label the ±, and its size relative to the mean implies an SD) for the seventeen PPO-labelled runs. Four of the eleven GRPO runs, 36% (Wilson 95% CI [15%, 65%], one run per configuration), carry the flag on this definition, including both Qwen3-8B runs and the Nemotron-120B run. That count is reported here as a description of the roster, not as evidence of length bias, because the flag does not isolate the behaviour it is named for. Three properties of the rule prevent the stronger reading. Its thresholds are post hoc: neither the 65% peak position nor the 0.90 terminal-to-peak ratio is pre-registered anywhere in the repository, and the rule cannot be traced to a commit that predates the traces it judges. It is not specific to length: the metric it tests is in-training reward, and the project's own source reads the flag as "a generic instability detector" rather than a length measure, so a run can carry it without any change in response length. And its base rate is high: applied to the wider trace pool rather than this eleven-run subset, the rule fires on the majority of runs — including PPO-labelled runs and the Dr. GRPO control in the one matched comparison — so 36% sits at or below the rate the rule produces on traces with no decay at all. The project's own length data point the other way: under the 200-token cap, length drifts from 194.4 to 183.5 tokens, i.e. shorter. The run-level audit therefore does not establish length inflation, and the pillar's negative finding rests on the controlled comparison below rather than on this roster count. The Nemotron run records the highest instability index in the pool at 1.194, with a regression severity of 1.0 and a terminal-to-peak reward ratio of 0.57. The two DeepSeek-V3.1 GRPO runs, by contrast, show markedly lower instability, with a mean of 0.143 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/archive/absorbed/U01_main_compendium/main.tex; the same rates appear in platform_tinker/reports/combined/combined_capstone.tex, where the trap rates are given as GRPO 36% and PPO 71%). The PPO rate of 12/17 has a Wilson 95% CI of [47%, 87%]. These are descriptive uncertainty intervals for heterogeneous roster counts; their overlap is not a formal test of a rate difference, and the pool composition prevents an algorithm-level stability comparison.

Two caveats on that comparison must travel with it. First, the PPO pool is dominated by classic-RL library runs (SB3, CleanRL, Tianshou) that failed to train at all, with last-10 rewards below 5%; a near-zero reward trajectory mechanically produces a high instability index. Restricted to LLM-native PPO, the PPO Llama instability is 0.105, lower than GRPO Llama's 0.228, and the source therefore explicitly declines to claim that GRPO is more or less stable than PPO at the algorithmic level (source: platform_hybrid/paper/archive/absorbed/U01_main_compendium/main.tex; platform_tinker/reports/combined/combined_capstone.tex). Second, the audit's reward metric is the in-training reward, not a held-out one, and the audit pool is single-seed per configuration: each Tinker configuration was run once and is reported descriptively. The only multi-seed matched base-versus-RL control on Qwen3-8B is the five-seed comparison discussed below.

An earlier draft of this report's summary attributed to P4 the claim that Dr. GRPO is the only algorithm without the verbosity-trap pattern. That claim could not be located in any P4 source. What the sources state is that 36% of the audit's GRPO runs carry the flag; the audit does not report a per-algorithm flag rate for Dr. GRPO, and in the controlled sixteen-run comparison the length-bias flag is zero for both algorithms while the peak-then-decay rule fires on both (three of three GRPO and two of three Dr. GRPO runs on GSM8K-CoT). The finding recorded here is therefore the sourced one: the peak-then-decay pattern is not specific to GRPO, it appears in both arms of the controlled comparison without any length inflation, and the project makes no claim that Dr. GRPO is uniquely free of it. The narrowest related statement the sources do support is a length-drift one — that Dr. GRPO is the only algorithm whose length keeps drifting upward after the initial compression phase (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias_iter48.tex) — which is a different statistic and is not the verbosity-trap flag.

### 6.5.3 Elevated analyses

Because the controlled 30–40-step null is a null, the pillar runs a sequence of progressively more sensitive analyses to establish whether the trap is present but invisible at that horizon. Four of these deserve report, and all four are exploratory. They were run across iterations 12, 20 and 24 of the analysis log after the controlled null was known. The seed counts are small: n = 5 arithmetic and n = 3 GSM8K seeds, and three seeds admit only 10 distinct bootstrap resamples. Several of the intervals resample autocorrelated steps or windows within runs rather than seeds. The p-values below are uncorrected unless stated, and roughly fifteen tests are reported across the three iterations.

A sliding-window trap-onset analysis with window W = 10 relaxes the flag from "peak before 65% of training" to "local peak followed by decay within a window", and finds traps in 2 of 10 arithmetic runs (20%), 4 of 6 GSM8K runs (67%) and 6 of 16 runs overall (37.5%) — every one of which is a local re-expansion rather than a terminal divergence (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex). This is the analysis that most nearly matches the roster-level trap rate. It is a point estimate of trap prevalence in the controlled data, not an upper bound (corrected; see Table E.1). The Wilson 95% CIs are [5.7%, 51%] for 2/10, [30%, 90%] for 4/6 and [18.5%, 61%] for 6/16, with runs as the unit.

A paired bootstrap on the Dr. GRPO minus GRPO difference in rank correlations gives, on arithmetic, Δρ(step, len) = +0.098 with an interval of [+0.073, +0.119] — excluding zero, indicating a less negative step–length rank correlation for Dr. GRPO rather than stronger length compression (seed-paired bootstrap, n = 5); Δρ(step, reward) = +0.043 with an interval of [−0.024, +0.112], which is not significant; and Δρ(len, reward) = +0.126 with an interval of [+0.045, +0.201], again excluding zero. On GSM8K the same three quantities are +0.267 [+0.164, +0.419], −0.020 [−0.069, +0.065] and −0.117 [−0.265, +0.015] (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex). Both methods have negative step–length correlations: −0.405 versus −0.307 on arithmetic and −0.622 versus −0.355 on GSM8K. The differences of +0.098 and +0.267 do not quantify comparative token reduction. A paired change in mean length or token-per-step slope would be needed for that estimand.

A decile-binned coupling analysis on GSM8K shows the mechanism directly: completions in the first length decile, spanning 153–180 tokens, earn a mean reward of 0.351, while completions in the eighth decile, spanning 194–195 tokens, earn 0.233. Longer completions are rewarded less, which is the opposite of the verbosity-trap prediction (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex). A 100-step cross-validation on the arithmetic metrics file returns ρ(step, len) = +0.41 and ρ(step, reward) = +0.27, but the absolute length range across that run is 0.08 tokens against a cap of 5 tokens — a positive correlation over a range too small to be a length effect (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex).

The length–ZVF coupling analysis at iteration 12 is the most mechanistically interesting of the elevated results, because its sign flips between tasks. On arithmetic, ρ(len, zvf) is −0.449 [−0.584, −0.324] for GRPO and −0.291 [−0.428, −0.139] for Dr. GRPO; on GSM8K it is +0.286 [+0.231, +0.348] and +0.187 [+0.148, +0.225]. The Dr. GRPO minus GRPO difference is +0.158 [+0.075, +0.239] on arithmetic and −0.099 [−0.132, −0.041] on GSM8K (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex). Conditioning ZVF on length bins makes the arithmetic structure concrete: the shortest bin has ZVF 0.92 and the longest 0.62, a drop of −0.30 [−0.34, −0.26], whereas on GSM8K the corresponding values are 0.30 and 0.41, a rise of +0.11 [+0.10, +0.13] (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex). On the easy task, shorter completions are more often all-same-reward; on the hard task, longer ones are.

The mechanism analysis at iteration 20 fits, per run, a regression of step-level mean length on step-level mean reward. On arithmetic, Dr. GRPO reduces the slope magnitude: GRPO −3.65 ± 0.08 against Dr. GRPO −3.22 ± 0.15 (mean ± SD over 5 seeds) (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex). The paired differences below are recomputed from `platform_hybrid/experiments/results/length_bias_mechanism_per_run.tsv` as Dr. GRPO minus GRPO, paired by seed, n = 5 (corrected; see Table E.1). Each is given with a paired-t 95% CI, the paired-t p and the exact sign-flip permutation p; the permutation p cannot fall below 0.0625 at n = 5. The OLS slope difference is +0.43 [+0.18, +0.68], paired t p = 0.009, permutation p = 0.0625. The Theil–Sen slope difference is +1.11 [+0.33, +1.88], p = 0.017, permutation p = 0.0625. The ρ(L, R) difference is +0.10 [−0.02, +0.22], p = 0.080. The lag-1 autocorrelation is essentially unchanged, −0.01 [−0.27, +0.26], p = 0.94. On GSM8K (n = 3) the OLS difference reverses sign, −4.41 [−37.7, +28.9], p = 0.63, far too wide to interpret. Across the fourteen exploratory tests of iterations 20 and 24, Benjamini–Hochberg correction leaves the arithmetic OLS and Theil–Sen differences at q = 0.116 each, the smallest q in the family, so no test in it survives correction (corrected; see Table E.1). The historical script also labels coefficient-product ratios of 42% and 55% on arithmetic and 28% and 12% on GSM8K as "proportion mediated". These are not supportable causal mediation estimates. The script multiplies a marginal correlation by a partial correlation and divides by another correlation; its "direct" quantity is also a partial correlation, so the terms are not common-scale path coefficients and need not add to the total. The same-step observational reward, length and ZVF series also do not establish temporal or causal identification. The four percentages are retained only to identify the historical output, with the mediation interpretation withdrawn (sources: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex; platform_modal/scripts/length_bias_mechanism.py, lines 189–235; platform_hybrid/experiments/results/length_bias_mechanism_mediation.tsv).

The drift analysis at iteration 24 closes the sequence. Windowed ρ drift on arithmetic is +0.326 for GRPO with p = 0.035, decomposing into an early −0.330 and a late −0.003, against +0.187 for Dr. GRPO with p = 0.399; on GSM8K the corresponding values are +0.019 with p = 0.905 and −0.084 with p = 0.769 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex). The sign-flip analysis of late windows (w = 10) gives mean late non-negative fractions of 0.527 and 0.382 for arithmetic GRPO and Dr. GRPO and 0.048 and 0.000 for GSM8K GRPO and Dr. GRPO. Tested against one half with an exact two-sided sign-flip permutation over seeds, the p-values are 0.688, 0.625, 0.250 and 0.250 (corrected; see Table E.1). The unit is the seed, not the within-run window: each seed contributes one fraction, so n = 5 on arithmetic and n = 3 on GSM8K, where 0.250 is the smallest p the test can return. The GSM8K Dr. GRPO cell, in which not a single late window flips sign on any of the three seeds, therefore reaches only that floor. The source's p < 0.001 for it was a one-sample t-test on three identical values, whose zero variance gives t = −∞. Recomputed over the same fourteen exploratory tests, Benjamini–Hochberg leaves no test below 0.05: the smallest q is 0.116 (the arithmetic OLS and Theil–Sen differences), and it stays 0.116 for every fourteen-test selection from the sixteen iteration-20 and iteration-24 tests (`platform_modal/scripts/length_bias_iter24.py`; `platform_hybrid/experiments/results/length_bias_iter24_signflip.tsv`). The iteration-24 drift p-values above are unchanged. The iteration-20 and iteration-24 analyses are therefore exploratory throughout, with no multiplicity-surviving result.

### 6.5.4 Held-out generalisation and the scale extension

The pillar's held-out results are the part of the chapter closest to a conventional capability claim, and they are reported with corresponding care. The only multi-seed matched base-versus-RL control on Qwen3-8B is a five-seed comparison on Qwen3-8B-Instruct, which moves from 82.0% to 83.3% with p = 0.26 (source: platform_hybrid/paper/archive/absorbed/U01_main_compendium/main.tex). Stated as a mean improvement over the pre-RL checkpoint, the gain is +0.013 with a paired p = 0.256 at n = 5 — small and not statistically significant, and a matched pre-RL control for the Qwen3-8B-Base checkpoint is not available (sources: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_results_intro.tex`, `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_intro.tex`). In summary, on this near-ceiling configuration, GRPO does not produce a held-out improvement that can be separated from noise at the available seed budget, and that GRPO and Dr. GRPO produce held-out gains that are indistinguishable from each other at that budget (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_abstract.tex).

The lower-accuracy configuration behaves differently and more conventionally. On Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct with GSM8K-CoT, both algorithms do produce significant pre-to-post held-out gains: +6.2 percentage points for GRPO and +5.0 for Dr. GRPO (n = 3 seeds × 200 held-out items per arm). The unit of analysis is the seed. A one-sample t on the three per-seed deltas gives p = 0.0051 for GRPO (deltas +0.055, +0.060, +0.070) and p = 0.0099 for Dr. GRPO (+0.045, +0.060, +0.045). As supporting detail, per-seed McNemar exact p-values are 0.071, 0.036 and 0.016 for GRPO and 0.122, 0.017 and 0.122 for Dr. GRPO; no pooled McNemar p-value is used, because the same 200 items recur across seeds (corrected; see Table E.1). The McNemar detail also overstates what is attributable to training, because the greedy pre-RL evaluation is not deterministic: the six runs evaluate the same base model on the same 200 items before any training, yet their stored pre-RL correctness vectors take two distinct forms, 40/200 and 41/200, which disagree on 7 of the 200 items, including the GRPO and Dr. GRPO runs of the same seed (recomputed from `platform_hybrid/experiments/results/drgrpo_gsm8k_cot_full.json`). About 3.5% of items can therefore flip with no training at all, so some discordant pairs arise without training. The one-item difference in total accuracy between the two observed vectors does not bound the per-run measurement error at ±1 item. Seed-level t-tests summarise the variability present in these runs, but without repeated unchanged-checkpoint evaluations they cannot separate training-seed variation from evaluation noise or guarantee that the latter is adequately represented; the per-seed McNemar p-values remain supporting detail only. The two algorithms' gains do not differ (paired by seed, p = 0.25) (recomputed from platform_hybrid/experiments/results/drgrpo_gsm8k_cot.json; source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_results_intro.tex). This is the project's clearest evidence that the pipeline trains something real; it is reported alongside the near-ceiling null precisely because the contrast between the two configurations is what the pillar's bounded-null interpretation rests on. Across the same comparison, the pooled mean completion length drifts from 194.4 to 183.5 tokens, that is downward (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_abstract.tex).

A scale extension tests whether the null survives when the cap is lifted. Qwen3-8B with LoRA rank 4 was run on GSM8K for 30 steps at G = 8 and batch size 4, three seeds per algorithm, with the completion cap raised fivefold to 1,024 tokens. All six arms compress: GRPO moves 1004 → 905, 981 → 944 and 996 → 900 tokens, and Dr. GRPO moves 999 → 931, 972 → 902 and 1000 → 878 — declines of between 3.8% and 12.2% in every arm. Late ZVF is 0.45, 0.70 and 0.72 for Dr. GRPO against 0.47, 0.47 and 0.55 for GRPO, and step-0 completions are approximately 98% of the cap (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/length_bias.tex). The step-0 saturation is the caveat that limits this extension: with completions already at 98% of the ceiling at the first step, the run starts truncated, and length compression under a binding cap is not the same phenomenon as length control in an uncapped regime. The pillar reports the extension as consistent with the null, not as an independent confirmation of it.

Finally, the P4-facing headline in the Miller-style error-bars audit is the iteration-136 arithmetic late-training-efficiency contrast. Late efficiency is training-reward gain per unit of length change. The contrast is Dr. GRPO minus GRPO, paired by seed, n = 5, with mean difference +0.020 (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/berkeley/adding_error_bars_summary.json; platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_conclusion.tex). Recomputed from `length_bias_iter136_step_coupling.tsv`, the effect size is $d_z$ = +2.68, the mean paired difference divided by the SD of the differences. The exact one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank p is 0.031, which is 1/32, its floor at n = 5 (corrected; see Table E.1). The paired t that matches $d_z$ gives t(4) = 5.96, two-sided p = 0.004. The two-sided exact sign-flip p is 0.0625, again the n = 5 floor: all five pairs point the same way, yet the non-parametric test cannot reach 0.05 two-sided. The contrast was one of eight directional hypotheses in the iteration-136 family (four per task). Under Holm correction across the eight, the family's own Wilcoxon p becomes 0.25. This chapter therefore grades it *suggestive*, not decisive (corrected; see Table E.1): a consistent direction across five seeds on a training metric, with two-sided non-parametric p = 0.0625 at n = 5. The audit's three NULL verdicts fall on Pillar-1 and Pillar-3 headlines rather than on this one. The pillar also records its own limitations plainly: a single-benchmark held-out evaluation, a single-seed top-10-checkpoint frontier comparison, weak length identifiability, and marginal paired tests (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_conclusion.tex).

## 6.6 Cross-Pillar Observations and Limitations

Three observations span the four pillars and are worth stating together, because the chapter's overall contribution is better captured by their pattern than by any single number.

First, the project's strongest results are its de-confounds, and its de-confounds mostly return nulls. The matched same-stack PPO-versus-GRPO contrast returns a seed-paired difference of −0.002 with p = 0.374 (n = 5 seeds, arms evaluated on largely different items, at a near-ceiling accuracy, not shown equivalent by TOST at ±0.005); the four-axis factorization audit attributes η² = 0.023 to the algorithm axis and η² = 0.54 of last-10 training-reward variance to the group-size axis (F(3, 8) = 3.08, p = 0.09, n = 12 runs); the controlled length-bias comparison returns zero length-bias flags across sixteen runs. The arithmetic PPO contrast retains prompt-exposure and evaluation-overlap confounders, while the primary G sweep fixes prompts and steps rather than completion or token counts (§4.3). Those two contrasts use saturated 0.5B arithmetic at evaluation accuracy 0.98–0.99. The sixteen-run length comparison additionally includes six GSM8K-CoT runs, whose 200-token cap binds a different limitation (§6.5.1). These designs found no detectable advantage on their respective contrasts, with the stated confounds and limits. The group-size axis carries the largest variance share, but at n = 3 seeds per arm it does not reach significance on either training reward or held-out accuracy. It is also a hyperparameter rather than an algorithm choice. The chapter's empirical conclusion is therefore conditional on saturation: on a 0.5B arithmetic task at evaluation accuracy near 0.99, no algorithm-label effect was detectable, which cannot distinguish "no effect" from "no headroom"; the sampling configuration is the more plausible lever but was not shown to matter at this seed budget. Rerun on the unsaturated Qwen2.5-1.5B GSM8K-CoT configuration (§6.5.5), the first version's +5.0-point (+0.050) gap for GRPO at G = 8 over a cold-critic PPO under a binding 200-token cap did not survive the corrected v2 rerun (512-token cap, warm-started critic, 1,319 test items): GRPO G8 − PPO is +0.76 percentage points (+0.0076; 95% CI [−0.34, +1.85] points), with an interim TOST calculation at ±2 points (±0.02) (Table 6.2, provisional; final equivalence not established), and G8 − G2 is not significant after Holm adjustment.

Second, the diagnostics and interventions are less prescriptive than their motivating literature suggests, and the project says so. ZVF reproducibly counts homogeneous or near-homogeneous reward groups under its declared definition. The historically reported catastrophic-collapse association (ρ = 0.56, 95% CI [0.19, 0.79], n = 23 cells) and nominal graded-outcome association (ρ = 0.27, 95% CI [−0.37, 0.88], historical n = 23) come from a mixed-source table and are withdrawn as evidence; they are retained only as identified historical values. Restricted to the eight non-simulated finite cells, the graded-outcome correlation is ρ = −0.37 (p = 0.36). Both calculations drop six missing-ZVF cells, leaving seventeen finite cells, nine synthetic, and mixes outcome definitions (§6.3.4); they cannot establish diagnostic reliability on measured runs. Independently of those correlations, the definition is unsigned and cannot distinguish mastery from incapacity. The difficulty curriculum, group-size sweet spot, length-bias loss formulations and step-1 layer freeze establish no reliable held-out advantage in their respective checks; only the explicitly matched-token comparisons support a matched-cost reading (§6.4.3), and two of them (the curriculum and the G = 4 win) were flagged as SUSPECT by an independent verification pass before multi-seed re-testing failed to reproduce either advantage reliably. Reporting an intervention as a null after an independent reviewer flagged it as overclaimed is, in this project's convention, part of the result.

Third, the single-seed regime is the dominant shared limitation. The cross-scale anchors are single-seed and descriptive. The roster-level verbosity-trap audit is single-seed per configuration, and its in-training reward metric is not a held-out one. The P3 sweep, the P4 controlled comparison, both same-stack comparisons (the v2 rerun at seven to ten seeds), the five-seed Qwen3-8B held-out control and the P4 scale extension operate at n ≥ 3, and the Qwen3-8B control is the only multi-seed matched base-versus-RL comparison on Qwen3-8B; the Qwen2.5-1.5B GSM8K-CoT pre/post runs and the small-sample pairs of Table 8.B are the other base-versus-trained comparisons. Every headline in this chapter is therefore stated at the strength that its own evidence base supports, which is in several cases weaker than the strength at which it would need to be stated to generalise beyond the named stack. The reconstructions (G = 32 and above in P3, the T = 64M grid, the difficulty-gated saving projection) are held at hypothesis-generator status for the same reason: they are joint-fit extrapolations, not trained arms, and the project declines to let them carry a recommendation.

Taken together, the four pillars establish no general algorithm ranking: the unsaturated v1 PPO gap did not survive the corrected v2 rerun, which finds no detectable GRPO advantage, while the group-size contrasts remain unresolved at these seed budgets. The method’s most widely repeated specific failure mode — length inflation — is absent in the regimes where it was tested directly and is not established by the roster flag, which is not specific to length.
