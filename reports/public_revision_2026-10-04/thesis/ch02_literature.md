# 2. Literature Survey

This chapter surveys the prior work on which the project is built and the gap it occupies. It revises the survey of the Phase-1 report (source: `platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex`), retaining that survey's seven-part structure and updating each part with the material the Semester 4 work added: the P1–P8 internal reports and the E1–E14 evaluation campaign. Material on RLHF, PPO, DPO, GRPO, DeepSeek-R1, parameter-efficient fine-tuning and evaluation benchmarks also draws on the Semester-3 Group 6 capstone literature survey [@group6capstone2026survey], a group work of six authors of whom the present author was one; where this chapter reuses that survey's findings they are attributed to it here. Two rules of construction carry over from Phase 1. The first concerns proportion. The contribution of this project is overwhelmingly a contribution of measurement and reporting practice rather than of a new training algorithm, so the literature that establishes measurement practice receives more room here than the literature that establishes methods. The second concerns evidence. A figure is quoted only where a repository source actually states it; where a source states a finding without giving a number, the finding is described qualitatively and the chapter says so. Where the project's own documents state the same quantity differently, the divergence is reported rather than silently reconciled, and Section 2.5 records one such divergence in full.

## 2.1 From RLHF and PPO to Group-Relative Methods

Policy-gradient post-training of language models begins with the RLHF pipeline. Christiano et al. [@christiano2017deeprlhf] established the pattern of reward-model training on human pairwise preferences followed by reinforcement learning against that reward model, on Atari and MuJoCo tasks starting from a randomly initialised policy; the supervised fine-tuning stage that precedes both in the language-model pipeline was added in later work [@stiennon2020summarize; @ouyang2022instructgpt]. Ziegler et al. [@ziegler2019finetuning] adapted the pattern to language, collecting approximately 60,000 comparisons and, following Jaques et al. [@jaques2017sequencetutor; @jaques2019wayoffpolicy], adopting the per-token KL penalty against a frozen reference policy that has been standard ever since. Stiennon et al. [@stiennon2020summarize] scaled the recipe to a 6.7B summarisation model with 64,832 comparisons, demonstrated transfer to an out-of-distribution summarisation set (CNN/DailyMail), and produced early empirical evidence of reward over-optimisation: continued optimisation against a frozen reward model eventually degrades the true objective. Ouyang et al. [@ouyang2022instructgpt] then built InstructGPT, whose three-stage pipeline used roughly 40 contractors to produce about 13,000 supervised fine-tuning prompts, about 33,000 reward-model prompts, and about 31,000 PPO prompts. Two details of that work matter for this project. First, the reward model was 6B rather than 175B because the larger reward model proved unstable during training — a reminder that the reward model is itself a trained component with its own failure modes. Second, the preference data were collected as rankings of K ∈ {4, …, 9} responses, which yields C(K, 2) pairwise comparisons from a single annotation batch; this is precisely the within-prompt comparative structure that group-relative methods would later exploit directly. InstructGPT's headline results were that a 1.3B InstructGPT was preferred to the 175B GPT-3 base model, that the 175B InstructGPT was preferred to GPT-3 in 85% of evaluations, that hallucination on a closed-domain task fell from 41% to 21%, that toxicity was reduced by roughly 25%, and that TruthfulQA performance roughly doubled; inter-annotator agreement was approximately 73%. The same source records four limitations — annotation cost, reward-model over-optimisation, the difficulty of aligning to user intent rather than to annotator preference, and the residual capability costs of alignment (source: `capstone-literature-survey/section_2_2_rlhf.tex`).

The optimiser for that pipeline was PPO [@schulman2017ppo]. PPO's clipping mechanism, with ε ≈ 0.2, restricts each update's policy ratio, and generalised advantage estimation [@schulman2016gae] with its standard defaults (λ = 0.95, γ = 0.99) provides the advantage estimate. Recasting language generation as an RL problem makes the state the prompt plus generated prefix, the action the next token, the policy the language model, and the reward sparse and terminal. The practical consequence is a four-model architecture — policy, reference, reward, and value — whose mixed-precision memory footprint is roughly four times that of supervised fine-tuning alone; PPO-ptx mitigates capability regression by mixing pretraining gradients into the update. Zheng et al.'s "Secrets of RLHF" study [@zheng2023secretsrlhf] found that policy constraints, not algorithmic choice, are the single most critical factor in whether RLHF succeeds: without them, training exhibits reward hacking, length explosion, and catastrophic pattern collapse (source: `capstone-literature-survey/section_2_3_ppo.tex`). That finding is the intellectual ancestor of the zero-variance analysis in this project, because it locates the failure mode in the constraint structure rather than in the objective.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), introduced with DeepSeekMath [@shao2024deepseekmath], removes the learned value network altogether. For each prompt it samples a group of G completions, scores them with a reward function, and standardises each completion's reward against the group mean and standard deviation; the resulting z-score serves as the advantage for every token of that completion. In the DeepSeekMath configuration G = 64 and β = 0.04. Eliminating the value model reduces the number of trained and served models from four to three; the paper motivates this as reducing PPO's memory usage but does not quantify the saving. DeepSeekMath-RL 7B reached 51.7% top-1 accuracy on MATH against a 46.8% supervised fine-tuning baseline, 60.9% with self-consistency over 64 samples, GSM8K improved from 82.9% to 88.2%, and CMATH improved from 84.6% to 88.8% despite no Chinese data in the RL training set. The paper's iterative variant retains 10% of historical data in a replay buffer to prevent catastrophic forgetting in the co-evolving reward model. RLOO [@ahmadian2024backtobasics] is the closely related leave-one-out estimator that baselines each sample against the mean of the *other* samples in the group; it already performs a within-group baseline, and is therefore directly relevant to the signal-starvation analysis this project conducts (sources: `capstone-literature-survey/section_2_5_grpo.tex`; `platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex`).

DeepSeek-R1 [@guo2025deepseekr1] then demonstrated that pure outcome-reward RL can elicit chain-of-thought behaviour from a base model without supervised cold-start data. R1-Zero was trained on DeepSeek-V3-Base, a 671B mixture-of-experts model, using rule-based rewards only and no neural reward model. AIME 2024 pass@1 rose from 15.6% to 71.0% over training in the original preprint — the *Nature* version cited here reports 77.9% after extended training — with consensus decoding over 64 samples (cons@64) reaching 86.7% and MATH-500 reaching 95.9%. The source states the training configuration as 16 sampled outputs per question (the group size), a training batch of 512 outputs (32 questions × 16 samples), a learning rate of 3 × 10⁻⁶, a KL coefficient of 0.001, and approximately 10,400 policy update steps. The paper documents spontaneous chain-of-thought, self-verification, and the widely reported "aha moment" as emergent rather than taught behaviours. The full R1 model, trained with a four-stage pipeline that includes a language-consistency reward, reports 79.8 on AIME, 97.3 on MATH-500, and 71.5 on GPQA, against 79.2 / 96.4 / 75.7 for o1-1217. Distillation into six students from 1.5B to 70B produced Distill-Qwen-1.5B at 28.9% AIME and 83.9% MATH-500, and Distill-Qwen-32B at 72.6% and 94.3%, against 47.0% and 91.6% for Qwen2.5-32B-Zero trained from scratch; the 7B and 16B base models failed to show meaningful reasoning gains from direct RL (source: `capstone-literature-survey/section_2_6_deepseek_r1.tex`).

For this project, the significance of that lineage is narrower than the headline numbers suggest. GRPO's defining operation is a within-group standardisation, and a standardisation is undefined when the group's rewards are all identical. That degenerate case is not an edge case: it is the modal case early in training on a hard task and late in training on an easy one. The rest of this chapter and the empirical chapters that follow are concerned with what that degeneracy does to the training signal, and with how honestly the field currently reports it.

## 2.2 Direct Preference Optimisation as a Contrast

An orthogonal branch of post-training removes on-policy sampling entirely. Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO) [@rafailov2023dpo] exploits the fact that the RLHF objective with a KL constraint admits a closed-form optimal policy, which permits the reward to be reparameterised in terms of the policy itself. The result is a simple classification loss over a fixed dataset of preference pairs, with no reward model, no value model, and no sampling loop. This removes components of the PPO training stack; no fixed memory or wall-clock saving is asserted here, because it depends on the implementation and workload. Four limitations are documented in the same source: the training data are static and offline, so the implicit reward goes stale as the policy drifts; there is no online exploration beyond the dataset's support; the loss can lower the absolute likelihood of preferred completions while widening their margin over dispreferred ones (likelihood displacement); and the method has weaker credit assignment for long-horizon reasoning, where correctness of later steps depends on earlier ones (source: `capstone-literature-survey/section_2_4_dpo.tex`).

The contrast is structurally instructive for this project. DPO cannot suffer zero-variance collapse, because it never standardises within a sampled group — its contrast is supplied by the dataset rather than constructed at training time. But it equally cannot exploit a verifier that is available in the loop, which is the property that makes RLVR attractive on mathematics. GRPO's zero-variance pathology is therefore the price of its on-policy, verifier-in-the-loop design, and measuring that price is exactly what makes the pathology worth quantifying. The DPO family has since diversified into inverse-preference and non-paired variants — IPO [@azar2023ipo], KTO [@ethayarajh2024kto], ORPO [@hong2024orpo], SimPO [@meng2024simpo], and SLiC [@zhao2023slichf] among them — which the project's cross-library protocol treats as no-rollout baselines (sources: `capstone-literature-survey/section_2_4_dpo.tex`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`).

The boundary between the two families is also less sharp than it first appears. Wu et al. [@wu2025ittakestwo] argue that GRPO is "secretly DPO" — that its efficacy stems from an implicit contrastive objective, which makes it structurally related to DPO — and report that 2-GRPO retains 97.6% of the performance of 16-GRPO at 12.5% of the rollouts (arXiv v3; the earlier v2 reported 98.1%). That reduction is the theoretical counterpart of the PCD statistic this project develops: if a group is exactly as informative as the correct/incorrect pairs it contains, then the pairwise-contrast density PCD = ((G − 1)/G) · E[p(1 − p)] is the natural magnitude-aware summary of a group's usable signal, and the group-size question becomes a question about how much contrast a given sampling budget buys. The present project is careful about what it inherits from that argument: the reduction is cited as prior theory, while the empirical claims resting on it are scoped to this project's own measurements, reported in Chapter 6 (sources: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p7_intro.tex`).

## 2.3 Fixing GRPO's Biases and Collapse

A dense and fast-growing literature targets specific GRPO pathologies. Dr. GRPO [@liu2025understanding] removes two normalisation terms from the standard objective — the per-response 1/L factor and the question-difficulty amplification — restoring the sequence-level gradient up to a global scale, and is motivated as a fix for an optimisation bias in GRPO that artificially inflates response length, especially of incorrect responses. DAPO [@yu2025dapo], from ByteDance, changes four things in one release: asymmetric Clip-Higher clipping, dynamic sampling that resamples until the batch carries signal, token-level loss normalisation, and over-long filtering; the reported result is 50 points on AIME 2024 with a 32B model. GSPO [@zheng2025gspo] (Qwen team, 2025) moves the importance ratio from the token level to the sequence level and was credited with stabilising the Qwen3 series, particularly its mixture-of-experts variants. Production-side variants include VAPO [@yue2025vapo], a value-based PPO framework for long chain-of-thought reasoning (sources: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_intro.tex`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p6_intro.tex`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`).

A third thread attacks the zero-variance failure mode directly, and this is the thread the project's own diagnostic sits in. GRESO [@zheng2025greso] pre-screens prompts before sampling so that uninformative prompts are never paid for. CPPO [@lin2025cppo] prunes completions after rollout, reporting up to 7.98× speedup on GSM8K. NGRPO [@nan2025ngrpo] adds advantage calibration and asymmetric clipping so that all-incorrect groups still carry gradient. Scaf-GRPO [@zhang2025scafgrpo] injects tiered scaffolding hints when learning stagnates, reporting a 44.3% relative improvement on AIME 2024. MO-GRPO [@ichihara2025mogrpo] addresses vanilla GRPO's collapse to a single reward component under multiple objectives through variance-based reward re-weighting. SSPO [@yang2025sspo] operates at subsentence granularity to balance GSPO's stability against token-level GRPO's expressiveness, raising GRPO's five-benchmark mathematics average from 43.01 to 46.72 on Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B. RL-ZVP [@le2025rlzvp] takes the opposite approach to filtering: it extracts signal *from* zero-variance prompts through entropy-guided advantage shaping. AERO [@zhang2026aero] instead adapts the rollout allocation and prunes rollouts to avoid zero-advantage dead zones. GVPO [@zhang2025gvpo] re-weights by within-group variance. The tail continues with λ-GRPO [@wang2025lambdagrpo], MC-GRPO [@kim2026mcgrpo] (median-centred advantage), G²RPO-A [@guo2025g2rpoa], ConSPO [@zhang2026conspo], and Goldilocks RL [@mahrooghi2026goldilocks] (sources: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p7_intro.tex`; `platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex`).

One act of bookkeeping in this literature deserves emphasis, because it is a claim about what may *not* be inferred. AVSPO [@he2026avspo] and other interventions are described in the project's own related-work source as prior or concurrent interventions, not as evidence for this project's runner, and the internal report explicitly claims neither priority nor an intervention effect over them (source: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`). The present thesis inherits that disclaimer without qualification.

Against this background, the project's own contribution to the area is deliberately meagre in algorithmic ambition and deliberately demanding in evidential standard. The Zero-Variance Fraction (ZVF) — the fraction of groups whose completions are all correct or all incorrect — was measured across libraries, model scales, and training phases in the companion ZVF report (P2), and that report stopped at description: ZVF tracks collapse, drifts upward, and is mechanically entangled with accuracy and group size, so it declined to promote ZVF to a predictive or causal statistic. A minimal binomial model of group degeneracy was then proposed, in which the expected ZVF indicator is $h_G(p) = p^G + (1-p)^G$ and the usable signal is $S = p(1-p)(1-h_G(p))$; three regularities follow in closed form, each paired with an empirical check drawn from a 368-run Weights & Biases audit that counts all logged runs and is therefore a superset of the benchmark's 70+ core training runs. Because $h_G(p)$ is symmetric under $p \leftrightarrow 1-p$, ZVF assigns the same value to a mastered prompt and to a hopeless one, producing the U-shape measured across six model labels in a 368-run audit; and exact-tie ZVF is sensitive to reward rounding: jitter of order 10⁻⁴ eliminates exact ties while shifting measured within-group contrast by less than 10⁻⁶. This does not hold for the declared sample-variance threshold of 10⁻⁶, which retains 95/600 groups before and after jitter (§6.3.1). Pairwise-contrast density is proposed as a magnitude-aware candidate control signal, without this test establishing its superiority over tolerance-based ZVF, and the toy-scale validation reports a gradient-norm correlation of corr = +0.71, marked directional only. The word *controller* in that work names the implemented policy, not an established improvement: the decisive static-G = 16 matched-token bakeoff remains unrun (sources: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p7_intro.tex`).

The one genuinely measured GRPO-variant comparison in the repository is a Dr. GRPO versus GRPO control. On Qwen2.5-0.5B two-digit arithmetic at group size 8, 40 steps, and 5 seeds, both arms were at ceiling and indistinguishable: GRPO 0.987 ± 0.003 against Dr. GRPO 0.992 ± 0.003 (mean ± SE over 5 seeds; SDs 0.006 and 0.008), paired difference +0.5 percentage points, t = 1.05, df = 4, p = 0.35; mean completion length was 4.74 against 4.71 tokens. At that ceiling, and with completions under five tokens, the task cannot show either an accuracy or a length difference. In a second regime — Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct on GSM8K with a 200-token chain-of-thought cap and 3 seeds — GRPO moved from 20.2% to 26.3% and Dr. GRPO from 20.5% to 25.5%; per-seed McNemar tests were significant in 2 of 3 seeds for GRPO (p = 0.071, 0.036, 0.016) and 1 of 3 for Dr. GRPO (p = 0.122, 0.017, 0.122), and the pooled p < 10⁻³ is expressly rejected as overstating significance; with the seed as the unit, one-sample t-tests on the three per-seed gains give p = 0.005 (GRPO) and p = 0.010 (Dr. GRPO) (recomputed from drgrpo_gsm8k_cot.json). Neither arm inflated length over 30 steps (GRPO 195 → 180 tokens; Dr. GRPO 194 → 187). The same source contains head-to-head and rank-correlation tables whose values are *projections*, not measurements: the GRPO baseline row is a synthetic baseline produced by a dry run of `platform_hybrid/experiments/variance_mitigation_integration.py`, the file states that it makes no measurement claim for any row of that table, the correlation coefficients (0.78 / 0.66 / 0.63 / 0.31) carry the same caveat, and the configuration proxies under `platform_local/unified/` are explicitly not faithful reimplementations. Those numbers are reported here only to mark them as unusable as evidence. The nearest thing to a headline cross-library effect in the project is also the one that must be read most carefully: holding the visible group size, learning rate, data, seed, and label fixed while changing stacks moved final GSM8K reward from 85.6% to 5.0%, a 17× swing; the audit that followed found the managed stack had also pinned a different base checkpoint (Qwen3-8B-Base on Tinker against the instruction-tuned Qwen3-8B on TRL), so the source describes this as an under-specification exhibit rather than a backend-only causal effect. The complementary telemetry fact is that running "DAPO" on an open Colab trainer whose dynamic sampling demonstrably filters zero-variance groups produced mean ZVF 0.00, while running "DAPO" on a closed managed stack where only an asymmetric-clip surrogate is user-settable produced mean ZVF 0.58 — the same label, opposite telemetry (sources: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/variance_mitigation_comparison.tex`; `platform_hybrid/experiments/results/drgrpo_vs_grpo.json`; `platform_hybrid/experiments/results/drgrpo_gsm8k_cot.json`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p6_intro.tex`).

## 2.4 Verifiable Rewards, Frameworks and Reporting Practice

RLVR — reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards — was formalised for open post-training in TÜLU 3 [@lambert2024tulu3], which is the source this project follows in treating a programmatic checker rather than a learned preference model as the reward. The implementation landscape the project unifies is crowded and heterogeneous. On the training side it includes TRL, OpenRLHF [@hu2024openrlhf], veRL (HybridFlow) [@sheng2025hybridflow], NeMo-RL, DeepSpeed-Chat [@yao2023deepspeedchat], trlX, Axolotl, and Tinker as a managed back-end; each makes different default choices about advantage normalisation, KL estimators, tokenisation loss masking, rollout batching, and importance-sampling granularity, and the project identifies those differences as the principal source of the cross-library gap it quantifies. On the inference side, vLLM [@kwon2023vllm] and SGLang [@zheng2024sglang] supply the rollout backends on which modern frameworks depend, with FlashAttention [@dao2022flashattention] and LoRA [@hu2021lora] as load-bearing kernels. Tinker is adopted here through its `message_with_tool` interface as the neutral substrate for tool-use extensions, and the same source explicitly cites tool-use and agentic RL settings as concurrent environments in which this project's ZVF measurements will need to be re-evaluated rather than assumed to transfer (sources: `platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`).

The evaluation benchmarks are equally well established and equally awkward. GSM8K [@cobbe2021gsm8k] comprises 8,500 problems split 7,473 / 1,319, on which a fine-tuned GPT-3 verifier reached 35% at release while current systems exceed 95%; GSM8K-Platinum [@vendrow2025platinum] has since documented label noise in the original set, which is why the source describes the benchmark as necessary but not sufficient. MATH [@hendrycks2021math] comprises 12,500 problems across seven subjects and five difficulty levels, on which the original results reached 6.9%; MATH-500 is the 500-problem subset introduced with the Lightman process-supervision work [@lightman2023lets], on which current systems sit at roughly 99%. AIME comprises 15 problems per year with integer answers in 0–999; reported AIME 2024 accuracies include GPT-5 at 95.7% and Grok 4 at 94.3%. Because these benchmarks are close to saturation, the source frames chain-of-thought as the action space that makes them interesting at all, and identifies process reward models as the bridge from terminal outcome signals to step-level credit (source: `capstone-literature-survey/section_2_8_mathematical_reasoning_benchmarks.tex`).

Reporting practice is where the project's own contribution is anchored, and its lineage is explicit. Model Cards [@mitchell2019modelcards] introduced documentation of intended use, out-of-scope use, and per-slice evaluation results; Datasheets for Datasets [@gebru2021datasheets] extended the idea to datasets, covering composition, preprocessing, annotation process, and discriminatory-impact mitigation; Data Statements [@bender2018datastatements] added the speaker-demographic axis; and Data Cards [@pushkarna2022datacards] bridged research and industry practice with schema, provenance, sampling-distribution, and audit-transparency fields. A cross-coupling audit at iteration 109 maps these four works onto the project's legacy 18-field experimental manifest, showing that they cover 12 distinct fields, that the remaining six are either RL-specific (per-step ZVF, group-size schedule) or stack-coverage items with no direct analogue in the prior literature, and that items 9–12 — annotation process and annotator demographics — have the lowest evidence density in the live manifests. The same iteration verified all four entries against CrossRef metadata (4/4 pass on year, five-gram title, and author-family overlap ≥ 0.6) and retrofitted two entries that had been present in the bibliography but never cited. The resulting standard, MIN-REPORT-RL, has eight items: seven run-manifest fields and one evaluation item (held-out pass@k), which belongs to the evaluation report rather than to run-start stack identity. The count was aligned at eight on 2026-07-11, and this thesis uses "the eight-item standard" and "the seven manifest fields" in that sense throughout (sources: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p5_related.tex`; `platform_hybrid/experiments/results/p5p8/p5_iter109_minreport_coupling.tsv`; `platform_hybrid/experiments/results/p5p8/p5_iter109_crossref_verify.tsv`; `platform_hybrid/experiments/results/p5p8/p5_iter109_bib_audit.tsv`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p6_intro.tex`).

The reproducibility literature to which this standard is a deliberate echo is older and better established. Henderson et al. [@henderson2018deep] showed that deep-RL results depend on codebase, hyperparameters, and even the choice of random seeds, to the point that the same algorithm from two repositories behaves as two algorithms. Colas et al. [@colas2019hitchhiker] and Agarwal et al. [@agarwal2021deep] (`rliable`) supplied the statistical repair kit — rigorous comparison protocols and small-sample-aware aggregate metrics with interquartile-mean reporting and performance profiles. Jordan et al. [@jordan2024benchmarking] argued that rigorous benchmarking in RL is computationally prohibitive at the scale it would require, and called for an additional experimentation paradigm to complement it. Dodge et al. [@dodge2019show] proposed reporting expected validation performance as a function of compute budget; the project's run manifest is the RL-for-LLM analogue, replacing *budget* with *stack*. Pineau et al. [@pineau2021reproducibility] formalised the NeurIPS reproducibility criteria, and the project draws the explicit lesson that self-attested checklists have limits, which motivates an artifact-computed badge rather than a declared one. Statistical treatments of evaluation noise (Miller [@miller2024errorbars]) quantify the uncertainty floor against which the project's own within-noise findings should be read, and the evaluation-stack sensitivity documented by the LM Evaluation Harness effort [@biderman2024lmeval], by audits of reasoning evaluations [@hochlehnert2025sober], and by contamination studies of the data side of the same coin, together establish that RL *training* inherits all of this — the verifier is an evaluator in the loop — plus the additional vectors unique to on-policy optimisation (sources: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p5_related.tex`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`).

Three existing resources come closest to the project's registry and are the reason it exists in its present form. HELM [@liang2022helm] aggregates evaluation across a large model and scenario matrix, but its unit is the (model, scenario) cell, not the training stack. The LM Evaluation Harness [@biderman2024lmeval] standardises evaluation by *executing* implementations, whereas the registry here standardises by *describing* stacks it cannot execute — many of them closed — which is precisely why the project required a null-versus-absent convention and an R5 rung that records un-auditability rather than converting it into a false value. Papers with Code catalogued paper–code–leaderboard linkage at scale, stopped at the repository boundary, and was discontinued in July 2025; the source treats its fate as a governance cautionary tale. The project's own claim to novelty is narrow and stated negatively: the targeted literature search through July 2026 did not identify an existing resource providing the executed loss form at GRPO-variant granularity, per-component implementation status, or an unauditability marker (sources: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p6_related_resources.tex`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p6_intro.tex`).

## 2.5 Scaling and Layer-Adaptation Context

Compute-optimal pretraining is governed by well-characterised scaling laws (Kaplan et al. [@kaplan2020scaling]; Hoffmann et al., Chinchilla [@hoffmann2022chinchilla]), but RL post-training is much less well characterised, and the sources the project builds on separate cleanly into those that predict and those that describe. Hilton et al. [@hilton2023rlscaling] derived compute-efficiency frontiers for single-agent RL across Procgen games, Dota 2 and a toy MNIST-based task. For RLHF, Gao et al. [@gao2023scaling] characterised the over-optimisation curve of reward models — the point at which additional optimisation against a frozen reward model buys a decreasing true objective — and Rafailov et al. [@rafailov2024rlhfscaling] extended over-optimisation scaling laws to direct alignment algorithms (DPO, IPO and SLiC), which have no separate proxy reward model. Nimmaturi et al. [@nimmaturi2025predictive] derived an empirical scaling law specifically for GRPO, identifying three training phases (slow start, rapid improvement, plateau) and reporting that most of the benefit is exhausted by roughly 80% of a single epoch. Tan et al. [@tan2025scalingrl] treat group size as a budget knob in an empirical scaling study of GRPO post-training, in an appendix analysis showing that the optimal rollout size shifts with the training budget. Snell et al. [@snell2024testtime] treat inference-time compute as a scaling axis in its own right, and the project's own related-work source cites these as the works against which its measurements should be read (source: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`).

The project's scaling study covers 70+ runs across seven RL libraries and five model families. This thesis uses one model range throughout: 0.6B–1T, with the anchors above 235B single-seed and descriptive. The rule is that the range spans every model with at least one completed run in the P1 roster. The source documents state narrower ranges because each covers a subset: 0.6B–~671B in the P2, P3 and P4 introductions, and 0.6B–235B for the multi-family data in the related-work source. The principal empirical result of the scaling work is a null: across that range, no reliable scaling law is recovered from reward curves. The companion group-size study adds a second bounded negative: a three-seed sweep does not establish an optimum, since the measured arms are statistically indistinguishable, and the measured signal-to-noise ratio grows at only about 52% of the √G ideal, with G ∈ {2, 4, 8, 16} measured and G ∈ {4, 8, 16, 32, 64} explicitly reconstructed rather than run (sources: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p1_intro.tex`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p2_intro.tex`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p3_intro.tex`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_intro.tex`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/frontier_synthesis_scaling.tex`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p1_conclusion.tex`).

Layer adaptation supplies the second half of this section's context, and connects the project's white-box investigation to the parameter-efficient fine-tuning literature. LoRA [@hu2021lora] freezes the pretrained weights and injects trainable low-rank matrices into the attention projections, so that a d × d projection at d = 4096 with rank r = 16 trains 2dr rather than d² parameters, a 128× reduction (this chapter's arithmetic; the paper itself reports up to 10,000× fewer trainable parameters for GPT-3 175B). QLoRA [@dettmers2023qlora] extends this by quantising the frozen base to NF4 and adding double quantisation, which saves about 0.37 bits per parameter — approximately 3 GB for a 65B model — and permits 65B fine-tuning on a single 48 GB GPU. The motivating arithmetic is that Adam at mixed precision costs roughly 16N bytes, exceeding 100 GB for a 7B model. Prefix tuning, prompt tuning, and adapter layers are the earlier members of the same family, and LoRA's advantages for RL specifically are that the frozen base doubles as the reference policy and that the trainable parameter count is small enough for modest hardware. The project's layer-adaptation pillar asks a narrower and orthogonal question: where inside the network GRPO-style adaptation concentrates, and whether the layers that will matter can be identified early enough to freeze the rest. The reported finding is moderate concentration of per-layer adaptation, with the important negative that step-1 predictability does *not* survive scaling. This connects directly to Mukherjee et al. [@mukherjee2025rlsubnetworks], who found that RL fine-tuning updates only 5–30% of model parameters across seven algorithms and ten model families, suggesting that RL activates a sparse subnetwork. The length-bias pillar completes the scaling picture with its own bounded null: under a 200-token GSM8K generation cap, GRPO and Dr. GRPO produce held-out gains that are indistinguishable at the project's seed budget, and neither arm shows length inflation; the source is careful to state that the 200-token cap is part of the estimand rather than a nuisance detail, so the null bounds the capped, short-horizon regime and cannot adjudicate uncapped reasoning or long agent trajectories (sources: `capstone-literature-survey/section2_7_peft.tex`; `platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p4_intro.tex`).

## 2.6 Process-, Tree- and Stability-Aware Variants

Beyond the group-baseline fixes, a parallel line of work changes *what* signal drives the update. Process reward models supply denser, step-level feedback instead of a single terminal reward, which reduces the chance that a whole group collapses to identical terminal rewards. Let's Verify Step by Step [@lightman2023lets] showed that step-level PRMs substantially outperform outcome reward models on MATH; Math-Shepherd [@wang2024mathshepherd] derives process rewards from automatically generated process annotations, avoiding costly human labels; PRM800K [@lightman2023lets] and PRMBench [@song2025prmbench] supply step-level evaluation data; OmegaPRM [@luo2024omegaprm] uses Monte-Carlo tree search to supervise process rewards without human-in-the-loop; ReasonEval [@xia2025reasoneval] scores reasoning steps for validity and redundancy with trained LLM evaluators; and Implicit PRM [@yuan2024implicitprm] learns process rewards implicitly from outcome labels, with VersaPRM [@zeng2025versaprm] extending process rewards to multi-domain tasks. Tree-of-thought search [@yao2023tot], self-consistency [@wang2023selfconsistency], and critic-style post-hoc verifiers all either benefit from or substitute for process supervision (source: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`). The same source documents the counter-current that matters most here: "A Sober Look" [@hochlehnert2025sober] warns that reward hacking and evaluation artefacts can inflate perceived gains on reasoning benchmarks, which is the project's stated motivation for insisting on binary verifiable rewards in its own mathematics work.

Stability-aware variants constrain the update itself. GSPO's sequence-level importance ratio, discussed above, is the clearest instance, credited with stabilising the Qwen3 mixture-of-experts series. SSPO's subsentence-granularity objective is explicitly framed as balancing GSPO's stability against token-level GRPO's expressiveness. Entropy-guided advantage shaping in RL-ZVP regulates the update when the group carries no reward contrast, and GRESO's pre-screening is a sampling-side stability control. On the reasoning-RL side, GRPO-Lead [@zhang2025grpolead] and LCPO [@aggarwal2025l1] tune reasoning length through length-aware rewards, LIMR [@li2025limr] selects a small high-impact subset of RL training data, Search-R1 [@jin2025searchr1] trains reasoning agents to interleave search with chain-of-thought, and TTRL [@zuo2025ttrl] performs test-time RL on unlabelled data using majority-vote rewards (source: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`).

The Phase-1 survey's judgement on this body of work is retained without amendment, because the Semester 4 evidence has not changed it. These methods propose *levers*; this project first builds the *instrumentation* needed to tell whether any such lever converts wasted rollouts into held-out accuracy — ZVF, group utilisation, pairwise-contrast density, and the collapse decomposition. The nulls reported in the results chapters are exactly the kind of evidence that instrumentation is designed to produce, and they constitute a standing caution against adopting a variant on the strength of a single-seed improvement. The project applies that caution to its own proposals: the adaptive group-size controller is described as an implemented policy rather than an established improvement, and its decisive matched-token comparison remains unrun (sources: `platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex`; `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p7_intro.tex`).

The portfolio's positioning against prior art is a per-pillar statement of what is claimed and, more importantly, what is not. Table 2.1 reproduces the Phase-1 positioning and updates it with the Semester 4 evidence.

| P2 (ZVF) | DAPO dynamic sampling, GVPO, AERO, RL-ZVP, GRESO, CPPO, NGRPO | *Measure* ZVF as an existence statistic; show it is entangled with accuracy and group size; propose PCD as the magnitude-aware replacement |

The two crowded pillars are P2 and P4, where the literature is dense and the project accordingly narrows its claim to measurement rather than to a new algorithm. The widest open ground remains the systems and reporting side, where the targeted literature search through July 2026 did not identify a GRPO-specific machine-readable reporting standard — the gap Section 2.4 establishes from three directions: HELM's unit is the (model, scenario) cell rather than the training stack, the LM Evaluation Harness standardises evaluation by executing code while the registry must describe stacks it cannot execute, and Papers with Code stopped at the repository boundary and was discontinued in July 2025 (sources: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p6_related_resources.tex`; `platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex`).

The Semester 4 evaluation campaign extends this positioning from method comparison to benchmark practice. Rather than adding one more leaderboard number, it evaluates a single trained actor against fourteen agentic and reasoning suites outside its recorded training-suite list, with content-level overlap unverified, each through its own native grader at a pinned revision, under receipt, budget and decontamination controls, and it reports every lane's terminal state rather than a cross-suite aggregate. Its results are in Chapter 8; Table C.3 gives each lane's scope and status. One finding bears directly on the benchmark-practice literature: E4's reported mean reward of 0.0 across 100 base-model trials coincided with a documented serving-bridge stop-sequence defect; its causal contribution to the aggregate is unresolved (Section 8.5), which is exactly the kind of unreported stack detail this positioning argues must be recorded (sources: `outputs/E1_E14_FINAL_RESULTS_2026-09-19.md`; `outputs/e1_e14_small_scale_2026-09-26/E4/BRIDGE_STOP_TOKEN_FINDING.md`).

Two gaps in this survey should be stated plainly, since the chapter's purpose is to locate the work rather than to flatter it. First, the primary papers for the fourteen evaluation suites are not surveyed here at the depth the algorithmic literature receives; the suites are discussed as instruments, sourced to the campaign's own results document, and their native protocols are documented in Chapter 8 and its receipt trail rather than in this chapter. Second, the survey's treatment of tool-use and agentic RL is inherited from the project's own related-work source and is explicitly flagged there as a concurrent setting in which the ZVF measurements will need to be re-evaluated rather than assumed to transfer (source: `platform_hybrid/paper/sections/related_work_v2.tex`). What the chapter does establish is that the project's contribution is a measurement and reporting contribution, that the literature it sits in is dense enough that claim-narrowing rather than claim-extension is the appropriate move, and that the specific gap the portfolio occupies — machine-readable, per-component, executed-loss-form disclosure for group-relative RL runs — is not filled by any of the resources surveyed above.
