# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Background: critic-free RL post-training and the group-relative estimator

Post-training is now the stage at which a base language model is turned into something that reliably follows instructions and solves problems. For several years the dominant recipe was reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), in which a learned reward model is trained on human preference comparisons and then used as the reward signal for a policy-optimisation loop. RLHF works, but it introduces a second learned model whose errors the policy can exploit, and it makes reward construction expensive and hard to audit. The alternative that has taken hold more recently is reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR): instead of a learned reward model, the reward comes from a programmatic checker — exact-match against a reference answer for grade-school mathematics, unit tests for code, a formal or semi-formal validator for structured output. A programmatic checker makes the scoring procedure explicit, but its runtime cost, determinism and semantic fidelity depend on the task and implementation. Its output records the result of that procedure, not necessarily the truth of the answer: parsers, reference keys, tests and model-based judges can all introduce error (source: platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex).

The workhorse optimiser for both settings has been proximal policy optimisation (PPO), which optimises a clipped surrogate objective against a value function critic. The critic is a second network, usually of comparable size to the policy, trained to predict the expected return of the current state; its predictions supply the baseline that the advantage estimator subtracts. This works, but it is expensive and fragile. The critic consumes memory and has its own learning rate and failure modes. Approximation errors can introduce bias through bootstrapped advantage estimators such as GAE with $\lambda<1$; an action-independent baseline subtracted from Monte Carlo returns does not, by itself, bias the policy gradient [@schulman2016gae].

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) removes the critic entirely. Rather than learn a value function, GRPO samples a group of $G$ completions for the same prompt, scores all of them with the verifier, and derives the advantage of each completion from the group's own reward statistics. Concretely, for a group with rewards $r_1, \dots, r_G$, the advantage assigned to completion $i$ is a within-group standardisation,

$$A_i = \frac{r_i - \mu_g}{\sigma_g + \varepsilon}, \qquad \mu_g = \frac{1}{G}\sum_j r_j, \qquad \sigma_g^2 = \frac{1}{G}\sum_j (r_j - \mu_g)^2,$$

which in the original GRPO formulation is then used in a clipped, KL-regularised policy-gradient update. Several inspected custom runners omit KL regularisation; the custom-loss Tinker reference loop is REINFORCE with a group-mean baseline and has no ratio or clipping. This does not describe every run: TRL configuration records include a reference policy and nonzero KL, while managed or unrecoverable settings remain unknown. Table 4.2 gives row-wise loss evidence; the campaign actor's original execution source is not recoverable (Appendix D). The empirical baseline is the group mean; there is no value network to train, no value loss to weight, and no critic to keep in sync with the policy. The trade changes the sampling unit: $G$ completions share one prompt and their estimated advantages are coupled through group statistics. The effective sample size cannot be inferred from the completion count alone.

That trade has a sharp consequence, and it is the observation this project is organised around. Because the advantage is a *within-group* standardisation, its numerator is a deviation from the group mean and its denominator is the group's own spread. When every completion in a group receives the same verifier outcome — all correct, or all incorrect — the group contributes nothing: $\sigma_g = 0$, every numerator is zero, and every advantage $A_i$ is exactly zero. The group has been fully sampled and fully scored, at full token cost, and the reward-relative part of the update is identically zero. In an objective with a KL penalty, that penalty would still pull the policy toward the reference; for verified zero-KL group-relative policy surrogates there is no such contribution, although auxiliary/value losses and optimiser state can still update parameters. Exact ties imply zero reward-relative advantage; the tolerance-based diagnostic in §4.3 also counts near-ties and does not assert exactly zero gradient for them. We call the fraction of a training batch that falls into this state the **Zero-Variance Fraction (ZVF)**, and it is the single measurement that most of this report's diagnostics are built around (source: platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex).

ZVF is not a rare edge case, but its size depends on the regime. The Phase-1 main configuration (Qwen3.5-4B, GSM8K, 40 steps, $G = 8$) records a ZVF of approximately 0.72–0.77, meaning that roughly three quarters of that rollout batch contributes no reward-relative gradient (source: platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex). Selected measured cohort means span 0.16 (Qwen3-8B on held-out GSM8K at $G = 8$) to 0.84 (Qwen2.5-0.5B arithmetic at $G = 2$), so no single value is typical; the measured values are collected in the canonical-numbers table (Table 1.1). The fraction responds to the one knob that most directly controls it: on the Qwen2.5-0.5B arithmetic sweep, mean ZVF falls from 0.838 at $G = 2$ to 0.631 at $G = 16$, a decline of 0.207 across that range (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/group_size.tex; source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/zvf_iter122.tex). Larger groups buy more contrast, with diminishing returns that saturate well before the group is small enough to be cheap.

There is a second and less comfortable observation in the background, and it is about attribution rather than mechanism. "GRPO" is not a single algorithm. It is a label that covers implementations in Tinker, TRL, SkyRL, verl, OpenRLHF and a growing number of research forks, each of which chooses its own answer parser, sampling temperature, top-$p$, KL coefficient, clip range, LoRA target set, precision and evaluation harness. Two runs that both say "GRPO" in their titles may differ in a dozen ways that plausibly move the outcome, and the reported headline number does not distinguish between them. The project's own stack-sensitivity probe makes this concrete: a nominally matched configuration (Qwen3-8B, seed 42, $G = 8$, learning rate $1\times10^{-5}$, GSM8K-500, 30 steps, visible hyperparameters held equal, although the managed Tinker run used the Qwen3-8B-Base checkpoint and TRL the instruction-tuned Qwen3-8B) was run across four framework paths, two of which completed and two of which are dry-run placeholders, with results recorded in `experiments/results/framework_comparison.json` (source: platform_hybrid/paper/main_eai_body.tex). Four separate marginal one-way ANOVAs over 42 experiments give $\eta^2 = 0.546$ for library or framework, $\eta^2 = 0.558$ for training algorithm, $\eta^2 = 0.471$ for model family and $\eta^2 = 0.322$ for model scale — with the important caveat, stated by the source itself, that these factors are confounded with one another and with back-end, reward implementation, hardware and task (source: platform_hybrid/paper/main_eai_body.tex). Because each value is a marginal per-factor $\eta^2$ on a collinear corpus, the four sum to 1.897 and are not a variance decomposition; none estimates a factor's independent contribution (Chapter 6 §6.1; source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/_shared_methods.tex). The framework factor is nonetheless not a nuisance parameter to be wished away: marginally, outcomes vary with it about as strongly as with the algorithm label.

## 1.2 A Motivating Example

Consider a single training step with a group size of $G = 8$ on an easy GSM8K prompt that the current policy already solves. All eight sampled completions produce the correct final answer, so each receives reward $r_i = 1$. The group mean is $\mu_g = 1$ and the group standard deviation is $\sigma_g = 0$, so every advantage $A_i = 0$. The compute spent on that group is real — eight full forward-decode passes at whatever temperature the sampler is configured with — and the group supplies no task-learning signal. It contributes zero reward-relative surrogate gradient in a verified group-centred implementation; KL and auxiliary losses, and optimiser state, remain separate. Now imagine a batch of 16 such prompts in which 12 are already solved and 4 are not: $12/16 = 0.75$ of the batch is "dark" compute (source: platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex). What the Semester 4 work adds to this picture is that 0.75 is not a worst case: it is close to the 0.72–0.77 measured on the Phase-1 main configuration. It is not a universal value either, since selected measured cohort means range from 0.16 to 0.84 depending on model, task and $G$ (Table 1.1), so any claim that GRPO is learning efficiently has to be read against the ZVF of its own regime.

Now suppose, having noticed this, that one wants to answer the obvious follow-up question: is the group-relative estimator actually a better choice than a learned critic? The natural experiment is a matched comparison — same model, same training prompts, same verifier, same step budget, same evaluation, with PPO and GRPO as the only difference. The project's first attempt at that contrast is recorded in a historical internal report under the label "Qwen3-8B", with a Welch $t$-test of $p = 0.7605$ computed over the final ten steps of a single run per arm (source: platform_hybrid/paper/main_eai_body.tex). Those ten steps are autocorrelated observations of one run, not ten independent runs, and the project's later methods note re-examined the row and marks it "provenance conflict; not estimable" (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/_shared_methods.tex). No effect size, confidence interval or power figure is therefore quoted for it here, and the Welch value is carried only as a provenance record (Chapter 6 §6.2.3). The project's own rule is that single-seed Tinker experiments have "zero statistical power" and are "treated as descriptive only and are not subject to significance testing" (source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/_shared_methods.tex).

Two further observations from the same historical source make the example sharper rather than cleaner. First, the same table reports the PPO-versus-GRPO contrast on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct in the opposite direction; it is also one run per arm, so again no inferential statistic is quoted, but the direction alone shows that the apparent answer is not stable across model families (source: platform_hybrid/paper/main_eai_body.tex). Second, the Qwen arms were not executed on a common back-end — the GRPO arm ran through Tinker-managed infrastructure and the PPO arm on a Modal H100 cluster — and the source states this explicitly and declines to draw the algorithmic conclusion it invites: "We therefore treat the PPO/GRPO rows as stack-conditioned evidence, not as an algorithm diagnostic baseline", and "We therefore withhold a directional Qwen algorithm claim" (source: platform_hybrid/paper/main_eai_body.tex). The same section records an aggregation gap that illustrates why such care is warranted: the Qwen PPO last-10 GSM8K figure is 22.5% in the primary results ledger but 35.0% in the statistical summary, and the source names the ledger value as primary and treats the summary value as evidence of an aggregation defect rather than as a second measurement (source: platform_hybrid/paper/main_eai_body.tex).

The genuinely same-stack evidence in the repository is also a null, and its scope has to be stated with it. **Pilot/feasibility only (19 October 2026):** the saturated contrasts in this paragraph do not answer RQ2. The same-stack PPO/GRPO run (Qwen2.5-0.5B, 40 steps, five seeds, paired by seed but not by evaluation item) is a two-digit addition task on which both arms are at ceiling: evaluation accuracy is 0.990 for GRPO and 0.992 for PPO, the paired difference is −0.002 (exact sign-flip p = 0.625; paired-t p = 0.374), and the 200 evaluation prompts are fresh random draws from the same generator used in training rather than a disjoint split; the two arms of a seed share only 3–7 of their 200 evaluation items, and PPO had drawn about 40% of its evaluation items during training against about 7% for GRPO (§6.2.3; sources: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/samestack_ppo_grpo.json; platform_hybrid/experiments/modal/modal_samestack_ppo_grpo.py). At that ceiling a null cannot separate "no effect" from "no headroom", so the result is that no difference was detectable on a saturated task, not that the estimators are equivalent; a paired TOST at a ±0.005 margin gives p = 0.10 (Table 4.2 gives the exact loss form of both arms). Rerun on the unsaturated Qwen2.5-1.5B GSM8K-CoT configuration with a disjoint test split, the unsaturated comparisons carry the RQ2 evidence, and this contrast remains baseline-specific and demoted: the minimal untuned-critic PPO arm (no warm-up, no GAE, no tuning; 8× the distinct-prompt exposure of GRPO G = 8; completions near the 200-token cap) shows no detectable gain while the GRPO arms gained, a post-training gap of +0.050 [+0.015, +0.085] over five paired seeds (exact sign-flip p = 0.0625 first — the n = 5 floor, not below 0.05; paired-t p = 0.016 second) (§6.5.5). That gap is not evidence about PPO versus GRPO as algorithms, and a powered, item-paired, disjoint-item design remains required future work. The variant audit (not to be confused with campaign lane E1) takes four GRPO-family variants (DAPO, GSPO, Dr. GRPO, AERO) plus the GRPO reference, runs five arms across eight paired seeds for 40 units at a frozen stack fingerprint, and evaluates on 500 held-out GSM8K examples per unit. Every one of the four contrasts is inconclusive. The figures below are paired-$t$ 95% intervals and paired-$t$ $p$-values over the eight seeds, recomputed from `zvf-program/audit/results/full/*.json`: DAPO $+0.00100$, CI $[-0.00632, +0.00832]$, $p = 0.756$; GSPO $+0.00500$, CI $[-0.00357, +0.01357]$, $p = 0.210$; Dr. GRPO $-0.00200$, CI $[-0.01272, +0.00872]$, $p = 0.673$; AERO $-0.00075$, CI $[-0.01029, +0.00879]$, $p = 0.858$. These paired-$t$ intervals replace the audit's own intervals (corrected; see Table E.1). After Benjamini–Hochberg correction every adjusted $p$ is at least 0.84. The pre-audit aggregator's verdict that the differences "disappear" is itself declared invalid, because the locked exact noncentral paired-$t$ minimum detectable effect of $0.010115893$ exceeds the $0.010$ margin the study was designed around (source: autoresearch/deli-neurips-tmlr-260802/audits/statistical_refutation.md). The audit's permitted wording is deliberately narrow: across eight paired seeds no arm produced a BH-significant held-out difference from GRPO, and none met the locked exact-power equivalence rule — which is not the same as saying the methods are the same, nor that they fail to improve. That audit runs on Qwen3-8B GSM8K with 500 evaluation examples per unit (Appendix A, P11).

Taken together, these two halves of the example motivate what follows. The mechanism half shows that sampling can produce groups with no reward-relative advantage signal even while reward rises. The attribution half shows that the comparative question — is this estimator better than the alternative? — cannot be answered from the labels "GRPO" and "PPO" alone. The historical comparisons are confounded or at ceiling; the later unsaturated comparison supplies a suggestive gap against an untuned critic, with unequal prompt exposure, rather than a general ranking. These observations motivate better measurement and fuller reporting, not a claim that the GRPO estimator is defective.

## 1.3 Problem Statement

The project is organised around four problems, in decreasing order of how often the literature acknowledges them.

**Attribution requires more than an algorithm label.** RL post-training frameworks can differ in parser, sampler, KL coefficient, clip range, LoRA targets, precision and evaluator. When those components vary together, a difference between runs labelled "GRPO" and "PPO" cannot be assigned to the estimator alone. The project's own marginal per-factor $\eta^2$ values put the framework factor at a magnitude comparable to the algorithm factor, although they are not a partition of the variance (source: platform_hybrid/paper/main_eai_body.tex; source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/_shared_methods.tex). Its same-stack comparison on saturated 0.5B addition returns a paired difference of −0.002 (exact sign-flip $p = 0.625$, paired-$t$ $p = 0.374$; pilot/feasibility only) (source: platform_hybrid/experiments/results/samestack_ppo_grpo.json). These local examples motivate an explicit stack record; they do not establish how often the wider literature makes invalid comparisons.

**Reward alone does not expose signal starvation.** Mean reward, loss, gradient norm, entropy and completion length do not directly report the fraction of groups whose relative advantage is zero. Rising reward can coexist with a high or low ZVF. The project therefore persists group rewards and reports this fraction explicitly (source: platform_tinker/reports/esa_phase1/Phase1_Project_Report_ZVF.tex). Prior methods already recognise and intervene on zero-variance groups, including DAPO and GRESO (§2.3); the contribution here is the recomputable measurement and its bounded interpretation, not discovery of the mechanism.

**GRPO-specific provenance is incompletely specified.** General reporting and reproducibility frameworks already exist (§2.4). The project's targeted literature search through July 2026 did not identify an equivalent GRPO-specific machine-readable run datasheet combining executed loss form, per-component implementation status and an explicit unauditability marker. This is a bounded search finding, not proof of absence. The practical problem addressed is how to record these distinctions consistently and validate the resulting records before using them in a comparison.

**Evaluation results are rarely reported with their terminal states.** Benchmark suites in the agentic and reasoning evaluation space are frequently multi-scope (a public split versus a private original contract, a benign-utility scope versus an adversarial scope), quota-limited, or gated behind private payloads. Reports tend either to omit such suites or to collapse them into a single number, which conceals how much of the suite was actually graded. The project treats this as a first-class concern rather than a presentational one: a result that is genuinely partial should be recorded as partial, with its coverage denominator and its reopen condition, and a lane that cannot be run at all should be recorded as externally blocked rather than quietly dropped.

## 1.4 Objectives

The project pursued eight objectives. The aims are distinguished from their current attainment in the table below.

**O1 — Build a single harness that can dispatch the same experiment to multiple back-ends.** Provide one control surface over Tinker, TRL, SkyRL, verl and OpenRLHF paths so that a configuration can be held fixed while the framework varies. The harness is the deliverable; it is not a completed multi-framework benchmark. Only Tinker and TRL produced completed runs, and those two used different base checkpoints (Qwen3-8B-Base and Qwen3-8B Instruct), so no framework comparison is claimed (Chapter 5 §5.2).

**O2 — Instrument the training loop so that signal starvation is directly observable.** Emit per-step telemetry carrying ZVF, gradient utilisation, policy entropy, completion length and KL divergence, and persist the raw per-group reward tensors so that any derived statistic can be deterministically recomputed after the fact rather than trusted from a log line.

**O3 — Measure the zero-variance regime on real reward tensors and test the levers that might move it.** Characterise ZVF empirically on genuine GRPO reward tensors, and investigate re-baselining and curriculum interventions as ways of converting dark compute into contrast.

**O4 — Establish and hold a scientific protocol.** Multi-seed runs, matched baselines, held-out evaluation with explicit denominators, deterministic recomputation, declared (not pre-registered) effect-size and power accounting, and reporting of negatives and nulls — including the cases where the answer is that the study was underpowered to answer.

**O5 — Identify a bounded contribution relative to prior work.** Use a literature scan to position a GRPO-specific reporting proposal against existing documentation and reproducibility frameworks. This produced the minimum reporting standard (P5) and machine-readable stack registry (P6); the originality claim is limited to the documented search and distinctions in §2.4 and Table 2.1.

**O6 — Turn the diagnostic into an intervention.** Derive an adaptive group-size controller from the ZVF measurement and evaluate it as a training-time policy rather than a post-hoc observation (P7).

**O7 — Build and exercise an evaluation-governance protocol on one trained actor.** Evaluate a single trained checkpoint across fourteen lanes with named native or replacement scopes, source-specific evaluator revision records, receipts, budgets and explicit terminal states. The suites are absent from the actor's recorded training-suite list; content-level decontamination remains unverified, and revision bindings have the limits reported in §8.2. The contribution is the protocol and the receipt-bound record it produces. The campaign is not evidence about GRPO: the actor's training run was short (40 steps, Chapter 8 §8.1.1), and the only trained-versus-base comparison is the small-sample Table 8.B.

**O8 — Report under a deliberate asymmetry.** Where the evidence supports a structural or descriptive claim, state it; where it does not, withhold it. No cross-suite aggregate, no pooling of original-contract and replacement-scope numbers, and no claim of improvement over the base model in the E1–E14 campaign. Its paired trained-versus-base comparison (Chapter 8, Table 8.B) is inconclusive in every lane. The controlled studies in Chapter 6 separately report pre-to-post gains and the M1b GRPO–PPO contrast, limited to the tested stack and an untuned critic; five seeds give an exact sign-flip p-value of 0.0625 despite the paired t-test p-value of 0.016.

The status below separates delivered software and analyses from experimental aims that remain open. A working component does not by itself establish its scientific benefit.

**Contributions.** Measured against these objectives, the thesis makes four contributions. Everything else in it is supporting evidence for one of them.

1. **A recomputable signal-starvation diagnostic and a loss-form audit.** ZVF is logged per step and recomputed from stored reward tensors against a closed-form Bernoulli null (§4.3, §6.3). Reading the runner code distinguishes custom REINFORCE group-mean loops from clipped surrogates, nonzero-KL configuration records and unknown managed settings (Table 4.2).
2. **Stack-conditioned reporting.** A minimum reporting standard (P5) and a machine-readable stack registry with `stackdiff`, which reports declared-difference codes (R0–R5; formerly "flip-risk" verdicts, renamed 19 October 2026 — not calibrated label-flip probabilities) between two runs from their records alone (Chapter 7).
3. **Calibrated negative results.** No reliable scaling law is recovered from reward curves; the same-stack PPO/GRPO and group-size contrasts are nulls on a saturated task, and on an unsaturated rerun only the PPO/GRPO gap appears, from an untuned critic; ZVF predictive validity remains unestablished because the historical correlation mixes synthetic cells and heterogeneous outcomes; and the ZVF-derived controller's zero-rescue counterfactual is imposed by a plug-in model fitted to observed all-equal groups, not a test of fresh-rollout recoverability. Each is reported with the power limitation that bounds it (Chapters 6–7).
4. **An evaluation-governance protocol.** Native graders at pinned revisions, per-lane receipts, a no-pooling rule, a per-lane serving audit and a paired same-engine trained-versus-base design, exercised on one lightly trained actor across fourteen suites absent from its recorded training-suite list (Chapter 8). Content-level decontamination remains unverified. The contribution is the protocol and the record it produces, not the actor's scores.

## 1.5 Scope and Non-Goals

The C1 extension dated 3 October 2026 adds a frozen-policy diagnostic whose chief limitation is the reference itself. Exact-match zeros can reflect an incorrect key, ambiguous wording or equivalent units as well as a wrong response. Among the first 64 eligible questions from a fixed 4,096-question screen, the frozen score records 11 fresh-response events; assistant-assisted review corroborates three numerical witnesses on the same denominator. This extension separates scoring from semantic support and does not imply reasoning improvement, useful RL gradients or training gain. Section 7.5 and Appendix G retain the full accounting, including disagreements. Historical V2 remains separate (sources: outputs/wrong_cohort_2026-10-02/continuation_c1/final_analysis/summary.json; outputs/wrong_cohort_2026-10-02/continuation_c1/assistant_audit/final_reconciliation/final_evidence_summary.json).


The scope of this report is measurement and diagnostics, not leaderboard performance. The controlled studies and the campaign actor use small-to-mid open models — the Qwen2.5, Qwen3, Qwen3.5 and Qwen3.6 families, from roughly 0.5B parameters up to the 35B sparse mixture-of-experts configuration used for the campaign actor. The P1 cross-scale roster is wider, 0.6B to 1T total parameters, but its frontier-scale anchors are single-seed runs through a managed API and are read descriptively (Chapter 6 §6.1; source: platform_hybrid/paper/sections/p1_abstract.tex). Verifiable rewards come from mathematics and code checkers, principally GSM8K exact-match and HumanEval-style unit tests. Held-out evaluation uses suites' own native evaluators at pinned revisions.

Several things are explicitly out of scope. This is not an attempt to set a state-of-the-art result on any benchmark, and no such claim is made anywhere in the report. It is not a study of frontier reasoning models. The evaluation sets used in the controlled comparisons are small, and their sizes differ by study: 200 fresh prompts for the 0.5B arithmetic runs, 200 per seed for the 1.5B GSM8K chain-of-thought runs, 500 for the variant audit, and only 8 to 20 in several Phase-1 openings (the layer-freeze result rests on n = 8). Table 1.1 gives the n behind each headline number. Claims about novel algorithms are not made: the length-weighting intervention (P4) and the adaptive controller (P7) were designed during the work and are reported as designed and simulated systems with their evaluation limits attached, not as demonstrated improvements.

The report states negative, null and withdrawn results alongside positive ones; this is its one general statement about reporting practice, and later chapters report the results without repeating it. Three evidentiary rules apply throughout and are worth stating here because they constrain what the later chapters are allowed to say. First, every number in this report is traceable to a repository file that was actually read; where a figure could not be sourced, the finding is described qualitatively and the figure is attributed to its source rather than reconstructed. Second, original-contract and replacement-scope benchmark results are never pooled and never averaged across suites — the two are different measurements of different things under different licences, and treating them as one column would manufacture a number that no suite reported. Third, no improvement over a baseline or over a base model is claimed unless the source states it with a test; where the source reports a difference without a test supporting it, or reports an inconclusive test, that is what is written here.
